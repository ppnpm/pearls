## Structure, Function and Morphology of Skin

### 1. Types of Scale

**PEARL:** Collarette scale — pityriasis rosea. Silvery/micaceous scale — psoriasis. Mica-like scale — pityriasis lichenoides. Branny (furfuraceous) scale — pityriasis versicolor.

**UNDERSTAND:** Scale morphology reflects how the stratum corneum is disturbed. Psoriasis has massively accelerated turnover (4 days instead of 28) with retained nuclei (parakeratosis), giving thick loose silvery scale. Pityriasis rosea’s scale detaches at its outer edge and stays attached centrally — hence the inward-facing collarette.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Pityriasis rosea: a herald patch precedes the eruption by 1–2 weeks; then oval lesions along the skin cleavage lines giving the “Christmas tree” pattern on the back, with peripheral collarette scale. Self-limiting in 6–8 weeks; linked to HHV-6/7. Always exclude secondary syphilis (which involves palms and soles — pityriasis rosea does not).
- Psoriasis signs: Auspitz sign (pinpoint bleeding on scraping — because of thinned suprapapillary plates over dilated capillaries), candle grease sign, ring of Woronoff (pale halo), and Koebner phenomenon (lesions at sites of trauma — also seen in lichen planus and vitiligo).
- Histology of psoriasis: parakeratosis, Munro microabscesses (neutrophils in the stratum corneum), elongated rete ridges (regular acanthosis), thinned suprapapillary plate, loss of the granular layer, and dilated dermal papillary capillaries.
- Nail psoriasis: pitting is the commonest (irregular, deep — versus the fine geometric pitting of alopecia areata), plus oil-drop/salmon patch, onycholysis, subungual hyperkeratosis and splinter haemorrhages. Nail involvement strongly predicts psoriatic arthritis (classically dip joints, dactylitis, and the “pencil-in-cup” deformity).
- Lichen planus for contrast: the 6 Ps — purple, polygonal, planar, pruritic, papules, plaques — with WICKHAM’S striae, and histology showing hypergranulosis, saw-tooth rete ridges and a band-like lymphocytic infiltrate.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Pure matching question; and separately, nail changes → psoriasis.

**REMEMBER:** *Collarette rosea, silvery psoriasis, mica lichenoides, branny versicolor.*

### 2. Modes of Glandular Secretion

**PEARL:** Holocrine — the whole cell disintegrates and becomes the secretion: sebaceous glands and meibomian glands. Apocrine — the apical portion of the cell pinches off: apocrine sweat glands, mammary gland. Merocrine (eccrine) — exocytosis with the cell left intact: eccrine sweat glands, salivary glands, pancreas.

**UNDERSTAND:** The three modes differ in how much of the cell is sacrificed. Sebum is lipid stored inside the cytoplasm, so the only way to release it is to destroy the cell — hence sebaceous glands must continuously regenerate from their basal layer.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Sebaceous gland histology (a common image question): lobules of large polygonal cells with foamy/vacuolated cytoplasm and central shrunken nuclei, opening into the hair follicle (the pilosebaceous unit) — except the free (non-follicular) sebaceous glands: Meibomian glands of the eyelid, Fordyce spots of the lips and buccal mucosa, Tyson’s glands of the prepuce, and Montgomery tubercles of the areola.
- Sebaceous glands are androgen-dependent — quiescent in childhood, activated at puberty (hence acne), and they are the target of isotretinoin.
- Eccrine glands: thermoregulatory, all over the body, densest on palms/soles/forehead, opening directly onto the skin surface, innervated by cholinergic sympathetic fibres.
- Apocrine glands: axilla, areola, genital/perianal; open into the follicle; active only after puberty; adrenergic.
- Skin appendage layout for a labelled diagram (superficial → deep): sebaceous gland attached to the upper follicle, arrector pili muscle inserting into the follicle below it, apocrine gland deep and opening into the follicle, and the eccrine gland coiled independently in the deep dermis with its own duct to the surface.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Histology image of a lobulated foamy-celled gland → “mechanism of secretion” → holocrine.

**REMEMBER:** *Holocrine sacrifices the whole cell (sebum), apocrine loses its head, merocrine just exports.*

### 3. Epidermal Layers — Which Cell, Which Layer

**PEARL:** Stratum basale contains keratinocytes, melanocytes and Merkel cells — but not Langerhans cells. Langerhans cells live predominantly in the stratum spinosum, which is also where desmosomes are most abundant.

**UNDERSTAND:** Position follows function. Melanocytes must sit at the base to hand melanin caps to the dividing keratinocytes they protect. Merkel cells sit at the base where nerve endings reach them. Langerhans cells sit higher, in the spinosum, forming a surveillance net just beneath the surface to catch antigens penetrating the barrier — an immunological sentinel line.

**MUST KNOW:**

- The stratum spinosum is “spiny” precisely because of its abundant desmosomes, which appear as intercellular bridges when the tissue shrinks during fixation. This is why pemphigus vulgaris (anti-desmoglein) splits SUPRABASALLY, right where those junctions are densest.
- Langerhans cells are bone-marrow-derived dendritic antigen-presenting cells containing Birbeck granules (tennis-racquet shaped), and are CD1a and S-100 positive — the markers used to diagnose Langerhans cell histiocytosis.
- Melanocytes are neural crest derived, sit in the basal layer at roughly 1 per 10 basal keratinocytes, and this ratio is identical in all races — racial pigmentation differences come from melanosome size, number and distribution, not melanocyte count.
- Merkel cells are slowly adapting type I touch receptors; their malignant counterpart, Merkel cell carcinoma, is aggressive and linked to Merkel cell polyomavirus and immunosuppression.
- Layer order (deep → superficial): basale → spinosum → granulosum → lucidum (palms and soles only) → corneum. Turnover ~28 days.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Labelled epidermis image → “which cell is not in this layer?” → Langerhans cells in the basal layer; and “desmosomes are most abundant in” → stratum spinosum.

**REMEMBER:** *Base = melanocytes and Merkel; spinosum = Langerhans and desmosomes.*

### 4. Skin — The Basics That Get Asked

**PEARL:** Epidermis is ectodermal; the dermis is mesodermal. The dermis is type I collagen (~80–85%) with type III (~10–15%). Skin is the largest organ, ~15% of body weight.

**understand:** The dual origin explains a lot of dermatology: epidermal appendages (hair, nails, sebaceous and sweat glands) are ectodermal, so they fail together in the ectodermal dysplasias; while melanocytes, Merkel cells and cutaneous nerves are neural crest, and Langerhans cells come from the bone marrow (mesoderm).

**MUST KNOW:**

- “Dermis is derived from ectoderm” is the classic false statement in this question.
- Epidermal layers (deep → superficial): Basale → Spinosum → Granulosum → Lucidum (palms and soles only) → Corneum — “Come, Let’s Get Sun Burnt” read upward. Turnover time ~28 days (accelerated to ~4 days in psoriasis).
- Cell populations: keratinocytes (~90%), melanocytes (basal layer, ~1:10 basal cells; the number is the same in all races — only melanosome size and distribution differ), Langerhans cells (stratum spinosum, antigen-presenting, Birbeck granules), Merkel cells (basal, touch).
- Type III collagen predominates in early wound healing and in fetal skin, being replaced by type I as the scar matures — and it is the collagen defective in vascular Ehlers–Danlos (type IV).
- Type IV collagen is in the basement membrane — the target in Goodpasture and defective in Alport syndrome.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Which is false about skin?” → dermis from ectoderm.

**REMEMBER:** *Epidermis from ectoderm, dermis from mesoderm, melanocytes from neural crest.*

### 5. Apocrine Glands

**PEARL:** Found only in the axilla, areola, periumbilical and genital/perianal regions — plus the modified forms: ceruminous glands (ear canal), Moll’s glands (eyelid) and the mammary gland. They open into the hair follicle, not onto the skin surface, and become active only at puberty.

**UNDERSTAND:** Apocrine glands are androgen-dependent — which is why they are silent in childhood, switch on at puberty, and why their diseases (hidradenitis, bromhidrosis) are diseases of adolescence and adulthood. Their secretion is odourless when fresh; body odour is produced by bacterial degradation of it — which is exactly why deodorants are antibacterial and antiperspirants target the eccrine glands.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Palms and soles have NO apocrine glands — they are the most eccrine-dense sites (which is why emotional sweating shows there). This is the standard exam trap.
- Eccrine glands: everywhere except the lip vermilion, nail bed, glans and labia minora; open directly onto the skin surface; cholinergic sympathetic supply — hence anticholinergics cause anhidrosis and heat stroke, and why botulinum toxin works for hyperhidrosis.
- Hidradenitis suppurativa follows the apocrine map exactly — axillae, groin, perineum, inframammary — with painful nodules, sinus tracts and rope-like scarring. It is now understood as follicular occlusion, not primary apocrine infection. Associations: obesity, smoking, metabolic syndrome, Crohn’s disease.
- Fox–Fordyce disease = itchy follicular papules from apocrine duct obstruction in the axilla.
- Sebaceous glands are holocrine and also open into the follicle (the pilosebaceous unit) — the site of acne.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Apocrine glands are present in” → axilla. The distractor is always palms and soles.

**REMEMBER:** *Apocrine follows hair and puberty — axilla, areola, groin. Never palms and soles.*

### 6. Annular Lesions — and the Wheal That Isn’t

**PEARL:** Urticaria produces wheals, not annular lesions. The classic annular (ring-with-central-clearing) list is tinea corporis, pityriasis rosea, porokeratosis, granuloma annulare, erythema annulare centrifugum and erythema migrans.

**UNDERSTAND:** An annular lesion means the process spreads centrifugally while the centre resolves — a fungus advancing outward through the stratum corneum leaves cleared skin behind it. A wheal is entirely different: transient dermal oedema from plasma leakage, appearing and vanishing within 24 hours without a trace, so it never has time to form a ring.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Defining wheal features: evanescent (< 24 h), intensely itchy, surrounding flare, resolves without any mark. A “wheal” lasting > 24 h or leaving bruising is urticarial vasculitis, not urticaria — and needs a biopsy.
- Tinea corporis — raised, scaly active advancing edge with central clearing; Trichophyton rubrum commonest.
- Pityriasis rosea — herald patch, then a “Christmas tree” distribution along skin cleavage lines, collarette of scale; linked to HHV-6/7.
- Erythema migrans in Lyme disease is the classic expanding annular lesion after a tick bite.
- Granuloma annulare — annular but non-scaly rings on the dorsa of hands/feet; scaling separates it from tinea.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Least likely to be annular” → urticaria. The examiner is checking that you know a wheal is defined by its transience, not its shape.

**REMEMBER:** *Rings spread and clear; wheals appear and vanish.*

### 7. Odland Bodies and the Skin Barrier

**PEARL:** Odland bodies (lamellar bodies / keratinosomes / membrane-coating granules) live in the stratum granulosum and upper spinosum, and discharge lipids into the intercellular space to create the permeability barrier.

**UNDERSTAND:** The barrier is a “bricks and mortar” structure — corneocytes are the bricks, and the mortar is the ceramide/cholesterol/free fatty acid lamellae secreted by these granules. This is why the barrier fails at exactly this level in ichthyoses and atopic dermatitis: no mortar, no waterproofing.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Stratum granulosum also contains keratohyalin granules (profilaggrin) — the source of filaggrin, whose loss-of-function mutation causes ichthyosis vulgaris and predisposes to atopic dermatitis and pompholyx.
- Desmosomes are most abundant in the stratum spinosum — hence “prickle cell layer”; their loss (acantholysis) causes pemphigus.
- Layers, deep to superficial: basale (germinativum, melanocytes, Merkel cells) → spinosum (desmosomes, Langerhans cells) → granulosum (keratohyalin + Odland bodies) → lucidum (palms/soles only) → corneum.
- Cell origins: keratinocytes — ectoderm; melanocytes — neural crest (basal layer); Langerhans cells — bone marrow (spinosum); Merkel cells — touch receptors (basal).
- Epidermal transit time is ~28 days, reduced to ~4 days in psoriasis.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Two paired questions — Odland bodies → stratum granulosum, and desmosomes most abundant → stratum spinosum. Do not swap them.

**REMEMBER:** *Granular layer makes the mortar; spinous layer holds the bricks together.*

### 8. Layers of the Epidermis — Cell Residents

**PEARL:** Keratinocytes, melanocytes, and Merkel cells reside in the stratum basale. Langerhans cells are not basal — they are predominantly in the stratum spinosum.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Langerhans cells = bone-marrow-derived antigen-presenting dendritic cells; CD1a+, S100+, Langerin (CD207)+, contain Birbeck granules (tennis-racquet shaped).
- Melanocyte:basal keratinocyte ratio ≈ 1:10 (constant across races — racial colour differences are due to melanosome size/dispersion, not melanocyte number).
- Merkel cells = touch (slow-adapting type I) mechanoreceptors, CK20+.

**REMEMBER:** *Basal = Keratinocyte + Melanocyte + Merkel; Spinous = Langerhans.*

## Papulosquamous Disorders and Eczema

### 9. Pityriasis Rubra Pilaris

**PEARL:** Salmon/orange-red scaly plaques that coalesce, leaving sharply defined “islands of sparing” of normal skin, with follicular “nutmeg-grater” papules on the elbows and wrists, and a thick orange-yellow palmoplantar keratoderma (“keratotic sandal”). Histology: alternating ortho- and parakeratosis in a checkerboard pattern.

**UNDERSTAND:** The orange hue and the islands of sparing are what separate it from psoriasis at a glance — psoriasis is salmon-pink with silvery scale and NO islands of sparing. The checkerboard ortho/parakeratosis reflects fluctuating, disordered keratinisation rather than psoriasis’ uniformly accelerated turnover.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Distinguishing from psoriasis is the whole point of the question: PRP has islands of sparing, follicular papules, orange colour and waxy keratoderma; psoriasis has well-demarcated silvery plaques, Auspitz sign, nail pitting, and Munro microabscesses. PRP notably lacks Munro microabscesses.
- PRP characteristically progresses CEPHALOCAUDALLY (head downward) and may evolve to erythroderma — but even then the islands of sparing persist, which is diagnostic in an erythrodermic patient.
- Six types (Griffiths): type I classic adult (commonest, best prognosis — most remit in 3 years), type III classic juvenile, type V atypical juvenile (CARD14 mutations, familial), and type VI associated with HIV — so test for HIV in an adult with new PRP.
- Treatment: systemic retinoids (acitretin/isotretinoin) are first-line; methotrexate second-line; biologics (anti-TNF, anti-IL-17) in refractory disease. Topical therapy alone is inadequate.
- Erythroderma differential worth carrying: psoriasis, eczema, drug reaction, PRP, and cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (Sézary syndrome).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Image of orange palmoplantar keratoderma + histology of ortho/parakeratosis → PRP.

**also know:** Histology: alternating ortho- and parakeratosis in a checkerboard pattern, follicular plugging with a “shoulder” of parakeratosis, dilated but non-tortuous dermal capillaries, sparse lymphocytic infiltrate, focal acantholysis.

**REMEMBER:** *Orange skin with islands of normal in a sea of red — pityriasis rubra pilaris.*

### 10. Lichen Nitidus

**PEARL:** Multiple tiny (1–2 mm), shiny, flat-topped, skin-coloured dome-shaped papules — typically on the forearms, dorsa of the hands, penis and abdomen — are lichen nitidus. Mucous membranes are not involved, Wickham striae are absent, and Koebnerisation IS seen.

**UNDERSTAND:** Lichen nitidus is histologically a tiny, sharply circumscribed lymphohistiocytic granuloma “clutched” by elongated rete ridges — the classic “ball and claw” appearance. Because each lesion is a discrete micro-granuloma rather than the broad band-like infiltrate of lichen planus, the papules stay minute, discrete and asymptomatic, and there is no thickened granular layer to produce the white lacy Wickham striae. The same explains why mucosa is spared.

**MUST KNOW:**

| | **Lichen nitidus** | **Lichen planus** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lesions | **Pinpoint 1–2 mm, shiny, skin-coloured** | **Violaceous, polygonal, flat-topped papules** (the 5 Ps: purple, pruritic, polygonal, planar, papules) |
| Itch | **Usually asymptomatic** | **Intensely pruritic** |
| Wickham striae | **Absent** | **Present** |
| Mucosa | **Spared** | **Involved (lacy buccal network) in 50%; can be erosive** |
| Nails | Rarely | **Pterygium, longitudinal ridging, 20-nail dystrophy** |
| Histology | **“Ball and claw” focal granuloma** | **Band-like (lichenoid) infiltrate, saw-tooth rete, Civatte bodies, hypergranulosis** |
| Koebner | **Positive** | **Positive** |

- Both are self-limiting; lichen nitidus usually resolves within a year and needs no treatment (topical steroids or phototherapy if extensive).
- Koebner (isomorphic) phenomenon — lesions appearing along a line of trauma — occurs in psoriasis, lichen planus, lichen nitidus, vitiligo, viral warts and molluscum. (Reverse Koebner — clearing at sites of trauma.)
- Lichen planus associations: hepatitis C, drugs (thiazides, antimalarials, gold, beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors — “lichenoid drug eruption”), and a small risk of squamous cell carcinoma in erosive oral/genital disease.
- “Id reaction” (autoeczematisation) — a distant sterile vesicular eruption in response to a remote focus of infection or dermatitis (classically dermatophyte or stasis dermatitis); it is not what lichen nitidus is.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “What is true about the diagnosis?” — the true statement is the absence of mucosal involvement; the distractors misattribute Wickham striae (lichen planus) or deny Koebnerisation.

**REMEMBER:** *Pinpoint shiny papules, skin only — a granuloma clutched by rete ridges.*

### 11. Psoriasis: Immunology and Nails

**PEARL:** Psoriasis is a state of excess Th1 and Th17 activity (IL-17, IL-23, TNF-α) — never deficiency. Nail changes are fine, regular, deep pitting with onycholysis, HLA-Cw6 (HLA-C*06:02) is the strongest genetic association, and arthritis occurs in 5–30%.

**UNDERSTAND:** The entire clinical picture follows from the cytokine axis. IL-23 sustains Th17 cells, which release IL-17 and IL-22; these drive keratinocyte hyperproliferation (epidermal turnover falls from ~28 days to 3–5 days) and neutrophil recruitment. Because the epidermis is turning over faster than it can mature, you get parakeratosis (retained nuclei), a thinned suprapapillary plate that bleeds when scale is removed (Auspitz sign), and neutrophils trapped in the stratum corneum (Munro microabscesses). That also explains why the modern biologics all target this one axis.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Clinical signs: Auspitz sign (pinpoint bleeding), candle-grease sign (grattage), Koebner phenomenon, well-demarcated salmon-pink plaques with silvery scale on extensor surfaces, scalp and lumbosacral area.
- Nails: pitting (irregular, deep and coarse — contrast the fine, regular, geometric pitting of alopecia areata), oil-drop/salmon patch, subungual hyperkeratosis, onycholysis, splinter haemorrhages. Nail involvement strongly predicts psoriatic arthritis.
- Psoriatic arthritis patterns: asymmetrical oligoarthritis (commonest), dip joint disease with nail changes, symmetrical rheumatoid-like, spondylitis/sacroiliitis, and arthritis mutilans (“pencil-in-cup” deformity, telescoping digits). Seronegative (RF negative), HLA-B27 associated for the axial forms; dactylitis (“sausage digit”) and enthesitis are hallmarks.
- Histology: parakeratosis, Munro microabscesses, spongiform pustules of Kogoj, acanthosis with regular elongation of rete ridges, thinned suprapapillary plates, dilated tortuous capillaries, absent granular layer.
- Treatment ladder: emollients + topical steroids and vitamin D analogues (calcipotriol), coal tar, dithranol; phototherapy (NB-UVB, PUVA); systemic methotrexate, ciclosporin, acitretin, apremilast; biologics — anti-TNF (etanercept, adalimumab, infliximab), anti-IL-17 (secukinumab, ixekizumab), anti-IL-23/12 (ustekinumab, guselkumab).
- Triggers: streptococcal throat infection (guttate psoriasis in children/young adults), trauma (Koebner), drugs — lithium, beta-blockers, antimalarials, NSAIDs, and steroid withdrawal (which precipitates pustular psoriasis), HIV, stress, alcohol.
- Never give systemic corticosteroids for plaque psoriasis — withdrawal triggers generalised pustular (von Zumbusch) or erythrodermic psoriasis.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Which is not true?” — the false statement inverts the immunology (“deficient” Th1/Th17). Every immune-mediated inflammatory disease question rewards knowing the direction of the cytokine change.

**ALSO KNOW:** Modern systemic therapy is target-driven: for psoriasis, IL-23 inhibitors (risankizumab, guselkumab, tildrakizumab) and IL-17 inhibitors (secukinumab, ixekizumab, bimekizumab) alongside TNF inhibitors and the oral TYK2 inhibitor deucravacitinib; for atopic dermatitis, dupilumab (anti-IL-4Rα), tralokinumab and lebrikizumab (anti-IL-13), and the oral JAK inhibitors upadacitinib and abrocitinib; for alopecia areata, baricitinib, ritlecitinib and deuruxolitinib; and for NON-segmental vitiligo, topical ruxolitinib cream — the first approved repigmenting therapy. All JAK inhibitors carry a boxed warning for thrombosis, major cardiovascular events, malignancy and serious infection.[^2]

**REMEMBER:** *Too much IL-17, not too little — the epidermis is running a sprint.*

### 12. Psoriasis — Never Give Systemic Steroids

**PEARL:** Systemic corticosteroids are contraindicated in chronic plaque psoriasis — withdrawal precipitates generalised pustular psoriasis and erythroderma.

**UNDERSTAND:** Steroids suppress the plaque but do not alter the underlying IL-17/IL-23 driven disease. On withdrawal there is a rebound flare into an unstable, more dangerous phenotype. The plaque form is a *stable* disease; converting it to the pustular form trades a chronic nuisance for a life-threatening emergency.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Drugs of choice by type: chronic plaque → methotrexate; erythrodermic → methotrexate; psoriatic arthritis → methotrexate/apremilast; pustular → acitretin; psoriasis in HIV → acitretin (avoid immunosuppressants); impetigo herpetiformis (pustular psoriasis of pregnancy) → systemic steroids — the one legitimate indication.
- Systemic therapy is indicated when BSA > 10%, or PASI > 10, or with severe impact/arthritis. NB-UVB and ciclosporin are alternatives; ciclosporin is the fastest-acting for severe flares.
- Acitretin is absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy and requires contraception for 3 years after stopping (it is re-esterified to etretinate, which is stored in fat) — contrast isotretinoin, only 1 month. Half-life ~50 h; relative contraindications include hyperlipidaemia, hepatic disease, alcoholism; causes photosensitivity.
- Koebner (isomorphic) phenomenon — new lesions along a line of trauma — is positive in psoriasis, vitiligo and lichen planus (the “true” Koebner triad).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Which will you not recommend” in extensive plaque psoriasis → oral steroids. The acitretin question is always about the 3-year contraception rule — the distractor offers 6 months.

### 13. Pompholyx (Dyshidrotic Eczema)

**PEARL:** Pompholyx = deep-seated “sago grain”/tapioca vesicles on the palms, soles and sides of the fingers that are both painful and intensely pruritic.

**UNDERSTAND:** The vesicles sit under the thick palmoplantar stratum corneum, so they cannot rupture outward. Trapped fluid under an unyielding roof stretches the tissue — producing pain in addition to the itch, which is what distinguishes it from ordinary eczema.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Predisposing: atopy, dry skin, filaggrin mutations, allergic or irritant contact dermatitis (nickel, chromate, epoxy, rubber), hyperhidrosis, stress. Also as an id reaction to tinea pedis.
- Treatment ladder: irritant/allergen avoidance and emollients → potent topical steroids → alitretinoin, PUVA, ciclosporin, methotrexate, azathioprine.
- Investigate with patch testing when recurrent, and KOH of the feet to exclude a dermatophytid reaction.
- Differential of palmar vesicles: scabies (burrows, web spaces), hand-foot-mouth disease, palmoplantar pustulosis (sterile pustules, smoking-related), contact dermatitis.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “All true except” → the false statement is “painful but not pruritic”. Pompholyx is emphatically both.

## Vesiculobullous and Immunobullous Diseases

### 16. Immunobullous Diseases — The Split Level Decides Everything

**PEARL:** Pemphigus vulgaris — suprabasal split, “row of tombstones”, flaccid bullae, mucosa involved, Nikolsky positive, DIF “fish-net”/chicken-wire intercellular IgG. Bullous pemphigoid — subepidermal split with eosinophils, tense bullae, mucosa usually spared, Nikolsky negative, DIF linear IgG and C3 at the basement membrane.

**UNDERSTAND:** The target antigen dictates the level, and the level dictates every clinical sign. Pemphigus antibodies attack desmogleins (desmosomes between keratinocytes) — so cells fall apart from each other high in the epidermis, the roof is thin, blisters rupture instantly, and you see erosions rather than blisters. Pemphigoid antibodies attack hemidesmosomes (BP180 > BP230, anchoring the basal cell to the basement membrane) — the whole epidermis lifts as an intact roof, so blisters are tense and survive.

**MUST KNOW:**

- “Row of tombstones” = the basal cells stay attached to the basement membrane while everything above them separates — a purely descriptive but pathognomonic image finding.
- Desmoglein compensation explains the mucosal pattern: oral mucosa expresses mainly Dsg3, skin expresses Dsg1 superficially. So anti-Dsg3 alone → mucosal-dominant pemphigus vulgaris; anti-Dsg1 alone → pemphigus FOLIACEUS (superficial, subcorneal split, scaly crusted lesions, NO mucosal involvement).
- DIF (direct immunofluorescence) of PERILESIONAL skin is the definitive test — histology alone suggests, DIF confirms. (This is why “row of tombstones” is *not* the conclusion drawn from a histology report.)
- Bullous pemphigoid is a disease of the elderly, often preceded by a pruritic urticarial/eczematous prodrome for months, and is now a recognised complication of anti-PD1 immune checkpoint inhibitors (nivolumab, pembrolizumab) and of gliptins (DPP-4 inhibitors).
- Dermatitis herpetiformis completes the trio: intensely itchy grouped vesicles on extensor surfaces, granular IgA in the dermal papillae, associated with coeliac disease, treated with dapsone and a gluten-free diet.
- Treatment: pemphigus — systemic steroids + steroid-sparing agents; rituximab is now first-line for moderate–severe disease.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Given as histology (“suprabasal vs subepidermal”), DIF pattern (“fish-net vs linear vs granular”), or a drug trigger. The DIF pattern alone answers most questions.

**REMEMBER:** *Pemphigus is high and flaccid (desmosomes, fish-net); pemphigoid is deep and tense (hemidesmosomes, linear).*

### 17. Dermatitis Herpetiformis vs Linear IgA Disease

**PEARL:** Dermatitis herpetiformis — intensely itchy grouped vesicles on extensor surfaces (elbows, knees, buttocks, scalp), DIF shows granular IgA in the dermal papillae, antigen epidermal transglutaminase-3, associated with coeliac disease. Linear IgA disease / chronic bullous disorder of childhood — DIF shows linear IgA at the dermo-epidermal junction, antigen BP180 (BPAg2), with tense blisters in a “string of pearls / cluster of jewels” annular arrangement.

**UNDERSTAND:** Both are IgA-mediated subepidermal blistering diseases — the DIF pattern (granular vs linear) is the only reliable way to separate them, and it is therefore what the exam always tests.

**MUST KNOW:**

- DH: HLA DQ2/DQ8; virtually all patients have gluten-sensitive enteropathy, though most have no bowel symptoms. Treat with a gluten-free diet (definitive, but takes months–years) plus dapsone for rapid symptom control (a response within 48–72 hours is almost diagnostic). Check G6PD before dapsone — haemolysis and methaemoglobinaemia are the key toxicities.
- Histology of DH: subepidermal blister with neutrophilic papillary tip microabscesses.
- CBDC: the commonest immunobullous disease of childhood; in young children lesions are perioral, perianal and perineal; treated with dapsone and often self-limiting by puberty. Drug-induced linear IgA disease is classically caused by vancomycin.
- The complete DIF map is worth owning: fish-net/intercellular IgG → pemphigus · linear IgG + C3 at BMZ → bullous pemphigoid · linear IgA at BMZ → linear IgA disease · granular IgA in dermal papillae → dermatitis herpetiformis · granular “full-house” at BMZ (lupus band) → SLE.
- Microabscess map: neutrophils in papillary tips → DH; neutrophils in stratum corneum (Munro) and Kogoj pustules → psoriasis; lymphocytes (Pautrier) → mycosis fungoides; eosinophils → pemphigus VEGETANS. *(Munro in Psoriasis, Pautrier in Mycosis fungoides.)*

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The DIF description in the stem is the answer — granular = DH, linear = linear IgA.

**ALSO KNOW:** Almost all have (usually silent) celiac disease; gluten-free diet is the definitive treatment. Dapsone gives dramatic itch relief within 24–48 h (does not treat gut disease). Also linked to autoimmune thyroid disease and intestinal lymphoma.

**REMEMBER:** *Granular in the papillae means gluten; linear at the junction means the string of pearls.*

### 18. Pemphigoid Gestationis

**PEARL:** Pemphigoid (herpes) gestationis begins in the periumbilical region in the 2nd or 3rd trimester, then spreads over the abdomen and trunk. It is an autoimmune subepidermal blistering disease (anti-BP180), not a herpes infection.

**UNDERSTAND:** It is essentially bullous pemphigoid triggered by pregnancy — antibodies against placental BP180 (which shares antigen with skin) cross-react with the maternal basement membrane. DIF shows linear C3 (± IgG) at the dermo-epidermal junction, exactly as in bullous pemphigoid.

**MUST KNOW:**

- It typically flares AT delivery, recurs in subsequent pregnancies (usually earlier and more severely), and with OCP use and menstruation.
- Fetal risks: prematurity and small-for-gestational-age babies; about 10% of neonates develop transient blistering from transplacental antibody, which resolves spontaneously.
- The critical differential is PUPPP (polymorphic eruption of pregnancy) — the commonest pregnancy dermatosis: urticarial papules and plaques beginning in the abdominal striae and characteristically sparing the periumbilical skin, in a primigravida in the 3rd trimester, with no fetal risk and negative DIF. “Pemphigoid gestationis loves the umbilicus; PUPPP avoids it” — that single contrast answers the question.
- Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy is the dermatosis that truly threatens the fetus — itching without a primary rash (only excoriations), palms and soles, raised bile acids, with risk of stillbirth → treat with ursodeoxycholic acid and plan delivery.
- Treatment of pemphigoid gestationis: potent topical steroids for mild disease, systemic prednisolone for blistering.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Most common site” → periumbilical region.

**REMEMBER:** *Blisters starting at the umbilicus = pemphigoid gestationis. Hives in the striae sparing the umbilicus = PUPPP.*

### 19. Bullous Pemphigoid vs Pemphigus Vulgaris

**PEARL:** Elderly patient + intensely itchy, tense bullae on the limbs and flexures with intact mucosa = bullous pemphigoid (subepidermal, anti-hemidesmosome). Flaccid bullae that rupture early, with painful oral erosions, = pemphigus vulgaris (intraepidermal, anti-desmoglein).

**UNDERSTAND:** The whole distinction follows from which adhesion structure is attacked and therefore where the split occurs. Pemphigoid antibodies target BP180 (collagen XVII) and BP230 in the hemidesmosome, splitting the skin below the entire epidermis — so the blister roof is full-thickness epidermis, which is thick and tense and survives days. Pemphigus antibodies target desmoglein 1 and 3 in the desmosomes between keratinocytes, splitting the epidermis within itself — a roof only a few cells thick, which is flaccid and ruptures immediately, leaving erosions. Mucosal involvement follows the antigen distribution: desmoglein 3 is abundant in mucosa (hence oral disease in pemphigus), while hemidesmosomal antigens are mainly cutaneous.

**MUST KNOW:**

| | **Bullous pemphigoid** | **Pemphigus vulgaris** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Age | **Elderly (>60)** | Middle age (40–60) |
| Blister | **Tense, itchy, survives** | **Flaccid, ruptures, painful** |
| Split | **Subepidermal** | **Intraepidermal (suprabasal)** |
| Antigen | **BP180 / BP230 (hemidesmosome)** | **Desmoglein 3 (± 1)** |
| Mucosa | Usually **spared** | **Oral erosions, often the first sign** |
| Nikolsky sign | **Negative** | **Positive** |
| Histology | Subepidermal blister with **eosinophils** | **Acantholysis, “row of tombstones”, TZANCK cells** |
| **DIF** | **LINEAR IgG and C3 along the basement membrane** | **INTERCELLULAR IgG — “fishnet/chicken-wire”** |
| Prognosis | Self-limiting over years, better | **Fatal untreated** |

- Treatment: pemphigoid — potent topical clobetasol for localised disease, oral steroids ± doxycycline/nicotinamide, azathioprine, and rituximab in refractory cases; pemphigus — systemic corticosteroids + rituximab (now first-line with steroids), azathioprine, mycophenolate, IVIG.
- Dermatitis herpetiformis: intensely itchy grouped vesicles on extensor surfaces (elbows, knees, buttocks), granular IgA in dermal papillae, associated with coeliac disease, treat dapsone + gluten-free diet.
- Linear IgA disease: linear IgA at the basement membrane, “string of pearls” annular blisters, drug-induced by vancomycin.
- Nikolsky sign positive also in staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The image plus one adjective settles it — tense and itchy (elderly) = pemphigoid; flaccid with mouth ulcers = pemphigus. DIF pattern (linear vs fishnet) is the other guaranteed question.

**REMEMBER:** *Deeper split, tougher blister — pemphigoid is tense and spares the mouth.*

## Bacterial Infections

### 21. Lupus Vulgaris

**PEARL:** The commonest form of cutaneous tuberculosis in India — an annular/serpiginous plaque with an active advancing edge and central atrophy and scarring, classically on the face and buttocks. Diascopy shows “apple-jelly” nodules. Treated with standard anti-tubercular therapy.

**UNDERSTAND:** It occurs in a person with good immunity and prior sensitisation — hence the paucibacillary, slowly progressive, granulomatous lesion that simultaneously heals in the centre while extending at the edge. Histology shows well-formed tubercles with scanty or absent caseation in the upper dermis.

**MUST KNOW:**

- “Apple-jelly nodules” — press a glass slide on the lesion (diascopy); blanching the erythema reveals the yellow-brown granulomas beneath. A pure bedside sign, easily asked.
- Lupus vulgaris can undergo malignant transformation to squamous cell carcinoma in long-standing scarred lesions.
- Differential for an annular plaque — worth having ready: tinea corporis (peripherally raised scaly margin with central clearing, itchy, KOH positive); cutaneous leishmaniasis (central crusting/ulcer, face, endemic area); granuloma annulare (no scale, dermal papules, dorsa of hands); sarcoidosis (also apple-jelly on diascopy — the other granulomatous mimic); discoid lupus erythematosus (follicular plugging, scarring alopecia); leprosy (anaesthetic, hypopigmented, hair loss).
- Other cutaneous TB forms: scrofuloderma (direct extension from a node/bone with sinuses), TB verrucosa cutis (warty plaque, in the previously sensitised — “prosector’s wart”), miliary TB, and the tuberculids (erythema induratum of Bazin, lichen scrofulosorum) which are hypersensitivity reactions with no organisms in the lesion.
- Anaesthesia in the patch points to leprosy, not TB — the single quickest bedside discriminator in Indian practice.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Image of an annular plaque with central scarring → “which drug/therapy?” → anti-tubercular therapy.

**REMEMBER:** *Heals centrally, creeps outward, apple-jelly on diascopy — lupus vulgaris.*

### 22. Pitted Keratolysis

**PEARL:** Numerous small, shallow, punched-out craters on the pressure-bearing areas of the soles, with malodour and maceration, caused by *Corynebacterium* (and *Kytococcus sedentarius*) whose serine proteases digest keratin in the horny layer. It is associated with hyperhidrosis — not anhidrosis.

**UNDERSTAND:** The bacteria need moisture to proliferate and their proteases need a hydrated stratum corneum to act on. Sweat is therefore the essential precondition — which is why the cure is to dry the foot, not merely to kill the organism, and why it plagues those in occlusive footwear (soldiers, athletes, farmers in gumboots).

**MUST KNOW:**

- Treatment is directed at the hyperhidrosis: aluminium chloride, potassium permanganate soaks, iontophoresis, breathable footwear — plus topical fusidic acid, clindamycin or erythromycin.
- Diagnosis is entirely clinical — cultures are unhelpful because the flora is mixed.
- Most patients are unaware of the pits and present because of the smell (produced by sulphur compounds/thiols).
- Put it beside the other corynebacterial skin diseases: erythrasma (intertriginous red-brown patches, coral-red on Wood’s lamp) and trichomycosis axillaris (yellow concretions on axillary hair). All three are *Corynebacterium*, all three favour warm moist sites, and they frequently coexist.
- Differential for pitted soles: punctate palmoplantar keratoderma, arsenical keratoses, basal cell naevus syndrome (palmar pits), and Cowden/Darier disease — but the malodour and hyperhidrosis point straight to pitted keratolysis.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Incorrect statement” → “usually associated with anhidrosis” — it is hyperhidrosis.

**ALSO KNOW:** Caused most commonly by *Corynebacterium* species, also *Kytococcus sedentarius* (and *Dermatophilus congolensis*). Protease digestion of the stratum corneum creates the pits. Treatment: topical clindamycin/erythromycin or benzoyl peroxide + control of sweating (aluminium chloride).

**REMEMBER:** *Sweaty smelly feet with punched-out pits — corynebacterial proteases eating keratin. Dry the foot to cure it.*

### 23. Erythrasma

**PEARL:** Erythrasma = Corynebacterium minutissimum, showing coral-pink/red fluorescence under Wood’s lamp due to coproporphyrin III.

**UNDERSTAND:** The organism itself synthesises porphyrins, which fluoresce. Because the pigment is a bacterial metabolic product in the stratum corneum, washing the area before examination abolishes the fluorescence — a real cause of false negatives.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Presents as sharply demarcated brown, finely scaly patches in intertriginous sites — axilla, groin, intergluteal, submammary, and interdigital toe web (the commonest site overall).
- Predisposed by diabetes, obesity, humidity, hyperhidrosis.
- Treatment: topical/oral erythromycin or clarithromycin, topical fusidic acid; Whitfield’s ointment for the toe webs.
- Wood’s lamp colour set: coral-red = erythrasma; golden-yellow = pityriasis versicolor; green = Microsporum tinea capitis; pale blue-white = vitiligo; pink-red urine = porphyria; ash-leaf macules = tuberous sclerosis.
- Distinguish from trichomycosis axillaris (*C. Tenuis*, yellow concretions on axillary hair) and pitted keratolysis (*Kytococcus/Dermatophilus*, punched-out pits on the soles).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Species-level recall — minutissimum, not *tenuis* (that’s trichomycosis) and not *diphtheriae*.

**REMEMBER:** *Coral red in the fold — minutissimum.*

## Viral Infections

### 24. Herpes Zoster

**PEARL:** Reactivation of latent VZV from the dorsal root ganglion, giving unilateral grouped vesicles in a single dermatome that do not cross the midline. Mucous membranes within the affected dermatome are involved, and the posterior (sensory) nerve roots and ganglia show the inflammatory changes.

**UNDERSTAND:** The virus travels centrifugally down the sensory nerve from the ganglion — so the eruption maps exactly onto that nerve’s sensory territory, including any mucosa it supplies, and the pain precedes the rash by 1–3 days because the ganglionitis comes first.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Dermatome frequency: thoracic > cervical > trigeminal (ophthalmic) > lumbosacral.
- Herpes zoster ophthalmicus (V1): Hutchinson’s sign — vesicles on the tip/side of the nose (nasociliary nerve) predicts ocular involvement (keratitis, uveitis) — an urgent ophthalmology referral.
- Ramsay hunt syndrome (herpes zoster oticus) — geniculate ganglion: facial palsy + vesicles in the external auditory canal/pinna + ear pain, ± vertigo and hearing loss. Worse prognosis than Bell’s palsy — treat with aciclovir + steroids promptly.
- The patient IS infectious — from vesicle fluid (and nasopharynx in disseminated disease) — and a susceptible contact develops chickenpox, not zoster. Contagious until lesions crust.
- Antivirals within 72 hours reduce acute pain and the risk of post-herpetic neuralgia (the commonest complication; risk rises steeply with age). PHN is treated with amitriptyline, gabapentin/pregabalin, topical lidocaine or capsaicin.
- Disseminated zoster (>20 lesions outside the dermatome), multi-dermatomal or recurrent zoster in a young person → investigate for HIV or malignancy.
- “Zoster sine herpete” = the pain without any eruption.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Image of dermatomal vesicles → which statement is correct → mucous membranes in the dermatome are involved (the distractors claim it is non-infectious, trigeminal-predominant, or anterior-root).

**REMEMBER:** *One dermatome, never crossing the midline, posterior roots, mucosa included — and the contact gets chickenpox.*

### 25. Roseola Infantum

**PEARL:** High fever for 3–5 days in an infant, and the rash appears AS the fever falls — a non-pruritic rose-pink morbilliform eruption starting on the trunk. Nagayama spots = ulcers/papules at the uvulopalatoglossal junction. Cause: HHV-6 (and 6B, 7).

**UNDERSTAND:** The defining feature is the temporal dissociation — in measles, rubella and most exanthems the rash appears with the fever; in roseola the child is febrile and often surprisingly well, and then defervesces just as the rash erupts. That sequence alone makes the diagnosis.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Roseola is the commonest cause of febrile seizures in infancy (HHV-6 is neurotropic) — the reassuring explanation for a well child who convulsed with a high fever.
- Also called exanthem subitum (“sudden rash”) or sixth disease; typical age 6 months–2 years; leucopenia with relative lymphocytosis; self-limiting, treat supportively.
- The classic childhood exanthems — a table worth owning: 1st measles (Koplik spots, rash from behind the ears descending, rash with fever), 2nd scarlet fever (sandpaper rash, strawberry tongue, circumoral pallor, Pastia’s lines, desquamation), 3rd rubella (suboccipital/postauricular lymphadenopathy, Forchheimer spots), 4th “Filatov–Dukes” (historical), 5th erythema infectiosum/parvovirus B19 (“slapped cheek” then lacy reticular rash; causes aplastic crisis in sickle cell disease and hydrops fetalis), 6th roseola.
- Chickenpox for contrast: pleomorphic lesions in different stages simultaneously (macule, papule, vesicle, crust), centripetal distribution, intensely itchy.
- Congenital rubella: cataracts, sensorineural deafness and PDA, with a “blueberry muffin” rash (dermal erythropoiesis).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Infant + fever that breaks as the rash appears + oral ulcers at the uvulopalatoglossal junction → roseola infantum.

**ALSO KNOW:** Commonest cause of febrile seizures in this age group; also causes a bulging fontanelle. Rash appears after defervescence — the key clue (in measles, rash appears with peak fever and Koplik spots precede it).

**REMEMBER:** *Fever first, rash when the fever breaks, Nagayama spots in the mouth — and it is why babies fit.*

### 26. Eczema Herpeticum (Kaposi Varicelliform Eruption)

**PEARL:** Eczema herpeticum occurs in an immunocompetent host with pre-existing skin disease — most often atopic dermatitis — and is usually due to HSV-1. It has nothing to do with Kaposi sarcoma or HHV-8.

**UNDERSTAND:** The defect is barrier failure, not immune failure. Atopic skin has filaggrin deficiency, reduced antimicrobial peptides and fissured stratum corneum, letting HSV spread laterally across a huge surface. Systemic immunity is intact — which is why the patient is otherwise well and why the eruption stays cutaneous.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Presents with fever and monomorphic, “punched-out” umbilicated vesicles/erosions on eczematous skin, becoming haemorrhagic and crusted; heals with scarring.
- Causative agents: HSV-1 (commonest), HSV-2, VZV, coxsackie A6/A16 (eczema coxsackium), vaccinia (eczema vaccinatum — why smallpox vaccine is contraindicated in eczema).
- Treatment: oral or IV aciclovir, promptly — it is a dermatological emergency; add antibiotics for staphylococcal superinfection. Ocular involvement needs ophthalmology review.
- Predisposing skin diseases beyond atopic eczema: Darier’s disease, Hailey–Hailey, pemphigus, ichthyosis, burns, mycosis fungoides.
- Diagnosis: Tzanck smear (multinucleate giant cells), PCR for confirmation.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The whole question is untangling the eponym. Every wrong option pulls “Kaposi” toward HHV-8 and Kaposi sarcoma; the correct one states the true fact — immunocompetent host.

**REMEMBER:** *Kaposi’s name, herpes’ disease, broken barrier — not broken immunity.*

## Fungal Infections

### 28. Tinea Capitis and Kerion

**PEARL:** Kerion = a boggy, tender inflammatory mass with thick crusting, matted hair, pus and sinuses — the severe inflammatory form of tinea capitis, typically caused by a zoophilic dermatophyte (from a pet dog/cat, e.g. *Microsporum canis*).

**UNDERSTAND:** The intensity of inflammation reflects how foreign the fungus is to the host. Zoophilic and geophilic species provoke a violent immune response (kerion); anthropophilic species cause quiet, chronic, scaly patches. The pus in a kerion is sterile inflammation, not bacterial superinfection — which is why antibiotics alone fail and why incision and drainage is wrong.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Tinea capitis always needs oral antifungals — topical therapy cannot reach the hair follicle. Griseofulvin remains first-line for *Microsporum*; terbinafine is superior for *Trichophyton*. Add a short course of oral steroids in a severe kerion to limit scarring alopecia.
- A kerion causes scarring (cicatricial) alopecia if treated late — hence urgency.
- Patterns: endothrix (fungus inside the shaft — “black dot” tinea capitis, *T. Tonsurans*, *T. Violaceum*, does not fluoresce) vs ectothrix (*M. Canis*, *M. Audouinii* — grey patch, fluoresces blue-green). Favus (*T. Schoenleinii*) gives cup-shaped scutula.
- Tinea capitis is essentially a disease of children; in an adult, reconsider the diagnosis.
- Examine and treat family members and the pet; screen with brush cultures to prevent reinfection.
- Differential for the boggy scalp swelling: furuncle/carbuncle (bacterial, but no hair matting or diffuse alopecia), dissecting cellulitis, and a misdiagnosis of “abscess” leading to unnecessary incision — the classic clinical error.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Child + pet + boggy scalp swelling → kerion.

**REMEMBER:** *A boggy pustular scalp mass in a child with a pet is fungal, not bacterial — give oral griseofulvin, not a scalpel.*

### 29. Chromoblastomycosis — Copper Penny Bodies

**PEARL:** Cauliflower-like verrucous plaque on the foot of a barefoot farmer + “copper penny” (sclerotic/Medlar) bodies = chromoblastomycosis, caused by dematiaceous (pigmented) fungi, usually Fonsecaea pedrosoi.

**UNDERSTAND:** The fungus enters by traumatic implantation — a thorn prick in bare feet. Because the organism is pigmented and thick-walled, it survives phagocytosis and divides by septation without budding, producing the characteristic brown, coin-like sclerotic bodies visible even on H&E without a special stain.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Key diagnostic detail: sclerotic bodies are brown, round, with cross-walls, and divide by binary fission in two planes — they do not bud.
- Treatment: itraconazole or terbinafine, often prolonged; surgical excision for small lesions; cryotherapy as an adjunct.
- Compare the implantation mycoses: sporotrichosis — *Sporothrix schenckii*, nodules ascending along lymphatics (“rose gardener’s disease”, cigar-shaped yeasts); mycetoma (Madura foot) — grains/granules discharged through sinuses, may be eumycetoma or actinomycetoma; phaeohyphomycosis — same pigmented fungi but forms hyphae, not sclerotic bodies.
- Chromoblastomycosis vs mycetoma: both cause a swollen foot, but mycetoma has sinuses discharging grains and destroys bone; chromoblastomycosis is verrucous and superficial.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Copper penny bodies” is a giveaway phrase that maps to exactly one answer. Phaeohyphomycosis is the sophisticated distractor — same fungal group, different tissue form.

**REMEMBER:** *Copper pennies in a cauliflower foot.*

## Leprosy

### 30. Leprosy — Nerves, Biopsy and Prophylaxis

**PEARL:** Superficial (sensory) branch of the radial nerve at the wrist is the nerve of choice for biopsy in pure neuritic leprosy — a sensory nerve is chosen, never a mixed trunk. The most commonly affected peripheral nerve is the ulnar; the most commonly affected cranial nerve is the facial (VII).

**UNDERSTAND:** Biopsying a mixed motor–sensory trunk like the ulnar would leave a motor deficit — so despite the ulnar being the most involved nerve, you sample a purely sensory, expendable branch: superficial radial, dorsal ulnar cutaneous, or sural. Diagnostic yield versus functional cost.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Nerve biopsy is the GOLD standard for pure neuritic leprosy — the form with nerve involvement but no skin lesion, common in India and easily missed.
- *M. Leprae* favours cool superficial sites — which is why it picks the ulnar at the elbow, common peroneal at the fibular neck, posterior tibial, great auricular, and the facial nerve (causing lagophthalmos — the sight-threatening complication), and why it spares the warm axilla, groin, scalp and midline back.
- Ridley–Jopling spectrum: TT → BT → BB → BL → LL. Borderline (BB) forms are immunologically unstable and most prone to type 1 (reversal) reactions — delayed hypersensitivity with acute inflammation of skin lesions and nerves → treat urgently with steroids to save nerve function. Type 2 (ENL, erythema nodosum leprosum) occurs in BL/LL — immune complex mediated — treated with steroids and thalidomide (never in women of childbearing potential without contraception).
- Chemoprophylaxis (single-dose rifampicin, SDR-PEP) for contacts: age >2 years, contact ≥20 hours/week for ≥3 months. Withhold if: signs of leprosy or TB, liver or renal disease, pregnancy, or rifampicin in the last 2 years. Give only 4 weeks after the index case starts treatment.
- Sharing clothes/towels is not a criterion — transmission is by prolonged droplet/nasal contact, not fomites. That is the intended trap.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Nerve biopsied in pure neuritic leprosy” → superficial radial (ulnar is the trap). “Commonest cranial nerve” → facial.

**ALSO KNOW:** Leprosy treatment changed IN India from 1 April 2025: paucibacillary disease now receives the same three drugs as multibacillary — rifampicin, clofazimine and dapsone — for 6 months (MB remains 12 months), replacing the old two-drug PB regimen. Single-dose rifampicin post-exposure prophylaxis (SDR-PEP) is given to contacts, and the national goal is zero transmission by 2027.[^1]

**REMEMBER:** *Ulnar is most affected, but you biopsy the superficial radial — take sensation, never motor.*

### 31. Tuberculoid Leprosy and the Lepromin Test

**PEARL:** Tuberculoid (TT) leprosy — few well-defined anaesthetic lesions, thickened nerves, well-formed epithelioid granulomas with giant cells that erode the Grenz zone up to the epidermis, scanty bacilli, and a strongly positive lepromin (Mitsuda) test. Lepromatous (LL) — the exact opposite: lepromin negative.

**UNDERSTAND:** The lepromin test measures cell-mediated immunity, not infection — so it is positive where immunity is strong (TT) and negative where it has failed (LL). It is therefore a prognostic/classification tool, not a diagnostic test (most healthy adults are also positive).

**MUST KNOW:**

- The Ridley–Jopling spectrum runs on immunity: TT — Th1 response, granulomas, few lesions, marked nerve damage, bacilli absent (paucibacillary); LL — Th2 response, foamy macrophages (Virchow/lepra cells) with abundant bacilli (multibacillary), symmetrical widespread lesions, LEONINE facies, madarosis, saddle nose, and a clear Grenz zone beneath the epidermis.
- The Grenz zone is the discriminator on histology: a clear band of uninvolved papillary dermis, present in lepromatous, absent/eroded in tuberculoid.
- Nerve damage is worst in tuberculoid (immune-mediated destruction) even though bacilli are fewest — an important paradox. Bacillary load is highest in LL and that is what drives transmission.
- Slit-skin smear and the bacteriological index classify for treatment: WHO paucibacillary (1–5 lesions) vs multibacillary (>5).
- MDT (WHO): PB — rifampicin + dapsone for 6 months; MB — rifampicin + clofazimine + dapsone for 12 months. India now uses a uniform 3-drug regimen for all cases.
- Nerves to palpate: ulnar (at the elbow), median, radial cutaneous, common peroneal (fibular neck), posterior tibial, great auricular, supraorbital.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Solitary nodule + slight sensory loss + well-formed granulomas + intradermal antigen” → tuberculoid with a positive dermal (lepromin) test.

**REMEMBER:** *Good immunity: few bacilli, big granulomas, positive lepromin, badly damaged nerves — that is tuberculoid.*

### 32. Trophic (Neuropathic) Ulcer in Treated Leprosy

**PEARL:** A painless, punched-out ulcer with regular margins and hyperkeratotic/callous borders on a weight-bearing area of an anaesthetic foot is a trophic ulcer. Management is rest, NON-weight-bearing splints/footwear and foot care — not restarting MDT, not aggressive debridement, and certainly not amputation.

**UNDERSTAND:** The ulcer is caused by repeated unfelt trauma in an anaesthetic foot, not by active infection or persisting *M. Leprae*. Therefore the treatment must remove the mechanical cause. Antibacterials and debridement address a problem that is not there; only offloading allows healing.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Deformity and disability in leprosy continue after bacteriological cure — a central concept for both clinical practice and the NLEP. Patients are discharged as “cured” while their anaesthetic hands and feet remain at lifelong risk.
- Prevention is the whole of management: daily self-inspection of hands and feet, soaking and oiling, MICROCELLULAR rubber (MCR) footwear, protective gloves, and avoiding hot objects.
- WHO disability grading: Grade 0 — no anaesthesia, no visible deformity; Grade 1 — anaesthesia present, no visible deformity; Grade 2 — visible deformity or damage (including lagophthalmos, ulcer, claw hand, foot drop). Grade 2 disability rate is a key NLEP indicator of late diagnosis.
- Restart MDT only for genuine relapse — new skin lesions with increasing bacteriological index — not for ulcers or reactions.
- Beware the malignant transformation of a chronic trophic ulcer to squamous cell carcinoma (Marjolin’s ulcer) — biopsy any long-standing ulcer with heaped edges.
- The same principles govern the diabetic neuropathic foot — offloading with a total contact cast is the evidence-based standard.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Treated leprosy + painless plantar ulcer → “next step” → rest, splints and foot care.

**REMEMBER:** *An anaesthetic foot ulcerates from walking, not from bacilli — take the weight off it.*

### 33. Borderline (BB) Leprosy — Swiss Cheese Plaques

**PEARL:** Borderline leprosy shows annular plaques with sharply “punched-out” inner margins and sloping outer margins, often with islands of normal skin inside, giving a “Swiss cheese” appearance, and moderately diminished sensation. It is the most immunologically unstable part of the spectrum.

**UNDERSTAND:** The morphology mirrors the immunology. BB sits exactly midway between tuberculoid (few, well-defined, markedly anaesthetic) and lepromatous (many, ill-defined, symmetrical, sensation preserved until late). So its lesions are intermediate in number, definition and anaesthesia — the “punched-out inner edge” is the visual signature of that halfway position.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Instability is the clinically dangerous part: BB patients readily downgrade or upgrade, and are at highest risk of type 1 (reversal) reaction — acute inflammation of existing lesions with nerve pain, sudden nerve function loss and oedema of hands/feet. This is a medical emergency: give prednisolone promptly to prevent permanent deformity. Continue MDT during a reaction.
- Type 2 reaction (ENL) occurs in BL/LL: crops of tender erythematous nodules with fever and systemic upset, immune-complex mediated; treated with steroids and thalidomide (teratogenic — strict contraception).
- Diagnosis of leprosy requires at least one of the three cardinal signs: (1) hypopigmented/erythematous patch with definite sensory loss, (2) thickened/tender peripheral nerve, (3) positive slit-skin smear. The investigations used are nerve examination, slit-skin smear and skin biopsy — the Mantoux test has NO role.
- WHO/NLEP treatment: paucibacillary (1–5 lesions, ≤1 nerve) — rifampicin + dapsone for 6 months; multibacillary (>5 lesions, or >1 nerve trunk, or smear-positive) — rifampicin + clofazimine + dapsone for 12 months. A patient with a satellite lesion and a thickened nerve is treated as multibacillary for 12 months.
- Untreated leprosy: spontaneous remission does occur — most often in indeterminate leprosy, which has a higher rate of spontaneous resolution than tuberculoid.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Description of punched-out annular plaques with moderate hypoaesthesia → borderline. And blister-pack images → count lesions/nerves to choose PB vs MB.

**REMEMBER:** *Punched-out inner edge and Swiss-cheese islands = borderline — the unstable middle that reacts.*

### 35. Leprosy — Nerve Involvement

**PEARL:** Caused by *Mycobacterium leprae*; the most commonly affected peripheral nerve is the posterior tibial, then ulnar, median, and lateral popliteal. The commonest cranial nerve involved is the facial nerve. Incubation 2–12 years.

## Parasitic Infections and Infestations

### 36. Post-Kala-Azar Dermal Leishmaniasis

**PEARL:** In a patient from Bihar/eastern India with a history of prolonged fever in childhood, presenting with normo-anaesthetic (sensation intact) hypopigmented macules and NO thickened nerves, the diagnosis is PKDL — not leprosy.

**UNDERSTAND:** The two conditions look alike, and the discriminator is neurological: leprosy lesions are anaesthetic with thickened nerves; PKDL lesions have completely normal sensation because the parasite is confined to the dermis and does not invade nerves.

**MUST KNOW:**

- PKDL appears months to years after apparently cured visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar) — in India typically 6 months to 3 years later, in 5–10% of treated cases. It may also arise without a recognised prior VL episode.
- Lesion sequence: hypopigmented macules → erythematous papules/plaques → nodules, classically starting around the mouth and face and spreading; the face may be diffusely infiltrated.
- PKDL patients are the human reservoir of *Leishmania donovani* between epidemics in India — which is why detecting and treating PKDL is central to the KALA-AZAR elimination programme. A pure PSM crossover point.
- Treatment is prolonged: liposomal amphotericin B or miltefosine for 12 weeks — much longer than for VL itself.
- Diagnosis: slit-skin smear/biopsy showing LD bodies, rK39 serology (often positive), and PCR (most sensitive in the macular form, where parasites are scanty).
- Differential for hypopigmented macules in India: leprosy (anaesthetic), PKDL (normal sensation), pityriasis versicolor (scaly, KOH positive), pityriasis alba, vitiligo (depigmented, not hypo), and post-inflammatory hypopigmentation.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The stem plants “normo-anaesthetic” and “no thickened nerves” precisely to exclude leprosy — read those words.

**REMEMBER:** *Hypopigmented patch from Bihar with normal sensation and old fever — PKDL, the reservoir of kala-azar.*

### 37. Cutaneous Larva Migrans vs Larva Currens

**PEARL:** Cutaneous larva migrans (“creeping eruption”) is caused by NON-human hookworms — *Ancylostoma braziliense* and *A. Caninum* — from skin contact with soil contaminated by dog or cat faeces. The human hookworms *Ancylostoma duodenale* and *Necator americanus* do not cause it.

**UNDERSTAND:** Human hookworm larvae penetrate the skin and keep going — into the circulation, lungs and gut, where they mature. Animal hookworm larvae lack the enzymes to breach the human basement membrane, so they are trapped wandering in the epidermis, producing the visible serpiginous track. The disease exists precisely because the parasite is in the *wrong host*.

**MUST KNOW:**

- The speed distinguishes the two “migrating” eruptions: CLM advances ~1–2 cm per day; *Strongyloides* larva currens advances ~5–10 cm per hour — dramatically faster, hence “currens” (running), and typically around the buttocks, perineum and thighs (from autoinfection at the perianal skin).
- *Strongyloides* is the one that can kill: autoinfection allows lifelong persistence, and immunosuppression — especially corticosteroids and HTLV-1 — precipitates hyperinfection syndrome/disseminated strongyloidiasis with Gram-negative sepsis. Always screen and treat for strongyloides before starting steroids or immunosuppression in someone from an endemic area.
- Treatment of both: ivermectin (single dose for CLM; albendazole is the alternative). CLM is otherwise self-limiting over weeks.
- Clinical clue: intensely itchy serpiginous erythematous track on the feet, buttocks or thighs, appearing within 2 weeks of a beach holiday or walking barefoot in a tropical area.
- Löffler syndrome (transient pulmonary infiltrates with eosinophilia) is the shared feature of larvae transiting the lung — *Ascaris*, hookworm, *Strongyloides*.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Least likely to cause this” → *Ancylostoma duodenale* — because it is a *human* hookworm and passes straight through.

**REMEMBER:** *Animal hookworms get lost in human skin (1 cm/day); Strongyloides races (10 cm/hour) and can disseminate on steroids.*

### 38. Pediculosis Corporis — Vagabond’s Disease

**PEARL:** Body lice (*Pediculus humanus corporis*) live and lay eggs IN the seams of clothing, not on the body — so the diagnosis is made by examining the clothes. Chronic scratching produces the pigmented, lichenified back of “vagabond’s disease”. It is a louse, not a tick.

**UNDERSTAND:** This is the only louse that is not a permanent resident of the host — it visits the skin only to feed. That single biological fact dictates the entire management: decontaminating the clothing and bedding is the treatment, and treating the patient’s skin alone will fail.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Body lice are the vector of three named diseases — worth memorising as a set: epidemic typhus (*Rickettsia prowazekii*), trench fever (*Bartonella quintana*), and louse-borne relapsing fever (*Borrelia recurrentis*). All are diseases of war, famine, crowding, homelessness and refugee camps.
- Management: hot washing (>60°C) and hot ironing of clothes and bedding, improved hygiene, plus topical permethrin/pyrethrins or malathion for 8–24 hours if needed. Ivermectin orally for mass treatment.
- Compare the three lice: head louse (*P. H. Capitis*) — nits cemented to hair shafts, no disease transmission, common in schoolchildren; body louse — as above, the only vector; pubic/crab louse (*Pthirus pubis*) — coarse hair, causes Maculae ceruleae (blue-grey spots), and its presence in a child should raise the possibility of sexual abuse; if found on the eyelashes (phthiriasis palpebrarum), treat with petrolatum, not insecticide.
- Pubic lice mandate full STI screening, since co-infection is common.
- In epidemic typhus, the louse dies of the infection — it is not a lifelong reservoir; Brill–Zinsser disease is the late recrudescence in humans years later.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Incorrect statement” → “the causative organism is a tick” — it is a louse.

**REMEMBER:** *Look in the seams, not on the skin — and remember typhus, trench fever and relapsing fever.*

### 39. Maculae Ceruleae — Pediculosis Pubis

**PEARL:** Maculae ceruleae — slate-blue/grey non-blanching macules on the chest, abdomen and thighs — are pathognomonic of Pediculosis pubis (*Pthirus pubis*, the crab louse).

**UNDERSTAND:** They are haemosiderin-stained bite sites: louse saliva contains an anticoagulant, and altered blood pigment deposits in the dermis. Because the macules mark where lice have fed, they appear on the trunk while the lice themselves live in the pubic and other coarse body hair — the lesion and the parasite are in different places, which is why the diagnosis is missed.

**MUST KNOW:**

- *Pthirus pubis* also infests axillary hair, beard, eyelashes and eyebrows. Phthiriasis palpebrarum (eyelash infestation) in a child should prompt evaluation for sexual abuse.
- Treatment: permethrin 1% or 5%, malathion; oral ivermectin for resistant cases; treat sexual contacts and screen for other STIs. Eyelashes → thick petrolatum, not insecticide.
- Compare: Pediculosis corporis (*Pediculus humanus corporis*) — the louse lives in clothing seams, causes vagabond’s disease (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation and lichenification), and transmits epidemic typhus, trench fever and relapsing fever. Pediculosis capitis — nits on scalp hair.
- Scabies is the differential — but scabies gives burrows in web spaces and intense nocturnal itch, not blue macules.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Blue-grey oval macules on chest and abdomen” is the whole question. The trap is answering *corporis* because the lesions are on the body.

**REMEMBER:** *Blue spots on the trunk, lice in the pubic hair.*

## Sexually Transmitted Infections

### 40. Disseminated Gonococcal Infection

**PEARL:** The classic triad is tenosynovitis + dermatitis + migratory asymmetric polyarthralgia. The skin lesions are few (5–50), small, tender, pustular or vesiculopustular on a haemorrhagic/necrotic base, sited peripherally near the affected joints, including palms and soles.

**UNDERSTAND:** DGI is the haematogenous phase of gonococcal infection, and it presents in two overlapping forms: the “arthritis–dermatitis syndrome” (bacteraemic — blood cultures may be positive, joint fluid usually sterile) and true purulent septic arthritis (joint fluid positive, skin lesions absent). Recognising the first form matters because the joints are not yet destroyed.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Gonococcus is the commonest cause of septic arthritis in young sexually active adults; it is often polyarticular and migratory, unlike staphylococcal septic arthritis which is monoarticular.
- Screen for terminal complement (C5–C9) deficiency in recurrent or disseminated Neisserial infection — an essential immunology link.
- Investigations: culture all mucosal sites (urethral, cervical, rectal, pharyngeal) plus blood and synovial fluid; NAAT is the most sensitive. Always test for co-existing chlamydia, HIV and syphilis, and treat partners.
- Treatment: IV/IM ceftriaxone (plus azithromycin/doxycycline for chlamydia). Fluoroquinolones are no longer used because of resistance.
- Differentiate the triads: Reiter/reactive arthritis — arthritis + conjunctivitis + urethritis (“can’t see, can’t pee, can’t climb a tree”), HLA-B27, with keratoderma blennorrhagicum and circinate balanitis, and it is sterile/post-infectious. BEHÇET — oral + genital ulcers + uveitis, with pathergy. FITZ-Hugh–CURTIS — perihepatitis with “violin-string” adhesions complicating PID (gonococcus or chlamydia).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The triad is the question — memorise it as a unit.

**ALSO KNOW:** Reiter (reactive arthritis): arthritis + conjunctivitis + non-specific urethritis — follows *Chlamydia*, *Campylobacter*, *Salmonella*, *Shigella*, *Yersinia*. Behçet: pathergy test positive; HLA-B51; oral ulcers are the required criterion; Silk Route distribution. *Behçet = mouth+genital+eye (B51); Reiter = joint+eye+urethra (B27); DGI = tendon+skin+joint.*

**REMEMBER:** *Tenosynovitis, pustules and flitting joint pains in a young adult — culture every orifice and give ceftriaxone.*

### 41. Genital Ulcers — Read Pain and Tenderness

**PEARL:** Painless ulcer + painless (non-tender) lymphadenopathy = primary syphilis. Pain in either the ulcer or the node points elsewhere.

**UNDERSTAND:** The syphilitic chancre is an endarteritis with a dense plasma-cell infiltrate but relatively little acute inflammation and no suppuration — no pus, no pain. Chancroid, by contrast, is a suppurative bacterial ulcer, so both the ulcer and the node hurt and the node can rupture.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Syphilis (T. Pallidum) — single, indurated, clean-based, painless chancre; rubbery painless bilateral nodes; incubation 9–90 days; diagnosis by dark-ground microscopy and serology (VDRL/RPR screening, TPHA/FTA-ABS confirmation).
- Chancroid (H. Ducreyi) — multiple, soft, ragged, painful ulcers with tender suppurative unilateral bubo; “school of fish” on Gram stain.
- Herpes genitalis — grouped vesicles → multiple shallow painful erosions, tender nodes, recurrent.
- LGV (C. Trachomatis L1–L3) — transient painless ulcer then painful matted nodes, groove sign.
- Granuloma inguinale/donovanosis (Klebsiella granulomatis) — painless beefy-red friable ulcer, no lymphadenopathy (pseudobubo), Donovan bodies.
- Painless pair: syphilis and donovanosis. Distinguish them by induration + nodes (syphilis) vs beefy granulation + no nodes (donovanosis).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The two adjectives in the stem — painless ulcer, non-tender nodes — are the entire discriminator. Read them before looking at the options.

**REMEMBER:** *No pain, no pus, no tenderness — treponeme.*

## Disorders of Pigmentation

### 43. Chemical (Contact) Leukoderma

**PEARL:** Bindi leukoderma → para-tertiary BUTYLPHENOL (PTBP) in adhesive bindis. Rubber/footwear/condoms → MONOBENZYL ether of hydroquinone (MBEH). Hair dye → PARAPHENYLENEDIAMINE (PPD). Alta (red foot dye) → azo dyes (Crocein Scarlet MOO, Solvent Yellow 3).

**UNDERSTAND:** These chemicals are structural analogues of tyrosine, so they are taken up by melanocytes, compete for tyrosinase, generate free radicals and induce melanocyte apoptosis — genuine destruction, not just inhibition. That is why the depigmentation is often permanent and can spread beyond the contact site.

**MUST KNOW:**

- This is a quintessentially Indian exam topic — bindi, alta, kumkum, hair dye and rubber chappals are the classic sources; the history of the offending product is the diagnosis.
- Distinguishing from vitiligo is the clinical challenge: chemical leukoderma is initially confined to the contact site, with confetti-like macules at the margins, and there is a clear exposure history. However, it can trigger true vitiligo in predisposed individuals, and the two may coexist.
- MBEH (monobenzone) is used therapeutically for permanent depigmentation of residual normal skin in extensive/universal vitiligo — the same toxicity used deliberately.
- PPD is also the commonest cause of allergic contact dermatitis to hair dye and “black henna” tattoos — with acute severe facial and scalp oedema. Genuine henna (lawsone) is safe; the added PPD is not.
- Management: identify and stop the exposure; treat as for vitiligo (topical steroids/calcineurin inhibitors, phototherapy) — repigmentation is often incomplete.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Bindi leukoderma is caused by” → PTBP. Learn all four pairs — they are examined interchangeably.

**REMEMBER:** *Bindi–PTBP, rubber–MBEH, hair dye–PPD, alta–azo dyes. All are tyrosine look-alikes that kill melanocytes.*

### 44. Segmental Vitiligo

**PEARL:** Segmental vitiligo — unilateral depigmented macules in a band/segmental (often dermatomal-like, Blaschkoid) distribution that do not cross the midline, with early leukotrichia (white hair), typically in children and young people.

**UNDERSTAND:** The leukotrichia matters prognostically — it means the melanocyte reservoir in the hair follicle bulge has been destroyed. Since repigmentation normally occurs by melanocytes migrating out of the follicle (producing the characteristic perifollicular repigmentation), loss of that reservoir means medical therapy will not work — which is why segmental vitiligo is the type referred for surgical grafting.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Segmental vs NON-segmental: segmental is unilateral, stabilises early (within 1–2 years), rarely associated with autoimmune disease, and responds poorly to medical treatment. Non-segmental (generalised/acrofacial) is bilateral and symmetrical, progressive, Koebner-positive, and strongly associated with autoimmune disease — thyroid (commonest), pernicious anaemia, type 1 diabetes, Addison’s disease, alopecia areata.
- Screen non-segmental vitiligo patients for thyroid disease (TSH, anti-TPO) — a routine and examinable point.
- Sites of predilection are areas of friction and trauma — dorsa of hands, fingers, elbows, knees, ankles (Koebner phenomenon).
- Differential diagnosis of a white patch: nevus depigmentosus (congenital, stable, non-progressive, does not enhance on Wood’s lamp), nevus anemicus (vascular — disappears on diascopy), piebaldism (congenital, autosomal dominant kit mutation, with a white forelock and a stable ventral midline patch), pityriasis alba, post-inflammatory hypopigmentation, leprosy (anaesthetic, hypo- not depigmented), and pityriasis versicolor.
- Treatment: topical steroids/calcineurin inhibitors, NB-UVB (best for generalised disease), and surgical melanocyte transfer for stable segmental disease. JAK inhibitors (ruxolitinib cream) are the newest option.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Image of a unilateral band of depigmentation with white hairs → segmental vitiligo (piebaldism is the congenital trap).

**REMEMBER:** *Unilateral, doesn’t cross the midline, white hairs early — segmental vitiligo, and it needs surgery not creams.*

### 45. Reticulate Pigmentary Disorders

**PEARL:** Dowling–Degos disease — reticulate (lacy/net-like) hyperpigmentation of the flexures, with comedo-like “dark dot” follicles and pitted acneiform scars near the angle of the mouth. Reticulate acropigmentation of Kitamura — hyperpigmented atrophic macules on the dorsa of the hands with palmar pits and breaks in the dermatoglyphics.

**UNDERSTAND:** The distinguishing axis is site: Dowling–Degos is flexural (axillae, groin, neck, inframammary) with perioral pitted scars; Kitamura is acral (hand dorsa and palms). Both are autosomal dominant disorders of keratin 5 / follicular epidermal proliferation.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Dowling–Degos is a differential for acanthosis nigricans (both flexural and hyperpigmented) — but DDD is reticulate/lacy with comedones and no velvety thickening, and there is no insulin resistance.
- Its overlap syndromes: Galli–Galli disease (Dowling–Degos with acantholysis on histology), and Haber syndrome (with a rosacea-like facial eruption).
- The other reticulate hyperpigmentations to keep separate: dyskeratosis congenita — the triad of reticulate pigmentation, nail dystrophy and oral leukoplakia, with bone marrow failure and cancer risk (a telomere disorder — the one that matters systemically); confluent and reticulated papillomatosis of Gougerot–Carteaud (treated with minocycline); erythema ab igne (from chronic heat exposure); prurigo pigmentosa; and poikiloderma.
- Rothmund–Thomson and Cockayne syndromes are DNA repair genodermatoses with photosensitivity — offered as distractors here, but they present with poikiloderma, growth failure and cataracts, not a purely flexural reticulate pattern.
- Treatment of DDD is unsatisfactory — laser and topical retinoids are tried; the importance is recognising it and not over-investigating for endocrine disease.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Fine reticular hyperpigmentation with pits near the angle of the mouth” → Dowling–Degos disease. The perioral pits are the discriminator from Kitamura.

**REMEMBER:** *Dowling–Degos folds inward (flexures, perioral pits); Kitamura goes outward (hand dorsa, palmar pits).*

### 46. Lentigo vs Freckle vs Naevus

**PEARL:** Lentigo simplex is due to an increase IN the number of melanocytes at the dermo-epidermal junction. An EPHELIS (freckle) has a normal number of melanocytes but increased melanin production, and darkens with sun exposure. A junctional naevus has melanocytes in nests.

**understand:** The three-way distinction is: more pigment (freckle) → more melanocytes, singly (lentigo) → melanocytes in clusters (naevus). It is a progression of melanocytic organisation, and it explains the clinical behaviour: freckles fade in winter; lentigines do not fade because the cells themselves have increased.

**MUST KNOW:**

- “Lentiginous” on histology = melanocytes increased singly along the basal layer, with elongated rete ridges.
- Lentigo simplex appears in childhood, is unrelated to sun exposure, and is few in number. Solar lentigo (“liver spot”) appears in older, sun-damaged skin; PUVA lentigines follow phototherapy.
- Multiple lentigines are a syndrome marker — an essential list: Peutz–Jeghers (perioral and buccal lentigines + hamartomatous GI polyps + intussusception + cancer risk), LEOPARD/Noonan with multiple lentigines, Carney complex (LAMB/name), Laugier–Hunziker (benign, lips and nails), and Bannayan–Riley–Ruvalcaba (penile lentigines, PTEN).
- Lentigo maligna is entirely different and must not be confused — it is melanoma in situ on chronically sun-damaged skin of the elderly face, presenting as a slowly enlarging irregular brown-black macule; progression to lentigo maligna melanoma occurs over years.
- Any lentigo that changes, enlarges, becomes irregular in border or colour, or is >6 mm needs dermoscopy and biopsy — the ABCDE rule applies.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Reason for development of simple lentigo” → increased melanocytes (increased melanin is the freckle — the intended trap).

**REMEMBER:** *Freckle = more pigment; lentigo = more melanocytes, singly; naevus = melanocytes in nests.*

### 47. Nevus Anemicus — The “Pharmacological” Nevus

**PEARL:** Nevus anemicus is due to localized vasoconstriction (hypersensitivity of vessels to catecholamines), not pigment loss. On diascopy, the lesion becomes indistinguishable from surrounding blanched skin. In vitiligo, melanin is lost, so the lesion stays paler.

**UNDERSTAND:** Press a glass slide on normal skin → blood is squeezed out → normal skin becomes as pale as the nevus → the border vanishes. Vitiligo has no melanin, so blanching cannot equalize it.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Rubbing/heat → surrounding skin flushes, lesion does not (no reactive hyperemia).
- Wood’s lamp: vitiligo enhances (chalky white); nevus anemicus does not.
- Associated with NF1 and tuberous sclerosis.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Differentiating hypopigmented macules — nevus anemicus (vascular), nevus depigmentosus (functional melanocyte defect), vitiligo (melanocyte destruction).

**REMEMBER:** *Nevus anemicus = pale because of vessels, not pigment — diascopy makes it disappear.*

### 49. Vitiligo — Definition and Types

**PEARL:** Acquired depigmentation from autoimmune destruction of dopa-positive melanocytes in the basal layer → milky-white, sharply demarcated macules.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Segmental: unilateral, band/dermatomal pattern, early onset, stabilizes fast, poor response to medical therapy — best for surgical grafting.
- Non-segmental (generalized): bilateral symmetrical macules, acrofacial or scattered; associated with other autoimmune disease (thyroid commonest) — screen TSH.
- Acrofacial: periorificial (eyes, lips) + acral (periungual, palms, soles); therapy-resistant because of the absence of hair follicles (follicular melanocyte reservoir supplies repigmentation).
- Repigmentation is typically perifollicular — hence hairy sites respond best; NB-UVB is the standard for extensive disease.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Wood’s lamp accentuates vitiligo (chalky/blue-white); leucotrichia indicates a poor prognosis.

**REMEMBER:** *No hair follicles = no melanocyte reservoir = acrofacial vitiligo resists treatment.*

## Hair and Nail Disorders

### 51. Hair Shaft Disorders — Read the Microscope

**PEARL:** Trichothiodystrophy — “tiger tail” alternating light and dark banding on polarised microscopy (sulphur-deficient brittle hair). Trichorrhexis nodosa — “two paintbrushes pushed together” (nodes and fractures). Trichorrhexis invaginata — “bamboo hair” / ball-and-socket, in Netherton syndrome. Monilethrix — regular beading (elliptical nodes with constrictions).

**understand:** Each pattern reflects a different structural failure: trichothiodystrophy lacks the sulphur-rich cysteine cross-links that give keratin its strength; trichorrhexis invaginata is a soft, incompletely keratinised shaft telescoping into itself; monilethrix is a periodic defect in shaft diameter.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Trichothiodystrophy — autosomal recessive DNA repair defect with brittle hair, ichthyosis, photosensitivity, intellectual impairment and short stature (“PIBIDS”). Crucially, unlike xeroderma pigmentosum (also a nucleotide excision repair defect), there is NO increased risk of skin cancer — a favourite discriminator.
- Netherton syndrome (SPINK5) — bamboo hair + ichthyosis linearis circumflexa (double-edged migratory scaly rings) + atopy with very high IgE; beware systemic absorption of topical agents through the defective barrier.
- Trichorrhexis nodosa is the commonest hair shaft abnormality — usually acquired from trauma (excess brushing, heat, chemicals), but classically associated with argininosuccinic aciduria (a urea cycle disorder) — the metabolic link worth remembering.
- Monilethrix — autosomal dominant (keratin genes), with keratosis pilaris on the nape and beaded hair that breaks, leaving alopecia.
- Pili torti (flattened, twisted 180°) → Menkes disease (kinky hair) and Björnstad syndrome (with deafness).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** A polarised light microscopy image of hair → name the disorder. The banding pattern is the whole question.

**REMEMBER:** *Tiger tail = trichothiodystrophy (no cancer risk); bamboo = Netherton; beads = monilethrix; paintbrush = trichorrhexis nodosa.*

### 52. Nail Signs

**PEARL:** Beau’s lines — transverse grooves (temporary arrest of nail growth after a systemic insult). Mees’ lines — transverse white bands (true leukonychia), classically arsenic, also thallium, chemotherapy and renal failure. LINDSAY’S “half-and-half” nail — proximal white, distal pink/brown, specific for chronic kidney disease. Terry’s nail — proximal white with a narrow distal pink band, in cirrhosis and hypoalbuminaemia.

**UNDERSTAND:** The key distinction is groove versus colour. Beau’s lines are a defect in nail plate thickness — the matrix stopped working for a while. Mees’ lines are a defect of pigment within the plate — so they move distally as the nail grows (allowing you to date the insult), and they do not blanch on pressure. Muehrcke’s lines do blanch because they are in the nail *bed*, not the plate — and therefore do not migrate.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Dating an illness with Beau’s lines: fingernails grow ~0.1 mm/day (3 mm/month), so the distance from the cuticle tells you roughly when the insult occurred. Causes: severe systemic illness, MI, high fever (measles, mumps, pneumonia), chemotherapy, major surgery, and Kawasaki disease. Complete shedding = onychomadesis (a severe version — classically after hand-foot-and-mouth disease).
- Clubbing — loss of the Lovibond angle, positive Schamroth’s window test; causes: lung cancer, bronchiectasis, lung abscess, empyema, ILD, cyanotic heart disease, infective endocarditis, IBD, cirrhosis. Not in COPD alone — a classic trap.
- Koilonychia (spoon nails) — iron deficiency. Trachyonychia (rough, sandpaper nails) — alopecia areata and lichen planus. Pterygium — lichen planus (scarring). Onycholysis — psoriasis, thyrotoxicosis (Plummer’s nail), trauma, tetracyclines.
- Psoriasis nails: irregular, deep pitting + subungual hyperkeratosis + oil-drop/salmon patch + onycholysis. Alopecia areata pitting is fine, shallow and geometric (regular) — that regularity is the discriminator.
- Splinter haemorrhages — trauma (commonest), infective endocarditis, vasculitis, psoriasis.
- AMOROLFINE is the only MORPHOLINE antifungal and is used as a nail lacquer for onychomycosis (ciclopirox is a hydroxypyridone; the “-azoles” are imidazoles).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Nail image after recent major surgery/illness → Beau’s lines; white transverse bands → Mees’; half-and-half → renal failure.

**ALSO KNOW:** Each sign localises to which part of the nail unit is diseased. Disease of the nail matrix (where the plate is made) produces surface defects — pitting, trachyonychia, Beau’s lines, and if the matrix is destroyed and scarred, the proximal nail fold fuses to the bed as a pterygium. Disease of the nail bed (beneath the plate) produces colour and adhesion changes — the salmon patch, onycholysis and subungual hyperkeratosis. So the site of the abnormality tells you where in the nail unit to look for the pathology. Psoriatic nail signs: irregular, deep, coarse pitting (matrix), salmon patch/oil-drop discoloration (bed), onycholysis with an erythematous border, subungual hyperkeratosis, and splinter haemorrhages. Nail involvement strongly predicts psoriatic arthritis, especially the dip pattern — because the nail matrix, dip joint and extensor enthesis are one functional unit. Auspitz sign — pinpoint bleeding after scraping the scale, from thinned suprapapillary plates over dilated dermal papillary capillaries; preceded by the candle-grease (grattage) sign. Both are elicited in the video-based version of this question. Lichen planus nails: longitudinal ridging, thinning, and dorsal pterygium from scarring of the matrix (permanent) — plus the skin 5 Ps and Wickham striae. *Matrix disease roughens the surface; bed disease colours it — the oil drop is psoriasis’ signature.*

**REMEMBER:** *Grooves = Beau (growth arrest). White bands = Mees (arsenic). Half-and-half = kidneys. Terry’s = liver.*

### 53. Trichotillomania

**PEARL:** NON-scarring alopecia, typically at the vertex/frontoparietal region, with hairs of different lengths and an irregular, bizarre outline. Histology: hair loss with haemorrhage into the follicles, empty follicles, trichomalacia (distorted, damaged shafts) and pigment casts, with only a mild inflammatory infiltrate.

**UNDERSTAND:** The follicular haemorrhage and trichomalacia are the fingerprints of traction — hairs physically pulled out mid-anagen. No spontaneous disease produces intrafollicular haemorrhage; the histology therefore proves the mechanism, which is why it is the exam’s chosen clue.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Distinguish from alopecia areata: areata gives smooth, round, completely bald patches with “exclamation mark” hairs at the margin, a positive hair pull test, fine geometric nail pitting, and histology of a peribulbar lymphocytic infiltrate (“swarm of bees”) around anagen follicles. In trichotillomania the patch is irregular with broken hairs of varying length and the pull test is negative.
- Distinguish from tinea capitis — scaling, black dots, lymphadenopathy, KOH/culture positive; and from telogen effluvium — diffuse shedding 2–3 months after a stressor (fever, surgery, childbirth, severe illness, iron deficiency), with a positive pull test and complete recovery.
- Classification: trichotillomania is a body-focused repetitive behaviour, grouped in DSM-5 with obsessive-compulsive and related disorders. Treatment: habit reversal training (first-line), N-acetylcysteine, and SSRIs/clomipramine for comorbid disease.
- TRICHOBEZOAR is the complication to remember — from trichophagia (eating the pulled hair): a gastric hair ball, and if it extends into the small bowel, RAPUNZEL syndrome, presenting with obstruction and a palpable mass.
- The scarring alopecias for contrast (loss of follicular ostia — irreversible): discoid lupus, lichen planopilaris, folliculitis decalvans, dissecting cellulitis, central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Non-scarring vertex alopecia + follicular haemorrhage on histology → trichotillomania (alopecia areata is the intended distractor).

**REMEMBER:** *Broken hairs of different lengths and blood in the follicle — the hairs were pulled, not lost.*

### 54. Alopecia Areata — The Terminology

**PEARL:** Alopecia totalis = loss of ALL scalp hair. Alopecia universalis = loss of ALL body hair. Ophiasis = a band of hair loss along the occiput/back of the scalp extending toward both ears (Greek *ophis*, snake). Spontaneous remission occurs in ~80% of limited patchy disease of <1 year.

**UNDERSTAND:** The ophiasis pattern carries a distinctly worse prognosis than ordinary patchy disease — as do early onset in childhood, atopy, nail involvement, extensive disease and a long duration. Prognosis in alopecia areata is read off the pattern, which is why the pattern names are examined.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Alopecia areata is an autoimmune, T-cell mediated attack on the anagen hair bulb with “collapse of immune privilege”. Histology: peribulbar lymphocytic infiltrate — the “swarm of bees”.
- Signs: smooth, completely bald, round or oval patches with NO scarring and preserved follicular ostia (hence reversible); “exclamation mark” hairs at the active edge (tapering proximally); a positive hair pull test at the periphery; and regrowth of fine white hairs initially. Nails show fine, regular, geometric pitting and trachyonychia.
- Associations to screen for: thyroid disease (commonest), vitiligo, type 1 diabetes, pernicious anaemia, atopy, and down syndrome.
- Treatment: intralesional/topical corticosteroids, topical minoxidil, contact immunotherapy (DPCP) for extensive disease, and now JAK inhibitors (baricitinib, ritlecitinib) — approved for severe alopecia areata and the major recent advance.
- “sudden WHITENING of hair” (as attributed to Marie Antoinette) is explained by alopecia areata preferentially attacking pigmented hairs and sparing white ones.
- Distinguish from androgenetic alopecia (patterned, miniaturisation, treated with minoxidil/finasteride), telogen effluvium (diffuse, post-stressor, reversible) and the scarring alopecias (ostia lost — irreversible).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Pure terminology — totalis = scalp, universalis = whole body, ophiasis = snake-like band at the back.

**REMEMBER:** *Totalis is the scalp, universalis is everything, ophiasis creeps around the back like a snake — and it does badly.*

### 55. The Hair Cycle

**PEARL:** 80–90% of scalp hairs are in anagen at any time. Anagen duration determines final hair length.

**UNDERSTAND:** Hair grows at a roughly constant ~1 cm/month, so the only variable that can set maximum length is how long the follicle stays in the growth phase. Scalp anagen lasts 2–6 years (hence long hair); eyebrow and eyelash anagen lasts weeks (hence short).

**MUST KNOW:**

- Anagen (growth, 2–6 years, 80–90%) — pigmented, angulated roots on trichogram. Catagen (involution by apoptosis of the lower follicle, ~3 weeks, 1–2%). Telogen (rest, ~3 months, 10–15%) — club hair, shed actively by exogen. Kenogen = the empty-follicle latency between shedding and the next anagen.
- Telogen effluvium — a stressor (fever, surgery, childbirth = telogen gravidarum, crash diet, haemorrhage) prematurely ends anagen; shedding begins 2–3 months later, diffuse, never total baldness, self-limiting.
- Anagen effluvium — chemotherapy/radiation, shedding within days, affects the 90% in anagen → near-total loss.
- Non-scarring alopecia: alopecia areata, telogen effluvium, androgenetic alopecia, tinea capitis, trichotillomania, traction. Scarring (cicatricial): frontal fibrosing alopecia, lichen planopilaris, discoid lupus, folliculitis decalvans, pseudopelade. The discriminator is loss of follicular ostia.
- Alopecia areata: T-cell mediated, exclamation-mark hairs, associated with vitiligo, thyroiditis, SLE; nail pitting.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Not true about the hair cycle” → the 50% in anagen option (it is 80–90%). Separately, the scarring/non-scarring grouping question always plants frontal fibrosing alopecia as the scarring one.

**REMEMBER:** *Nine in ten are growing; how long they grow sets how long they get.*

### 61. Epidermolysis Bullosa and the Collodion Baby

**PEARL:** EB simplex — keratin 5/14 (intra-epidermal, basal cell) · junctional EB — laminin-332 (within the lamina lucida) · dystrophic EB — collagen VII, the anchoring fibrils (below the lamina densa). Blisters appear at sites of friction and mechanical trauma.

**UNDERSTAND:** The depth of the defective protein determines the depth of the split and therefore the scarring. Keratin defects split high → heal without scarring (simplex). Collagen VII anchors the epidermis to dermis → its loss splits below the basement membrane → scarring, milia, and mitten deformity (pseudosyndactyly) in dystrophic EB.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Recessive dystrophic EB carries a very high lifetime risk of aggressive squamous cell carcinoma in chronically eroded skin — the leading cause of death in adults.
- Junctional EB (Herlitz) is often lethal in infancy — exuberant granulation tissue, especially perioral and perinasal, plus airway involvement.
- A positive family history with a sibling death in the neonatal period (as in this stem) points to an inherited mechanobullous disease rather than an acquired or infectious one.
- Collodion baby — a neonate encased in a shiny, taut, parchment-like membrane, with ectropion and eclabium — belongs to the autosomal recessive congenital ichthyosis spectrum: lamellar ichthyosis, congenital ichthyosiform erythroderma, harlequin ichthyosis, bathing suit ichthyosis, and self-improving congenital ichthyosis.
- Ichthyosis vulgaris is not part of that spectrum — it is autosomal dominant, due to filaggrin (FLG) mutations, appears after the first few months, spares the flexures, shows hyperlinear palms, and is strongly associated with atopic dermatitis (filaggrin is the key barrier protein). X-linked ichthyosis (steroid sulfatase deficiency) has dark “dirty” scales, corneal opacities, and is associated with cryptorchidism and prolonged labour.
- Collodion baby management: humidified incubator, emollients, fluid and electrolyte monitoring, sepsis watch — the membrane sheds over 2–4 weeks.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Neonatal blistering with a dead sibling → epidermolysis bullosa. Collodion baby → “unlikely diagnosis” → ichthyosis vulgaris.

**ALSO KNOW:** Diagnosis: skin biopsy with immunofluorescence antigen mapping / EM (defines the level of split).

**REMEMBER:** *Simplex heals clean (keratin, high); dystrophic scars and turns malignant (collagen VII, deep).*

### 62. Carney Complex — NAME and LAMB

**PEARL:** Name = Nevi, Atrial myxoma, Myxoid neurofibroma, Ephelides. LAMB = Lentigines, Atrial myxoma, Blue naevi. Both are subsets of Carney complex — autosomal dominant, PRKAR1A mutation. Ebstein anomaly is not part of it.

**UNDERSTAND:** Carney complex is a multiple endocrine and myxomatous neoplasia syndrome with spotty skin pigmentation. The unifying lesion is the myxoma — in heart, skin and breast — and the unifying skin sign is pigmented macules in unusual sites (lips, conjunctiva, vaginal and penile mucosa).

**MUST KNOW:**

- Cardiac myxoma is the feature that kills — it is the commonest primary cardiac tumour, usually in the left atrium attached to the fossa ovalis, causing a “tumour plop”, positional syncope/dyspnoea, embolic stroke, and constitutional symptoms with raised IL-6. In Carney complex the myxomas are multiple, recurrent, and can be in any chamber — unlike sporadic ones.
- Endocrine features: primary pigmented nodular adrenocortical disease → ACTH-independent Cushing syndrome (with a paradoxical rise in cortisol on dexamethasone — Liddle’s test), pituitary adenoma → acromegaly, large-cell calcifying Sertoli cell tumour of the testis, and thyroid nodules.
- Screen affected individuals with annual echocardiography — the single most important surveillance action.
- Differentiate from Peutz–Jeghers syndrome (STK11): perioral/mucosal lentigines with hamartomatous GI polyps and intussusception, and from LEOPARD syndrome (PTPN11): Lentigines, ECG abnormalities, Ocular hypertelorism, Pulmonary stenosis, Abnormal genitalia, Retardation of growth, Deafness.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “All are part of name syndrome except” → Ebstein anomaly (a cardiac lesion inserted to catch you).

**also know:** Gene: PRKAR1A (17q). Atrial myxoma is the lethal component — echo screening is mandatory.

**REMEMBER:** *Myxomas plus spotty pigmentation plus endocrine overactivity — and always echo the heart.*

### 63. Tuberous Sclerosis — Major vs Minor Criteria

**PEARL:** Definite TSC = 2 major, or 1 major + 2 minor. Probable = 1 major + 1 minor. Possible = 1 major, OR 2 or more minor. So gingival fibromas + bone cysts + pitted dental enamel (three minor features) = possible TSC.

**UNDERSTAND:** The split is by specificity: major features are lesions that are essentially diagnostic hamartomas (facial angiofibromas, ungual fibromas, shagreen patch, cortical tuber, cardiac rhabdomyoma, renal angiomyolipoma); minor features are common or non-specific findings (dental pits, bone cysts, gingival fibromas) that only count in combination.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Genetics: autosomal dominant, TSC1 (hamartin, chr 9) or TSC2 (tuberin, chr 16) — both inhibit mTOR, which is why mTOR inhibitors (everolimus, sirolimus/topical rapamycin) now treat SEGAs, angiomyolipomas and facial angiofibromas. TSC2 disease is more severe, and its contiguous deletion with PKD1 gives TSC with polycystic kidneys.
- The skin signs by age: hypomelanotic “ASH-leaf” macules are the earliest sign, present at birth — use a Wood’s lamp to find them in a fair infant with seizures. Then facial angiofibromas (adenoma sebaceum, age 3–5, over the nasolabial folds — sparing the upper lip), shagreen patch (lumbosacral connective-tissue naevus), “confetti” lesions, and periungual fibromas (Koenen tumours) at puberty.
- The classic triad (Vogt) — seizures, intellectual disability and adenoma sebaceum — occurs in fewer than a third; do not require it.
- Infantile spasms with hypsarrhythmia is the classic neurological presentation — vigabatrin is the drug of choice specifically in TSC-associated spasms.
- What to screen for and why: cardiac rhabdomyoma (commonest fetal cardiac tumour; often regresses spontaneously), renal angiomyolipoma (risk of catastrophic haemorrhage — Wunderlich syndrome; the leading cause of adult death in TSC), SEGA (obstructive hydrocephalus at the foramen of Monro), and lymphangioleiomyomatosis in women.
- Contrast NF1 diagnostic criteria (≥2 of): ≥6 café-au-lait macules, ≥2 neurofibromas or 1 plexiform, axillary/inguinal freckling (Crowe’s sign), optic glioma, ≥2 Lisch nodules, a bony lesion (sphenoid dysplasia), and a first-degree relative.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The question gives you a list and asks for the diagnostic category — count majors and minors.

**REMEMBER:** *Two majors make it definite; gingival fibromas, bone cysts and dental pits are all minor — so only “possible”.*

### 64. The Buttonhole Sign

**PEARL:** The buttonhole (invagination) sign — a soft lesion that can be invaginated into the subcutis with the fingertip and springs back on release — is seen in neurofibromas (von Recklinghausen’s disease), anetoderma and dermatofibroma. It is not a feature of bullous pemphigoid.

**UNDERSTAND:** The sign requires a defect or laxity in the dermal connective tissue through which the lesion can be pushed. In anetoderma the dermal elastic tissue is lost (leaving a herniating sac); in neurofibroma the tumour is soft and the overlying dermis attenuated.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Dermatofibroma actually has a better-known sign — the dimple (Fitzpatrick) sign: lateral compression produces a central dimple, because the lesion is tethered to the overlying epidermis. Both signs can be elicited; the dimple sign is the classic one.
- Anetoderma = localised loss of dermal elastic tissue → soft, wrinkled, herniating outpouchings; associated with antiphospholipid antibodies and SLE — worth screening.
- NF1 skin lesions: café-au-lait macules (≥6, >5 mm prepubertal / >15 mm postpubertal), axillary and inguinal freckling, cutaneous and plexiform neurofibromas (the plexiform type can undergo malignant transformation to MPNST — suspect with rapid growth or pain), and Lisch nodules (iris hamartomas — a slit-lamp finding, present in nearly all adults with NF1).
- NF1 systemic risks: optic pathway glioma, learning difficulties, scoliosis, phaeochromocytoma and renal artery stenosis (causing hypertension — always check the BP), and pseudarthrosis of the tibia.
- NF2 is different: bilateral vestibular schwannomas, few skin lesions, posterior subcapsular cataract (“NF2 = 2 ears”).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Buttonhole sign is seen in all except” → bullous pemphigoid (a blistering disease, not a dermal defect).

**ALSO KNOW:** NF1 diagnostic criteria: ≥6 café-au-lait macules, ≥2 neurofibromas or 1 plexiform, axillary/inguinal freckling (Crowe’s sign), Lisch nodules, optic glioma, bony dysplasia, first-degree relative. Gene: NF1 on 17q (neurofibromin, a Ras-GAP). NF2 = 22q, bilateral acoustic schwannomas.

**REMEMBER:** *You can push it in and it pops back — neurofibroma, anetoderma, dermatofibroma. Blisters do not buttonhole.*

### 65. Harlequin Ichthyosis

**PEARL:** The most severe autosomal recessive congenital ichthyosis — ABCA12 mutation — presenting at birth with thick armour-like truncal plates separated by deep fissures, severe bilateral ectropion and eclabium, and flattened ears and nose. Respiratory failure from restricted chest movement is the leading cause of death.

**UNDERSTAND:** ABCA12 is a lipid transporter that loads lamellar bodies in the stratum granulosum. Without it, the intercellular lipid “mortar” of the stratum corneum is never delivered — so the skin becomes a rigid, impermeable but non-functional plate. Defective lamellar bodies on electron microscopy are pathognomonic.

**MUST KNOW:**

- The neonatal threats are mechanical and physiological, not cosmetic: restricted chest expansion → respiratory failure; massive transepidermal water loss → dehydration and hypernatraemia; impaired thermoregulation; fissures → sepsis; and constricting bands → digital ischaemia.
- Management: humidified incubator, meticulous fluid and electrolyte monitoring, emollients, ophthalmic lubricants for the ectropion, sepsis surveillance — and early systemic retinoids (acitretin), which have substantially improved survival. Survivors transition to a severe congenital ichthyosiform erythroderma phenotype.
- The autosomal recessive congenital ichthyosis (ARCI) spectrum: harlequin (ABCA12) > lamellar ichthyosis (TGM1 — large dark plate-like scales, ectropion) > congenital ichthyosiform erythroderma (fine white scale on a red base) > bathing suit ichthyosis > self-improving collodion ichthyosis. All can begin as a collodion baby.
- Contrast the mild dominant forms: ichthyosis vulgaris (filaggrin, dominant, spares flexures, hyperlinear palms, atopy) and X-linked ichthyosis (steroid sulfatase, “dirty” dark scales, corneal opacities, cryptorchidism, failure of labour to progress).
- Genetic counselling and antenatal diagnosis are central — ABCA12 testing allows prenatal diagnosis in subsequent pregnancies.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Neonatal photograph of thick fissured plates with ectropion → harlequin ichthyosis.

**also know:** Main cause of death: respiratory failure (restricted chest wall, alveolar collapse); also sepsis, dehydration, hypernatremia. Treatment: intensive supportive care + early oral retinoids (acitretin/etretinate) markedly improve survival.

**REMEMBER:** *ABCA12, no lamellar bodies, armour plates — and the baby dies of not being able to expand the chest.*

### 66. Lisch Nodules and the Neurocutaneous Syndromes

**PEARL:** Lisch nodules — dome-shaped, pigmented melanocytic hamartomas of the iris, best seen on slit lamp, causing NO visual disturbance — belong to neurofibromatosis type 1, not to tuberous sclerosis. Facial angiofibromas (adenoma sebaceum) belong to tuberous sclerosis. Confusing the two is the intended trap.

**UNDERSTAND:** Both are autosomal dominant tumour-suppressor disorders with high spontaneous mutation rates, and both produce hamartomas in skin, eye and brain — which is why the lesions get mixed up. The way to keep them apart is by tissue of origin: NF1 lesions are of neural crest/melanocytic and Schwann cell origin (café-au-lait macules, neurofibromas, iris melanocytic hamartomas, optic glioma); tuberous sclerosis lesions are of fibrovascular and glial origin (angiofibromas, shagreen patch, ungual fibromas, cortical tubers, subependymal nodules, cardiac rhabdomyoma and renal angiomyolipoma).

**MUST KNOW:**

- **NF1 (NF1 gene, chromosome 17, neurofibromin — a RAS-GAP): diagnosis needs 2 or more of — *≥6 CAFÉ-AU-LAIT macules (>5 mm prepubertal, >15 mm postpubertal), ≥2 neurofibromas or 1 plexiform neurofibroma, axillary OR inguinal freckling (Crowe’s sign), optic glioma, ≥2 Lisch nodules, a distinctive bony lesion (sphenoid dysplasia, tibial pseudarthrosis) and an affected first-degree relative.* Complications: learning difficulties, scoliosis, hypertension (renal artery stenosis, phaeochromocytoma), and malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumours.
- NF2 (chromosome 22, merlin): *bilateral vestibular schwannomas*, meningiomas, ependymomas, juvenile posterior subcapsular cataract — and few skin lesions.
- Tuberous sclerosis (TSC1 hamartin/TSC2 tuberin, driving mTOR): *ASH-leaf (hypomelanotic) macules seen with a Wood’s lamp — the earliest sign*, facial angiofibromas, shagreen patch, periungual (Koenen) fibromas, cortical tubers and subependymal giant cell astrocytoma, infantile spasms, intellectual disability and autism, cardiac rhabdomyoma (often antenatal, regresses), renal angiomyolipoma and lymphangioleiomyomatosis; retinal astrocytic hamartomas in the eye. MTOR inhibitors (everolimus, sirolimus) treat SEGAs and angiomyolipomas, and vigabatrin is first-line for the spasms.
- Sturge–Weber (port-wine stain, leptomeningeal angioma, glaucoma, seizures) and von Hippel–Lindau (retinal and CNS haemangioblastomas, renal cell carcinoma, phaeochromocytoma, pancreatic cysts) complete the classic list of phakomatoses.
- Eye lesion by syndrome — a reliable one-line discriminator: Lisch nodule → NF1; retinal astrocytic hamartoma → tuberous sclerosis; retinal haemangioblastoma → von Hippel–Lindau; glaucoma/choroidal haemangioma → Sturge–Weber; posterior subcapsular cataract → NF2.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “False about Lisch nodules” → that they are associated with facial angiofibroma (that is tuberous sclerosis).

**REMEMBER:** *Lisch is NF1’s harmless iris freckle; angiofibroma belongs to the other one.*

### 67. Ataxia–Telangiectasia (Louis–Bar Syndrome)

**PEARL:** In ataxia–telangiectasia the first clinical manifestation is progressive cerebellar ataxia, around age 1 — the telangiectasia appears later (age 3–6), starting on the conjunctiva.

**UNDERSTAND:** The ATM gene senses double-strand DNA breaks and halts the cell cycle for repair. Purkinje cells are post-mitotic and metabolically intense, so unrepaired damage kills them earliest — neurological signs precede everything, including the vascular sign the disease is named after.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Autosomal recessive; ATM on chromosome 11q; features chromosomal instability and extreme radiosensitivity — avoid diagnostic X-rays/CT.
- Immunodeficiency: low IgA and IgG2, sinopulmonary infections, thymic hypoplasia; markedly raised α-fetoprotein is a useful screening test.
- High risk of lymphoma and leukaemia; heterozygous carriers have increased breast cancer risk.
- Other cutaneous features: premature greying, café-au-lait macules, poikiloderma; plus hypogonadism and insulin resistance.
- Other DNA repair disorders: xeroderma pigmentosum (nucleotide excision repair, UV sensitivity, skin cancers), Bloom syndrome (sister chromatid exchange), Fanconi anaemia (cross-link repair), HNPCC (mismatch repair).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The stem shows conjunctival telangiectasia and asks for the first manifestation — deliberately baiting you into answering with the visible sign. The answer is the cerebellar degeneration that came first.

**REMEMBER:** *Ataxia comes before the telangiectasia, despite the name’s order.*

### 71. Ichthyosis Vulgaris

**PEARL:** The commonest ichthyosis — autosomal dominant, filaggrin (FLG) mutation. Not present at birth; appears in the first months of life.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Filaggrin aggregates keratin and generates natural moisturizing factor in the stratum corneum.
- Fine light-grey scales on extensor surfaces + trunk, with flexural sparing; hyperlinear palms and soles; keratosis pilaris.
- Strong association with atopic dermatitis, asthma, allergic rhinitis.
- Histology: reduced/absent granular layer; immunohistochemistry shows reduced filaggrin.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Flexural sparing = ichthyosis vulgaris; flexural involvement = X-linked ichthyosis (steroid sulfatase deficiency, dark scales, neck involvement, corneal opacities, maternal failure of labour).

## Connective Tissue and Autoimmune Disorders

### 74. Scleredema vs Scleroderma

**PEARL:** Scleredema — the dermis is 3–4× thicker than normal with erythema and a peau d’orange appearance, typically over the upper back, neck and shoulders, with the hands and feet characteristically spared. It has NO sclerodactyly, NO Raynaud phenomenon, NO nailfold capillary changes and NO autoantibodies — that is the whole distinction from systemic sclerosis.

**UNDERSTAND:** In scleredema the fibroblasts overproduce type I collagen and mucin without proliferating, and in the diabetic form the collagen is irreversibly glycosylated and resistant to collagenase — so it accumulates. There is no vasculopathy, which is precisely why Raynaud’s and nailfold changes are absent.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Three types of scleredema (of Buschke): post-streptococcal (usually resolves), diabetic (scleredema diabeticorum — the commonest, persistent, in long-standing poorly controlled type 2 diabetes), and one associated with monoclonal gammopathy/myeloma.
- Systemic sclerosis cutaneous features: Raynaud’s (almost universal and usually the first symptom), sclerodactyly, salt-and-pepper pigmentation (depigmentation with perifollicular pigment retention, on upper back, chest and scalp), digital pitted scars and ulcers, microstomia, matted telangiectasia and calcinosis.
- Antibodies localise the disease: anti-centromere → limited cutaneous (crest) with pulmonary hypertension; anti-Scl-70 (topoisomerase I) → diffuse cutaneous with interstitial lung disease; anti-RNA polymerase III → diffuse disease with scleroderma renal crisis (treat with ACE inhibitors — never steroids, which precipitate it).
- Nailfold capillaroscopy showing dilated and dropped-out capillaries distinguishes secondary from primary Raynaud’s.
- Other scleroderma mimics: scleromyxoedema (with paraproteinaemia), nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (gadolinium in renal failure), eosinophilic fasciitis (“groove sign”, spares the fingers), morphoea (localised, no Raynaud’s).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “False regarding scleredema” → “associated with sclerodactyly or Raynaud phenomenon”. Separately, an image of mottled depigmentation with retained follicular pigment → salt and pepper → systemic sclerosis.

**REMEMBER:** *Scleredema: thick back, spared hands, no Raynaud’s. Scleroderma: tight hands, Raynaud’s first, salt-and-pepper skin.*

### 75. Morphoea and the “Box Sign”

**PEARL:** Morphoea (localised scleroderma) — well-demarcated indurated sclerotic plaques. The “box sign” is the squared-off, boxy appearance of the biopsy caused by sclerosis of collagen throughout the full-thickness reticular dermis extending into the subcutaneous septa.

**UNDERSTAND:** In normal skin, collagen bundles become progressively coarser with depth and the dermis blends into fat. In morphoea the entire reticular dermis is uniformly sclerosed and the fat septa are thickened, so the specimen keeps a rectangular shape instead of tapering — hence “boxed dermis”.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Morphoea has NO Raynaud’s phenomenon, NO sclerodactyly, NO nailfold capillary changes and NO internal organ involvement — this is the crucial separation from systemic sclerosis, and it determines prognosis and treatment entirely.
- Clinically: an ivory/white sclerotic centre with a violaceous “LILAC ring” at the active advancing edge.
- Types: plaque (commonest), linear (children — “EN coup DE sabre” on the forehead, which may associate with Parry–Romberg hemifacial atrophy and neurological/ocular disease), generalised, and pansclerotic.
- Linear morphoea crossing a joint in a child can cause contractures and limb length discrepancy — so it needs active treatment (methotrexate ± systemic steroids, and phototherapy), not just observation.
- Lichen sclerosus is the close relative — porcelain-white atrophic plaques, classically anogenital (“figure-of-8”), intensely itchy, causing phimosis in men and architectural loss in women, with a small risk of squamous cell carcinoma requiring long-term follow-up; treated with potent topical steroids (clobetasol).
- Eosinophilic fasciitis — sudden onset after exertion, “groove sign”, peripheral eosinophilia, spares the fingers and has no Raynaud’s.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Box sign is associated with” → morphoea.

**REMEMBER:** *A squared-off block of sclerosed dermis — morphoea, and no Raynaud’s means it is not systemic.*

### 76. Dermatomyositis

**PEARL:** Heliotrope rash (violaceous periorbital oedema), Gottron papules (flat-topped papules over the knuckles/PIP and dip joints), SHAWL and V signs (photodistributed macular erythema), nailfold changes with dilated capillary loops and ragged cuticles, plus proximal muscle weakness with a raised CK. Muscle tenderness IS uncommon.

**UNDERSTAND:** The weakness is proximal and painless — patients describe difficulty climbing stairs, rising from squatting, and combing the hair, not pain. Prominent muscle tenderness should make you reconsider the diagnosis (think polymyalgia rheumatica, myositis from infection, or statin myopathy).

**MUST KNOW:**

- 20–40% of adults with dermatomyositis have an underlying malignancy — ovarian, lung, gastric, colorectal, and nasopharyngeal carcinoma (especially in Asian populations). Age-appropriate malignancy screening is mandatory at diagnosis and for the following 3–5 years. Risk is highest with anti-TIF1-γ antibodies.
- Dysphagia indicates a worse prognosis (pharyngeal/oesophageal muscle involvement → aspiration). Interstitial lung disease — especially with anti-MDA5 (often with amyopathic disease and skin ulcers) or anti-Jo-1 (antisynthetase syndrome: ILD, arthritis, mechanic’s hands, Raynaud’s, fever) — is the other major cause of death.
- Gottron papules are over the knuckles; in SLE the rash spares the knuckles and involves the phalanges between them — a genuinely high-yield discriminator.
- Investigations: CK, aldolase, LDH, AST; EMG (short-duration polyphasic units, fibrillations); MRI to guide biopsy; muscle biopsy showing perifascicular atrophy and perivascular CD4/B-cell infiltrate (dermatomyositis is humoral and perivascular; polymyositis is CD8-mediated and endomysial).
- Juvenile dermatomyositis differs — calcinosis cutis is common and malignancy is not associated.
- Treatment: high-dose corticosteroids plus a steroid-sparing agent (methotrexate, azathioprine), IVIG for refractory disease, hydroxychloroquine and rigorous photoprotection for the skin.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Which is not true?” → “muscle tenderness is common”. Also image-based: V sign / heliotrope → dermatomyositis.

**ALSO KNOW:** Dermatomyositis is a complement-mediated microangiopathy: antibodies and C5b-9 membrane attack complex deposit on endomysial capillaries, causing ischaemic perifascicular atrophy. The muscle fibres die from ischaemia rather than from an acute inflammatory invasion of the fibre itself, so the process is insidious and painless. That is the mirror image of polymyositis (direct CD8 T-cell invasion of non-necrotic fibres) and of viral or drug myositis, which are genuinely painful. Antibodies: anti-Mi-2 → classic skin disease, good prognosis, steroid-responsive; anti-Jo-1 (anti-synthetase) → mechanic’s hands, arthritis, Raynaud, interstitial lung disease, fever; anti-MDA5 → clinically amyopathic DM with rapidly progressive ILD (poor prognosis); anti-TIF1-γ and anti-NXP2 → strongest malignancy association.

**REMEMBER:** *Weak but not sore, purple eyelids, papules ON the knuckles — and in an adult, go looking for a cancer.*

### 77. Cutaneous Manifestations of SLE

**PEARL:** The commonest cutaneous manifestation of SLE is photosensitivity — not the malar rash.

**UNDERSTAND:** UV light induces keratinocyte apoptosis, exposing nuclear antigens (Ro/SSA, dsDNA) at the cell surface where circulating autoantibodies can bind. Since every patient with circulating autoantibodies is vulnerable to this, photosensitivity is far more prevalent than the specific butterfly eruption.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Order of frequency: photosensitivity > Raynaud’s phenomenon > malar rash > chronic urticaria (> 36 h) > non-scarring alopecia > oral ulceration.
- The malar rash spares the nasolabial folds — the feature that separates it from dermatomyositis (which involves them) and from rosacea.
- Oral/nasal ulcers in SLE are typically painless and on the hard palate.
- Salmon-pink evanescent rash = adult-onset Still’s disease (with quotidian spiking fever, arthralgia, very high ferritin) — not lupus.
- Discoid lupus scars and causes scarring alopecia; SLE’s diffuse alopecia is non-scarring.
- Antibodies: ANA (sensitive), anti-dsDNA (specific, tracks nephritis), anti-Sm (most specific), anti-Ro (neonatal lupus, photosensitivity, congenital heart block).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Two questions from one topic — “commonest cutaneous manifestation” (photosensitivity) and “which is not a manifestation” (salmon rash → Still’s disease).

**REMEMBER:** *Everyone with lupus burns; only some get the butterfly.*

## Urticaria, Angioedema and Drug Reactions

### 81. Erythema Multiforme

**PEARL:** The commonest trigger is infection — above all herpes simplex virus (then *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*, especially in children). Drugs are a much less common cause of true EM.

**UNDERSTAND:** EM is a cell-mediated cytotoxic reaction against keratinocytes displaying viral antigen — which is why it is recurrent (following each HSV recurrence) and why prophylactic aciclovir prevents recurrent EM while stopping drugs does not.

**MUST KNOW:**

- The target (iris) lesion is diagnostic: <3 cm, with three zones — a dusky/purpuric or bullous centre, a paler oedematous ring, and an outer ring of erythema. Distribution is acral — palms, soles, extensor limbs — spreading centripetally.
- The crucial distinction from SJS/ten: SJS/ten is drug-induced (sulfonamides, anticonvulsants, allopurinol, NSAIDs, nevirapine), has atypical flat targets, prominent mucosal involvement, systemic illness and skin detachment, and is classified by body surface area — SJS <10%, overlap 10–30%, ten >30%. Modern teaching treats EM and SJS/ten as separate diseases, not a spectrum.
- Ten management: STOP the drug immediately (the single most important determinant of survival), transfer to a burns/ICU setting, fluid and thermal support, ophthalmology review. Prognosis by SCORTEN. Nikolsky positive; histology shows full-thickness epidermal necrosis.
- EM major = EM with significant mucosal involvement; still HSV-driven.
- Mycoplasma-induced rash and mucositis (MIRM) — prominent mucositis with sparse skin lesions in children — is now considered a separate entity.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Image of target lesions → “most common triggering factor” → infection (not drugs — the intuitive wrong answer).

**ALSO KNOW:** Recurrent EM → suppressive acyclovir. SJS/ten is drug-induced and pathogenetically distinct — dusky atypical targets, truncal onset, extensive mucosal involvement.

**REMEMBER:** *EM is a virus (herpes) with true 3-zone targets; SJS/ten is a drug with flat targets and dying mucosa.*

### 82. Fixed Drug Eruption

**PEARL:** FDE = sharply demarcated, dull red to violaceous/brown round plaques that recur AT exactly the same site on re-exposure, leaving post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. In the amoebiasis context the drug is metronidazole; overall, NSAIDs/paracetamol, co-trimoxazole and tetracyclines are commonest.

**UNDERSTAND:** Memory CD8⁺ T cells persist in the epidermis at the site of the previous reaction. On re-exposure to the drug they are reactivated in situ — which is exactly why the lesion recurs at the identical spot, faster and more intensely each time, and why the pigmentation accumulates.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Site preferences by drug are examinable: NSAIDs → lips and genitals · co-trimoxazole/tetracycline → genitals (a classic cause of a recurrent penile ulcer misdiagnosed as herpes) · metamizole → trunk and limbs · carbocysteine → face.
- New lesions appear at additional sites with each re-exposure, but the original sites always reactivate.
- Generalised bullous FDE is the dangerous variant and can be mistaken for SJS/ten — the discriminators are well-defined lesions on a background of normal skin, minimal mucosal involvement, less systemic upset, and a history of identical prior episodes.
- Diagnosis is clinical; confirm if needed with oral provocation (gold standard, but risky) or topical provocation on a previously affected site. Histology: interface dermatitis with pigment incontinence and melanophages in the dermis (explaining the lasting pigmentation).
- Management: identify and permanently avoid the drug — give the patient a written list of the culprit and its cross-reactants. Topical steroids for the acute lesion.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Skin manifestation of the drug given for amoebic dysentery” → fixed drug eruption (metronidazole).

**REMEMBER:** *Same drug, same spot, every time — memory T cells are waiting there.*

### 83. Bleomycin — Flagellate Dermatitis

**PEARL:** Flagellate (whiplash-like) linear bands of pruritic erythema on the trunk and limbs, healing with persistent linear hyperpigmentation — virtually pathognomonic of bleomycin.

**UNDERSTAND:** The linear pattern follows scratching — minor trauma concentrates the drug and provokes inflammation along the scratch line (a Koebner-like phenomenon), which is why controlling the itch with antihistamines limits the pigmentation.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Onset ranges from hours to 6 months after administration and is dose-independent; it resolves on stopping the drug and needs no specific treatment.
- The other bleomycin toxicity that matters far more is pulmonary fibrosis — dose-related (cumulative dose >400 units), worsened by high FiO2 (relevant to any subsequent anaesthetic — keep FiO2 low), radiotherapy and renal impairment. Monitor with DLCO.
- Bleomycin causes minimal myelosuppression — which is why it is included in ABVD for Hodgkin lymphoma and in BEP for testicular tumours.
- Other causes of flagellate erythema (worth knowing to avoid over-calling it): shiitake mushroom dermatitis, adult-onset Still’s disease, and dermatomyositis.
- Chemotherapy skin signs worth pairing: hand-foot syndrome (capecitabine, doxorubicin, 5-FU), acneiform rash (egfr inhibitors — and it predicts response), photosensitivity (vandetanib, 5-FU), nail changes with taxanes, and acral erythema.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Image of linear whip-like pigmented streaks → name the drug → bleomycin.

**ALSO KNOW:** Appears in scratched/pressure areas, dose-independent, may occur weeks after therapy. Other bleomycin skin toxicity: Raynaud’s, digital gangrene, and its dose-limiting pulmonary fibrosis.

**REMEMBER:** *Whip marks that were never whipped — bleomycin, and remember its lungs.*

### 84. Dermographism

**PEARL:** Simple dermographism — the triple response (erythema → oedema/weal → surrounding flare). White dermographism — capillary vasoconstriction on light stroking, pronounced in atopic eczema. Black dermographism — skin discoloration after pressure from a metallic object. Cholinergic dermographism — an erythematous line studded with punctate weals.

**UNDERSTAND:** Simple dermographism is Lewis’s triple response — histamine causes local capillary dilatation (erythema), increased permeability (weal), and an axon reflex producing arteriolar dilatation beyond the stroke (flare). The flare is neurogenic, which is why it is absent when the sensory nerve is degenerated.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Dermographism is the commonest physical/inducible urticaria, affecting up to 5% of the population; treat symptomatic cases with non-sedating H1 antihistamines, up-dosed to 4× standard if needed.
- White dermographism in atopic dermatitis reflects the abnormal vascular reactivity of atopic skin — a classic bedside sign of atopy.
- Black dermographism is not a true urticaria at all — it is a chemical deposit of metal (from jewellery, coins) reacting with cosmetics/skin, and it rubs off.
- Other inducible urticarias: cold urticaria (ice-cube test — beware systemic reaction on swimming), cholinergic urticaria (tiny 1–3 mm weals with large flares, on heat/exercise/emotion), solar, aquagenic, delayed pressure, and vibratory urticaria.
- Urticaria in general: weals lasting <24 hours with no residual mark. If individual lesions last >24 hours, are painful/burning, or leave bruising → suspect urticarial vasculitis and biopsy.
- Chronic spontaneous urticaria is rarely allergic — most is autoimmune (anti-FcεRI); escalate to omalizumab if antihistamines fail.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Pure matching question — anchor on simple = triple response and black = metallic.

**REMEMBER:** *Simple is the triple response, white belongs to the atopic, black is just metal, cholinergic is a line of tiny weals.*

### 85. Hereditary Angioedema

**PEARL:** HAE is autosomal dominant, caused by mutations of the SERPING1 gene encoding C1 inhibitor (types I and II). Type III is associated with a gain-of-function F12 mutation. It is aggravated by oestrogens (OCPs, HRT) — not by NSAIDs.

**UNDERSTAND:** HAE is bradykinin-mediated, not histamine-mediated. C1-INH normally restrains the contact/kallikrein system; without it, bradykinin accumulates and causes vascular permeability. This single fact explains everything clinically important: the swelling is NON-pruritic, has NO urticaria, and does not respond to antihistamines, steroids or adrenaline.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Failure to respond to adrenaline/antihistamines in “anaphylaxis” should raise HAE — and the airway risk is real: laryngeal oedema is the leading cause of death. Recurrent abdominal pain from bowel wall oedema frequently leads to unnecessary laparotomy.
- Investigations: low C4 is the best screening test (low even between attacks); then C1-INH level (low in type I, ~85%) and C1-INH function (low with normal or high level in type II). C1q is normal in hereditary and low in acquired angioedema (associated with lymphoproliferative disease/paraproteins) — the key discriminator.
- ACE inhibitors cause a bradykinin-mediated angioedema by the same mechanism and are absolutely contraindicated in HAE; oestrogens increase attacks (they upregulate factor XII).
- Acute treatment: C1-INH concentrate, icatibant (bradykinin B2 receptor antagonist) or ecallantide — plus FFP if nothing else is available. Prophylaxis: attenuated androgens (danazol), tranexamic acid, or lanadelumab.
- Triggers: trauma — especially dental procedures and intubation — infection and stress. Give short-term prophylaxis before any surgery or dental work.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “All statements are false except” → associated with mutations of SERPING1.

**also know:** Type III (normal C1-INH): gain-of-function F12 mutation in ~20%; oestrogen-dependent, female-predominant. *HAE = bradykinin, low C4, oestrogen worsens, antihistamines useless.*

**REMEMBER:** *Bradykinin, not histamine — no itch, no weals, no response to adrenaline, and never an ACE inhibitor.*

### 86. Darier’s Sign and Urticaria Pigmentosa

**PEARL:** Darier’s sign = urtication and erythema of a lesion after rubbing or stroking it — characteristic of urticaria pigmentosa (cutaneous mastocytosis). Do not confuse it with Darier’s disease (keratosis follicularis), which is a different condition entirely.

**UNDERSTAND:** The lesion is packed with mast cells; mechanical stimulation degranulates them, releasing histamine locally — producing a weal confined precisely to the lesion. It is a bedside provocation test that requires no equipment.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Urticaria pigmentosa is the commonest form of cutaneous mastocytosis — reddish-brown macules, papules and plaques, symmetrical, sparing palms and soles; in children it usually resolves by puberty, while adult-onset disease more often reflects systemic mastocytosis (*kit* D816V mutation).
- Systemic features to ask about: flushing, anaphylaxis, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, bone pain; serum tryptase is the screening test (persistently raised suggests systemic disease).
- Avoid mast cell DEGRANULATORS: opioids (morphine, codeine), NSAIDs/aspirin, vancomycin, radiocontrast, alcohol, quinine, and physical triggers (heat, friction, cold). This matters most before anaesthesia — these patients can have catastrophic peri-operative anaphylaxis; premedicate with H1 and H2 blockers.
- Darier’s sign is not 100% specific — occasionally seen in juvenile xanthogranuloma and leukaemia cutis. “pseudo-Darier’s sign” (induration and piloerection, not urtication, on stroking) occurs in smooth muscle hamartoma.
- Darier’s disease (keratosis follicularis) — autosomal dominant ATP2A2: greasy keratotic papules in a seborrhoeic distribution, nail V-shaped nicking with red and white longitudinal bands, palmar pits; histology shows corps ronds and grains.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Darier’s sign is seen in” → urticaria pigmentosa (Darier’s disease is deliberately offered).

**REMEMBER:** *Rub it and it weals — mast cells. Darier’s sign and Darier’s disease are unrelated.*

## Skin Tumours, Naevi and Vascular Lesions

### 87. Syringoma

**PEARL:** Multiple small, skin-coloured to yellowish, flat-topped papules, classically on the lower eyelids, also neck, chest, axillae and vulva/thighs — a benign tumour of the eccrine sweat duct. Appears around puberty, commoner in women.

**UNDERSTAND:** Naming skin adnexal tumours is simply a matter of which part of the pilosebaceous–sweat apparatus they arise from — and the exam nearly always asks for the cell of origin. Syringoma = duct (not gland, not follicle, not sebaceous).

**MUST KNOW:**

- Histology is highly characteristic: small ducts with a “comma” or tadpole-shaped tail of epithelium in a sclerotic stroma — the “paisley tie” pattern.
- Association: eruptive syringomas are notably common in down syndrome, and clear-cell syringomas are associated with diabetes mellitus.
- Treatment is cosmetic only — electrodesiccation, CO2 laser or excision; recurrence is common.
- The adnexal tumour map — worth owning as a set: syringoma — eccrine duct · CYLINDROMA — sweat gland (“turban tumour” of the scalp; multiple in Brooke–Spiegler syndrome) · HIDRADENOMA/spiradenoma — sweat gland · trichoepithelioma and pilomatricoma — hair follicle (pilomatricoma: “tent sign”, calcified, ghost/shadow cells, *CTNNB1*) · sebaceous adenoma — sebaceous gland (→ think Muir–torre syndrome, a Lynch syndrome variant, and screen for internal malignancy) · naevus SEBACEUS of Jadassohn — a hamartoma on the scalp that becomes verrucous at puberty and may develop basal cell carcinoma/trichoblastoma.
- Differential for periorbital papules: syringoma, xanthelasma (yellow, plaque-like, check lipids), milia, and trichoepithelioma.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Site of origin of the tumour shown” → sweat ducts.

**ALSO KNOW:** Increased frequency in Down syndrome.

**REMEMBER:** *Flat papules under the eyes with tadpole-tailed ducts on histology — syringoma, from the sweat duct.*

### 88. Bowen’s Disease

**PEARL:** Squamous cell carcinoma IN situ — a persistent, slowly enlarging, flat red scaly or crusted plaque with a well-defined irregular border, showing full-thickness epidermal dysplasia on histology. Its potential for invasive malignancy is low (~3–5%), and it may even undergo partial spontaneous resolution.

**UNDERSTAND:** “In situ” means the atypical keratinocytes occupy the full thickness of the epidermis but have not breached the basement membrane — so there is no access to lymphatics or vessels and therefore no metastatic potential while it remains in situ. That is precisely why the low-risk statement is true and the exam’s “high potential” option is false.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Risk factors: chronic sun exposure, arsenic exposure (a genuinely important Indian/Bangladeshi association — think of it with palmar keratoses and multiple lesions on covered skin), HPV, immunosuppression, chronic radiation.
- Clinical mimics: psoriasis, discoid eczema, superficial BCC and tinea — a “psoriatic plaque” that is solitary, on sun-exposed skin in an older patient and fails to respond to steroids should be biopsied. That single clinical rule is the practical value of this topic.
- Treatment: 5-fluorouracil or imiquimod cream, cryotherapy, curettage, photodynamic therapy, or excision (excision for lesions that are large, on the digit or persistently recurrent).
- ERYTHROPLASIA of QUEYRAT is Bowen’s disease of the glans penis — a velvety red plaque, with a higher rate of progression to invasive SCC than cutaneous Bowen’s.
- Contrast the other precancer, actinic keratosis: small, gritty, “better felt than seen” rough scaly macules on sun-damaged skin, with partial-thickness dysplasia; a small proportion progress to SCC. Bowen’s is full-thickness and further along the same road.
- Invasive SCC red flags: rapid growth, induration, ulceration, pain, and arising in a scar or chronic ulcer (Marjolin’s).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Not true regarding Bowen’s disease” → “high potential for invasive malignancy”.

**REMEMBER:** *Full-thickness dysplasia but the basement membrane is intact — so it looks alarming and behaves mildly. Biopsy the steroid-resistant “psoriasis”.*

### 89. Vascular Birthmarks — Salmon Patch, Port-Wine Stain and Haemangioma

**PEARL:** The salmon patch (naevus simplex, “stork bite” on the nape of the neck, “ANGEL’S KISS” on the glabella and eyelids) is a very common capillary vascular malformation of newborns — flat, pink, blanching, deepening when the baby cries — and needs NO treatment: facial lesions fade within 1–2 years and the nuchal one often persists harmlessly under the hairline. It is a capillary malformation, not a true haemangioma (which is a proliferative tumour).

**UNDERSTAND:** The distinction that governs management is tumour versus malformation. An infantile haemangioma is a benign endothelial tumour — it is often absent at birth, proliferates for 6–12 months and then involutes over years, so most need only observation. A vascular malformation (salmon patch, port-wine stain, lymphatic and venous malformations) is present at birth, grows only in proportion to the child, and never involutes — so a port-wine stain will not disappear and is treated, if at all, with the pulsed-dye laser.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Port-WINE stain (naevus flammeus) in the ophthalmic (V1) trigeminal distribution raises the question of *Sturge–Weber syndrome* (GNAQ mutation): leptomeningeal angioma with seizures, hemiparesis, intellectual disability, “TRAM-track” gyriform intracranial calcification, and glaucoma (requiring lifelong ophthalmic review). A limb port-wine stain with limb hypertrophy and varicosities suggests Klippel–TRÉNAUNAY syndrome.
- Infantile haemangiomas needing treatment — periocular (amblyopia), airway (“beard” distribution with stridor), nasal tip, ulcerated, large facial (PHACE syndrome) or lumbosacral (spinal dysraphism) — are treated with *oral propranolol*, which has replaced corticosteroids. Kasabach–Merritt phenomenon (consumptive coagulopathy with thrombocytopenia) occurs with kaposiform haemangioendothelioma, not ordinary haemangiomas.
- Other newborn skin findings that need only reassurance: erythema toxicum neonatorum (eosinophil-rich pustules on a blotchy erythematous base, days 2–3), milia, Mongolian spots (dermal melanocytosis over the sacrum — *document them to avoid a mistaken suspicion of non-accidental injury*), transient neonatal pustular melanosis, and miliaria.
- *A midline lumbosacral lesion — a large haemangioma, tuft of hair, dimple above the natal cleft, lipoma or skin tag — may mark occult spinal dysraphism* and warrants spinal ultrasound (or MRI after 3 months).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “True about salmon patch” → a common vascular birthmark of the nape in children that needs NO surgical excision.

**REMEMBER:** *Tumours involute, malformations do not — and the stork bite needs nothing but reassurance.*

### 90. Basal Cell Carcinoma: The Rodent Ulcer

**PEARL:** A slowly enlarging ulcer with a pearly, rolled, “rolled-out” beaded edge, central depression and telangiectasia over the surface, on the face above a line from the angle of the mouth to the ear lobe, is a basal cell carcinoma (rodent ulcer).

**UNDERSTAND:** BCC arises from the basal layer of pilosebaceous skin, so it occurs only where hair follicles exist (never on mucosa or palms/soles) and is locally invasive but essentially never metastasises — its cells depend on the surrounding dermal stroma and cannot survive transplantation to a distant site. That biology explains the whole management: wide local excision cures it, and lymph node dissection is unnecessary.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Strongest risk factor: cumulative Ultraviolet (UVB) exposure; also arsenic, coal tar, ionising radiation, immunosuppression, fair skin, and Gorlin (naevoid BCC) syndrome (PTCH1 mutation, multiple BCCs, odontogenic keratocysts, palmar pits, calcified falx). The PTCH/Sonic hedgehog pathway is the key molecular driver.
- Types: nodular/nodulocystic (90%), pigmented (mimics melanoma), superficial (trunk), morphoeic/sclerosing (ill-defined, highest recurrence), and cicatrising.
- Treatment: surgical excision with 3–5 mm margins; MOHS micrographic surgery for the face, recurrent, morphoeic or large lesions (best cure rate with maximal tissue conservation); alternatives — imiquimod or 5-FU for superficial lesions, cryotherapy, radiotherapy; vismodegib (hedgehog inhibitor) for locally advanced or metastatic disease.
- Contrast squamous cell carcinoma: everted edges, faster growing, arises in actinic keratosis, Bowen disease, chronic ulcers/scars (Marjolin), burns and sinuses, and does metastasise to regional lymph nodes. It occurs below the BCC line and on mucosa.
- Ulcer edges (the classic set): rolled/beaded = BCC; everted = SCC; punched-out = syphilitic gumma or ischaemic; undermined = tuberculous; sloping = venous/healing.
- Melanoma — ABCDE (Asymmetry, Border, Colour, Diameter >6 mm, Evolution); Breslow thickness is the single most important prognostic factor; excision margins depend on it; sentinel node biopsy for ≥0.8 mm.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** An image of a facial ulcer — the edge is the whole answer. Pearly and rolled = BCC; everted and fleshy = SCC.

**REMEMBER:** *Rolled pearly edge, gnaws locally, never spreads — the rodent ulcer.*

### 91. Mycosis Fungoides — Extracorporeal Photopheresis

**PEARL:** Extracorporeal photopheresis is a systemic therapy for mycosis fungoides / Sézary syndrome (cutaneous T-cell lymphoma) — leukapheresis, psoralen + UVA activation ex vivo, then reinfusion of the buffy coat.

**UNDERSTAND:** It is not simple cytotoxicity. Psoralen cross-links DNA in the separated leucocytes so they undergo apoptosis; when reinfused, these apoptotic malignant cells are taken up by antigen-presenting cells, generating a specific anti-tumour immune response. The treatment is effectively an autologous vaccine.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Mycosis fungoides evolves patch → plaque → tumour; Pautrier microabscesses and epidermotropism of atypical CD4+ cerebriform lymphocytes are the histology. Sézary syndrome is the leukaemic, erythrodermic variant.
- Early stage → skin-directed therapy: topical steroids, topical mechlorethamine/carmustine, PUVA/NB-UVB, and total skin electron beam — MF is highly radiosensitive.
- Advanced stage → systemic: photopheresis, pegylated interferon-α, bexarotene, brentuximab vedotin (anti-CD30), mogamulizumab, methotrexate.
- Photopheresis is also used for chronic graft-versus-host disease and cardiac transplant rejection.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Direct matching — photopheresis is asked about only in the context of CTCL. Melanoma and SCC are noise.

**REMEMBER:** *Take the cells out, irradiate them, put them back as a vaccine.*

## Acne, Sebaceous and Sweat Gland Disorders

### 94. Isotretinoin in Acne

**PEARL:** Systemic isotretinoin is the single most effective treatment for acne — the only agent that targets ALL four pathogenic factors: it shrinks sebaceous glands (↓sebum), normalises follicular keratinisation, reduces *Cutibacterium acnes*, and is anti-inflammatory.

**understand:** Every other treatment attacks one or two factors: antibiotics hit bacteria and inflammation; topical retinoids hit keratinisation; hormonal therapy hits sebum. Only isotretinoin does all four — which is why it can produce prolonged remission after a finite course, unlike everything else.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Indications: nodulocystic/severe acne, scarring acne, acne failing conventional therapy, and acne with significant psychological impact. Standard cumulative dose target 120–150 mg/kg.
- Pregnancy IS the absolute contraindication — profoundly teratogenic (craniofacial, cardiac, CNS, thymic defects). Requires two negative pregnancy tests, two forms of contraception, monthly testing, and contraception for 1 month after stopping (contrast acitretin — 3 years).
- Side effects: cheilitis (near-universal — its absence suggests non-compliance), xerosis, dry eyes (avoid contact lenses), epistaxis, myalgia, raised triglycerides and transaminases (monitor lipids and LFTs), photosensitivity, and benign intracranial hypertension — so never combine with tetracyclines.
- Do not do elective dermabrasion, laser resurfacing or waxing for 6 months after isotretinoin (risk of atypical scarring).
- Mood change/depression is debated but must be discussed and monitored.
- Acne pathogenesis in order: the comedo is the primary lesion — a keratin plug of the pilosebaceous duct; closed = whitehead, open = blackhead (black from oxidised melanin, not dirt).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Nodulocystic acne on face and upper back → “most effective treatment” → systemic isotretinoin.

**REMEMBER:** *Isotretinoin is the only drug that fixes all four causes — and the only one that can cure. Never in pregnancy.*

### 95. Treatment-Resistant Acne in a Woman

**PEARL:** Acne that fails isotretinoin and antibiotics in a female → evaluate for hyperandrogenism (pcos, CAH, androgen-secreting tumour).

**UNDERSTAND:** Isotretinoin acts on the sebaceous gland and P. Acnes; it cannot outrun a continuing hormonal drive. If androgens keep stimulating sebum production, the disease keeps relapsing. Treating the cause — not escalating the same class of drug — is the logical next step.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Red flags for hyperandrogenism: late-onset or persistent adult acne, hirsutism, androgenic alopecia, menstrual irregularity, acanthosis nigricans, virilisation.
- Workup: total and free testosterone, DHEAS, 17-hydroxyprogesterone (for late-onset CAH), LH:FSH, prolactin, pelvic ultrasound. Very high DHEAS or rapid virilisation → adrenal tumour.
- Anti-androgen therapy: combined oral contraceptive (with cyproterone acetate or drospirenone), spironolactone, and metformin for insulin resistance.
- Four pathogenic steps in acne: follicular hyperkeratinisation, excess sebum, Cutibacterium (Propionibacterium) acnes proliferation, inflammation — each drug class targets one.
- Isotretinoin requires two negative pregnancy tests, contraception for 1 month before/during/after, and monitoring of lipids and LFTs.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Next best investigation” — a *step* question. Antibiotic resistance is a real consideration but is subordinate to finding a hormonal cause; the exam wants the systemic diagnosis.

**REMEMBER:** *When the sebum keeps coming back, look for the androgen driving it.*

### 96. Miliaria — Blocked Eccrine Ducts

**PEARL:** Miliaria is a disorder of eccrine sweat ducts. The level of the block defines the type: crystallina — stratum corneum; rubra — mid-epidermis (Malpighian); profunda — dermoepidermal junction.

**UNDERSTAND:** The deeper the obstruction, the more inflammation the leaked sweat provokes. A block right at the surface lets sweat pool under a paper-thin roof — clear, asymptomatic, non-inflamed vesicles like dewdrops. A block in the mid-epidermis spills sweat among living keratinocytes → erythema and prickling (prickly heat).

**MUST KNOW:**

- Miliaria crystallina — clear 1–2 mm vesicles with NO erythema, common in neonates (immature ducts) and after fever; asymptomatic, resolves spontaneously.
- Miliaria rubra — itchy/prickly erythematous papulovesicles; miliaria pustulosa when secondarily infected.
- Miliaria profunda — flesh-coloured deep papules; risk of anhidrosis and heat exhaustion if extensive.
- Pathogenesis: Staphylococcus epidermidis extracellular polysaccharide (“slime”) plugs the duct; parakeratotic plugs come later, during repair.
- Management: cooling, loose clothing, avoiding occlusion. Eccrine glands are the body’s thermoregulatory glands (cholinergic sympathetic supply); apocrine glands open into the follicle and are the ones involved in hidradenitis suppurativa, Fox–Fordyce disease and bromhidrosis.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Neonate + clear dew-drop vesicles + “disorder of which gland” → eccrine. Note the trap option “holocrine” — that is a *mode of secretion* (sebaceous), not a gland type in this list.

**REMEMBER:** *Deeper the plug, angrier the rash.*

## Skin in Systemic Disease and Miscellaneous

### 97. The Eschar — What Causes It

**PEARL:** A black necrotic eschar should trigger a short differential: scrub typhus, cutaneous anthrax, spider bite (brown recluse), rickettsial spotted fevers, and mucormycosis. Kyasanur Forest Disease does not cause an eschar — it causes a petechial/haemorrhagic rash.

**UNDERSTAND:** An eschar marks the site of inoculation where the organism or toxin produced local vascular thrombosis and necrosis — so it appears in vector-bite and direct-inoculation diseases, not in systemic viral haemorrhagic fevers.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Scrub typhus (*Orientia tsutsugamushi*, trombiculid mite larva “chigger”): eschar + regional lymphadenopathy + maculopapular rash + fever, often with hepatosplenomegaly, ARDS, meningoencephalitis, AKI and thrombocytopenia. Look for the eschar in hidden areas — axilla, groin, under the breast, waistband — it is painless and easily missed. Diagnosis: IgM ELISA (Weil–Felix OX-K is old and insensitive). Treatment: doxycycline (azithromycin in pregnancy and children). A leading cause of acute undifferentiated febrile illness in India.
- Cutaneous anthrax — a painless ulcer with a black centre and marked surrounding oedema, in those handling animal hides/wool; “malignant pustule”. Treat with ciprofloxacin or doxycycline.
- Kyasanur forest disease — a Flavivirus of Karnataka, transmitted by *Haemaphysalis spinigera* ticks, with monkeys as amplifying hosts. Biphasic fever, conjunctival suffusion, severe prostration, haemorrhage and later neurological features — petechiae, no eschar. A vaccine exists for endemic districts.
- Painless eschar = anthrax or scrub typhus; painful necrotic lesion with a red-white-blue sign = brown recluse spider.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Image of an eschar in a forest worker → “which is not a differential?” → KFD.

**REMEMBER:** *Eschar = inoculation site. Scrub typhus, anthrax, spider bite — never KFD.*

### 98. Phrynoderma

**PEARL:** “Toad skin” — groups of follicular keratotic papules with a central horny plug, classically on the elbows and knees (extensor surfaces), associated with vitamin A deficiency — the child with night blindness.

**UNDERSTAND:** Vitamin A maintains normal epithelial differentiation. Without it, follicular epithelium undergoes squamous metaplasia and keratinises excessively, plugging the follicle — the same process that causes conjunctival xerosis and Bitot’s spots in the eye.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Phrynoderma is not specific to vitamin A — it also occurs with deficiencies of B complex, riboflavin, vitamin C, vitamin E and essential fatty acids, and in general protein-energy malnutrition. Treatment is therefore a multivitamin plus dietary correction, not vitamin A alone.
- Distinguish from keratosis pilaris: an extremely common inherited disorder of keratinisation on the extensor upper arms and thighs, appearing at puberty, with perifollicular erythema, in an otherwise healthy person — and it is a marker of atopy and ichthyosis vulgaris, not malnutrition.
- Darier disease is the other differential — autosomal dominant (ATP2A2), greasy keratotic papules in a seborrhoeic distribution, with nail V-nicking and red-white longitudinal bands and palmar pits; histology shows acantholysis with corps ronds and grains.
- Vitamin A deficiency’s ocular sequence: night blindness → conjunctival xerosis → Bitot’s spots → corneal xerosis → keratomalacia. National prophylaxis: 100 000 IU at 9 months, then 200 000 IU 6-monthly up to 5 years.
- Look for other nutritional skin signs together: angular stomatitis and glossitis (B2, B12, iron), pellagra (niacin), flaky paint dermatosis (kwashiorkor), perifollicular haemorrhage and corkscrew hairs (vitamin C).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Night blindness + keratotic papules on elbows → phrynoderma.

**REMEMBER:** *Toad skin on the elbows with night blindness — but treat the whole diet, not just vitamin A.*

### 99. Cutis Marmorata

**PEARL:** Cutis marmorata is a physiological, reversible pink-blue mottled/marbled skin pattern in infants on exposure to cold, caused by simultaneous dilatation of capillaries and constriction of venules. It resolves completely on rewarming and needs no treatment.

**UNDERSTAND:** It reflects the immature autonomic control of the neonatal cutaneous vasculature — hence its presence in up to 50% of normal infants and its disappearance with maturity. The single most useful clinical question is: does it disappear on warming?

**MUST KNOW:**

- Persistent cutis marmorata (not resolving on rewarming) is the abnormal finding — associated with trisomy 18 and 21, hypothyroidism, Cornelia de Lange syndrome, and homocystinuria.
- Cutis marmorata TELANGIECTATICA congenita (CMTC) is a distinct congenital vascular malformation — fixed, does not resolve on warming, often segmental with atrophy and ulceration, and associated with limb length/girth asymmetry, glaucoma and other anomalies (and with macrocephaly–capillary malformation syndrome). It requires ophthalmology review and limb monitoring.
- Distinguish from livedo reticularis in adults, where a persistent net-like pattern suggests antiphospholipid syndrome, vasculitis (polyarteritis nodosa), cryoglobulinaemia, cholesterol embolisation or amantadine — and livedo racemosa (broken, irregular) is more strongly pathological (Sneddon syndrome).
- Other benign newborn skin findings to have ready: erythema toxicum neonatorum (eosinophil-rich pustules, days 2–3, benign), transient neonatal pustular melanosis, milia, Mongolian spot (dermal melanocytosis), harlequin colour change, and neonatal cephalic pustulosis.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Cutis marmorata occurs due to” → exposure to cold temperature.

**REMEMBER:** *If it vanishes on warming it is physiological; if it is fixed, think CMTC or a trisomy.*

### 100. Dermatitis Artefacta

**PEARL:** Self-inflicted skin lesions with bizarre, angular, geometric or linear shapes and clear-cut borders, surrounded by entirely normal skin, appearing fully formed with no prodrome, in accessible areas — and accompanied by a “hollow history” (the patient cannot describe how they evolved).

**understand:** The morphology is the diagnosis: no natural dermatosis produces straight lines and sharp geometric angles, because disease follows biology, not the hand. Lesions also spare areas the patient cannot reach (classically the mid-upper back).

**MUST KNOW:**

- Typically affects adolescent and young adult females; associated with psychosocial stress, borderline personality traits and depression.
- Key contrast with allergic contact dermatitis (which the henna history is meant to suggest): contact dermatitis shows an evolving inflammatory sequence — erythema, oedema, vesicles — conforming exactly to the shape of the contactant, and it itches. Artefacta lesions appear abruptly, fully formed, with an unnatural outline.
- Management is delicate: do not confront the patient. Treat the skin (occlusive dressings — lesions heal beneath them, which is itself diagnostic), build rapport, and involve psychiatry non-judgementally. Confrontation ends the therapeutic relationship.
- Related psychodermatoses: dermatitis simulata (dyes/paint applied, no real damage), malingering (external incentive), trichotillomania (irregular broken hairs of varying length), acne excoriée, neurotic excoriations (patient admits scratching), and delusional infestation/parasitosis (with the “matchbox sign” — treat with antipsychotics such as risperidone or olanzapine).
- Always exclude a genuine dermatosis first — artefacta is a diagnosis of exclusion, and misdiagnosis in both directions is harmful.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Bizarre angular lesions after applying henna → dermatitis artefacta, not contact dermatitis. The unnatural geometry beats the exposure history.

**REMEMBER:** *Straight lines and sharp angles on normal skin, in places the hand can reach — the skin was not sick, it was injured.*

### 101. Malakoplakia

**pearl:** Soft yellow-brown plaques/nodules in an immunosuppressed patient (classically post-transplant), most often urinary tract, and cutaneously in the perianal and genital region. Histology: sheets of foamy macrophages (von Hansemann cells) containing Michaelis–Gutmann bodies — laminated, targetoid, calcified intracytoplasmic inclusions.

**UNDERSTAND:** The lesion results from a defect in macrophage phagolysosomal function — bacteria (usually *E. Coli* or *Proteus*) are ingested but not digested. The undigested bacterial debris becomes mineralised with calcium and iron, forming the Michaelis–Gutmann bodies — which is why they stain with von Kossa (calcium) and Perls’ Prussian blue (iron) as well as PAS.

**MUST KNOW:**

- The name means “soft plaque” (Greek) — soft, yellow, slightly raised.
- The Michaelis–Gutmann body is pathognomonic and is the entire answer to any question about this condition.
- Risk factors: renal/organ transplantation and immunosuppressants (mycophenolate, tacrolimus, steroids), HIV, malignancy, diabetes, chronic debilitating illness.
- Treatment: antibiotics that concentrate inside macrophages — fluoroquinolones, co-trimoxazole, rifampicin — plus reduction of immunosuppression, and bethanechol/ascorbic acid (which raise cyclic GMP and improve phagolysosomal function). Surgical excision for localised disease.
- Differential in a transplant patient with progressive nodules: cutaneous TB, atypical mycobacteria, deep fungal infection (cryptococcus, histoplasma), Kaposi sarcoma, and post-transplant lymphoproliferative disease — so biopsy with culture is mandatory.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Transplant patient + perianal/scalp plaques + targetoid intracytoplasmic inclusions → malakoplakia.

**also know:** Commonest organism: E. Coli (also Proteus); commonest site overall = urinary tract; cutaneous form favours perianal/genital skin. Classic setting: renal transplant/immunosuppression.

**REMEMBER:** *Macrophages that swallow but cannot digest — and the debris calcifies into Michaelis–Gutmann bodies.*

### 102. Acanthosis Nigricans

**PEARL:** Velvety, symmetrical, hyperpigmented thickening of flexural skin — axillae, neck, groin, submammary folds. The commonest association is obesity (and it regresses with weight loss).

**UNDERSTAND:** It is driven by insulin resistance: high circulating insulin cross-reacts with IGF-1 receptors on keratinocytes and fibroblasts, stimulating proliferation. So an is essentially a visible marker of hyperinsulinaemia — which is why it appears in obesity, type 2 diabetes and pcos, and why it is a recognised screening sign for metabolic syndrome in children.

**MUST KNOW:**

- The clinically critical variant is malignant acanthosis nigricans — suspect when it is of sudden onset, extensive, involves mucosal surfaces, palms (“tripe palms”) and is itchy, in a thin or weight-losing adult. The commonest underlying tumour is gastric adenocarcinoma (~60%), then other intra-abdominal adenocarcinomas.
- Associated paraneoplastic signs to look for together: tripe palms, the sign of Leser–Trélat (sudden eruption of multiple seborrhoeic keratoses), and florid cutaneous papillomatosis — all point to the same internal malignancy.
- Other associations: pcos, type 2 diabetes, acromegaly, Cushing syndrome, hypothyroidism/Hashimoto’s, and drugs — nicotinic acid, systemic corticosteroids, OCPs, insulin itself, and protease inhibitors.
- Hair-an syndrome = HyperAndrogenism + Insulin Resistance + Acanthosis Nigricans.
- Treatment is of the cause — weight loss and insulin sensitisers (metformin); topical retinoids/keratolytics are cosmetic adjuncts.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Most commonly associated condition” → obesity (diabetes and pcos are the tempting wrong answers — they are associated, but obesity is commonest).

**REMEMBER:** *Velvety flexures = too much insulin. Sudden, itchy, on the palms and mucosa in a thin adult = look for a stomach cancer.*

### 103. Diabetic Skin Markers

**PEARL:** Diabetic dermopathy (“shin spots”, Binkley’s spots) — asymptomatic oval dull-red papules on the shins that evolve into atrophic hyperpigmented scars. It is the commonest cutaneous marker of diabetes and correlates with microvascular complications (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy).

**UNDERSTAND:** The pathology is microangiopathy — arteriolar basement membrane thickening with haemosiderin and melanin deposition. That is precisely why it acts as a visible surrogate for microvascular disease elsewhere: if you see shin spots, look harder at the eyes and kidneys.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Distinguish from necrobiosis lipoidica diabeticorum: larger, well-defined plaques on the Pretibial area with a yellow-brown atrophic centre, prominent telangiectasia and a violaceous border; it may ulcerate. It is far rarer, occurs mostly in women, and — importantly — its course does not parallel glycaemic control, and it can precede diabetes or occur without it. Histology: palisading granulomas with necrobiosis through the full dermis.
- Granuloma annulare is the third member of the differential: annular rings of firm dermal papules without scale or epidermal change (versus tinea, which is scaly), on the dorsa of hands and feet; the generalised form has the diabetes association.
- Other diabetic skin signs worth listing: acanthosis nigricans (insulin resistance), diabetic bullae (bullosis diabeticorum) — spontaneous painless blisters on acral skin, scleredema diabeticorum (thick upper back), diabetic cheiroarthropathy with the “prayer sign” (waxy skin, limited joint mobility), eruptive xanthomas (with hypertriglyceridaemia), rubeosis faciei, and recurrent candidal and staphylococcal infections.
- Malignant otitis externa and rhino-orbital mucormycosis are the diabetic infections that kill.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Asymptomatic red-brown papules on the shins + high blood sugar → diabetic dermopathy (necrobiosis lipoidica is the tempting but wrong answer — it is yellow, atrophic and much rarer).

**REMEMBER:** *Shin spots are common, brown and harmless but flag microvascular disease; necrobiosis lipoidica is rare, yellow, atrophic and may ulcerate.*

### 104. Wood’s Lamp Fluorescence

**PEARL:** Erythrasma — coral red. Pityriasis versicolor — yellow / golden. Tinea capitis (*Microsporum*) — blue-green. Vitiligo — chalky/milky white with sharp accentuation. Pseudomonas — green. Porphyria cutanea tarda urine — pink-orange.

**UNDERSTAND:** Wood’s lamp emits long-wave UV (365 nm), and fluorescence comes from the organism’s own metabolites — coproporphyrin III in *Corynebacterium minutissimum* gives erythrasma its coral red; pteridines in *Microsporum* give the blue-green.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Crucial negative: *Trichophyton* species do not fluoresce — and *T. Tonsurans* and *T. Violaceum* are the commonest causes of tinea capitis in India. So a negative Wood’s lamp does not exclude tinea capitis — an important, frequently examined limitation.
- Vitiligo vs its mimics is the highest-yield clinical use: vitiligo enhances to bright chalky white (complete melanocyte loss), whereas nevus depigmentosus and nevus anemicus do not enhance, and pityriasis alba is only faintly accentuated. Wood’s lamp also reveals subclinical lesions and defines margins before treatment.
- Erythrasma — moist intertriginous areas (groin, toe webs, axillae), often mistaken for tinea; treat with topical fusidic acid, clindamycin or oral erythromycin.
- Pityriasis versicolor — *Malassezia*; “spaghetti and meatballs” on KOH; hypo- or hyperpigmented macules with fine branny scale and a positive scratch/Besnier sign.
- Examine in a completely dark room after 1 minute of dark adaptation; remember false positives from soap residue, lint, deodorants and tetracyclines.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Straight matching question — coral red is the anchor everyone remembers; learn the other three around it.

**REMEMBER:** *Coral red Corynebacterium, golden Malassezia, blue-green Microsporum, milky white vitiligo.*

### 105. Anti-Psoriatic Drugs — Matching the Toxicity

**PEARL:** PUVA → long-term risk of NON-melanoma skin cancer. Ciclosporin → highly effective but nephrotoxic. Methotrexate → abortifacient/teratogenic, hepatotoxic, myelosuppressive. Acitretin → mucositis, hyperlipidaemia, and teratogenic.

**UNDERSTAND:** Psoriasis is a chronic disease of young people, so cumulative toxicity — not acute toxicity — governs drug choice. That is why ciclosporin is used for short crisis courses rather than maintenance, and why PUVA’s dose is tracked lifelong.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Acitretin’s teratogenicity outlasts the drug: contraception is required for 3 years after stopping (it is re-esterified to etretinate, which is stored in fat). Alcohol accelerates this conversion — so patients must avoid alcohol. Isotretinoin needs only 1 month. This difference is heavily examined.
- Methotrexate: give weekly, never daily (fatal errors occur); supplement folic acid; monitor FBC, LFT, renal function; avoid with trimethoprim/co-trimoxazole and NSAIDs; contraindicated in pregnancy (stop 3 months before conception in both sexes).
- Acitretin is the retinoid of choice in pustular and erythrodermic psoriasis, and is preferred in HIV-associated psoriasis because it is not immunosuppressive.
- Systemic steroids are avoided in psoriasis — withdrawal precipitates pustular or erythrodermic flares. A safety point worth owning.
- Biologics: TNF-α inhibitors (screen for latent TB before starting), anti-IL-17 (secukinumab), anti-IL-12/23 and anti-IL-23. Anti-IL-17 agents can worsen inflammatory bowel disease and cause candidiasis.
- Topical first-line: potent corticosteroid + vitamin D analogue (calcipotriol); coal tar, dithranol, and NB-UVB phototherapy.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Column-matching question. Acitretin ↔ mucositis/teratogenic and PUVA ↔ skin cancer are the two anchors.

**REMEMBER:** *PUVA burns you later (cancer), ciclosporin hits the kidney, methotrexate the fetus and liver, acitretin the mucosa — for three years.*

### 106. Patch Testing

**PEARL:** In patch testing for allergic contact dermatitis (a type IV delayed hypersensitivity reaction), the patches are applied to the back under occlusion and removed AT 48 hours for the first reading. A further reading at 72–96 hours is essential for confirmation.

**UNDERSTAND:** Delayed hypersensitivity needs time for T-cell recruitment — so reading too early misses reactions. The second, later reading distinguishes a true allergic reaction (which crescendos — becomes stronger with time) from an irritant reaction (which DECRESCENDOS — fades). That is the entire reason for two readings.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Do not confuse the tests: patch test → type IV delayed hypersensitivity (contact allergens); prick/intradermal test → type I immediate IgE (read in 15–20 minutes); photopatch test → photoallergic contact dermatitis (duplicate patches, one irradiated).
- Preparation: the patient should stop systemic steroids and immunosuppressants, avoid the back being sun-exposed or having topical steroids applied, and the back must be kept dry during the 48 hours.
- The Indian Standard Series includes nickel (the commonest contact allergen worldwide), PPD, fragrance mix, potassium dichromate (cement — occupational hand dermatitis in construction workers), parthenium (Parthenium hysterophorus — the classic Indian airborne contact dermatitis, affecting exposed sites), and preservatives like Methylisothiazolinone.
- Methylisothiazolinone (MI) is a preservative in wet wipes, cosmetics, shampoos and paints — a major and rising cause of allergic contact dermatitis, including airborne dermatitis from freshly painted walls.
- Allergic vs irritant contact dermatitis: allergic requires prior sensitisation, affects a minority exposed, spreads beyond the contact site, and is intensely itchy; irritant affects anyone with enough exposure, is confined to the contact area, and burns more than it itches.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “When should the patch be removed?” → 48 hours (72–96 h is the *final reading*, the intended trap — read the question).

**REMEMBER:** *Off at 48 hours, read again at 96 — allergy crescendos, irritation fades.*

### 107. Tzanck Smear

**PEARL:** Scrapings from a herpetic (HSV or VZV) vesicle show both acantholytic (Tzanck) cells and multinucleate giant cells with moulded nuclei and margination of chromatin.

**UNDERSTAND:** Herpesviruses fuse infected keratinocytes into syncytia — hence the multinucleate giant cells — and simultaneously break the desmosomal connections, releasing rounded acantholytic cells. So a herpetic lesion produces both findings, which is why “both A and B” is the answer.

**MUST KNOW:**

- The Tzanck smear is quick and cheap but not specific — it cannot distinguish HSV-1 from HSV-2 from VZV. PCR is the investigation of choice; direct fluorescent antibody or viral culture are alternatives.
- Tzanck (acantholytic) cells alone, without giant cells, occur in pemphigus vulgaris — the other classic indication for the test.
- Molluscum contagiosum gives Henderson–Paterson bodies (large intracytoplasmic inclusions) — a poxvirus, with umbilicated papules; extensive facial molluscum in an adult should prompt HIV testing.
- Genital ulcer differential (essential for India): herpes — multiple, grouped, painful, shallow, recurrent; primary syphilis — single, painless, indurated chancre with clean base, dark-ground microscopy positive; chancroid (*H. Ducreyi*) — multiple, painful, ragged/undermined edge, “school of fish” on Gram stain; LGV — transient ulcer with prominent groove sign lymphadenopathy; donovanosis (klebsiella granulomatis) — beefy-red painless granulomatous ulcer, donovan bodies on Giemsa.
- Herpes treatment: aciclovir/valaciclovir; suppressive therapy if ≥6 recurrences/year; and in pregnancy caesarean section is indicated for active genital lesions at delivery.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Genital vesicles/ulcers → “scrapings show” → both Tzanck cells and multinucleate giant cells.

**REMEMBER:** *Herpes fuses cells (giant cells) and unglues them (Tzanck cells) — you see both.*

### 108. Munro vs Pautrier

**PEARL:** Munro microabscess → Psoriasis; Pautrier microabscess → Mycosis fungoides. (Cross the letters.)

**MUST KNOW:**

- Munro = neutrophils in the stratum corneum; spongiform pustule of Kogoj = neutrophils in the stratum spinosum (also psoriasis).
- Pautrier = intraepidermal collections of atypical CD4+ lymphocytes with epidermotropism.
