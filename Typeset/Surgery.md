## General Surgical Principles — Wound Healing, Sutures, Incisions and Asepsis

### 1. Pneumoperitoneum: Causes and Non-Causes

**PEARL:** Free gas under the diaphragm means a perforated viscus or an iatrogenic cause — acute intestinal obstruction does not cause pneumoperitoneum (until it perforates).

**UNDERSTAND:** Gas enters the peritoneal cavity only when the bowel wall is breached or gas is deliberately introduced. Obstruction distends bowel *from within* — the gas stays intraluminal, which is why the film shows dilated loops and air–fluid levels rather than a crescent of free air.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Causes: perforated duodenal/gastric ulcer (commonest surgical cause), any bowel perforation, post-laparoscopy (residual CO₂, benign for ~3–7 days), peritoneal dialysis, post-laparotomy.
- Best film: erect chest X-ray — detects as little as 1–2 mL of free gas; if the patient cannot sit up, use a left lateral decubitus film (gas over the liver).
- Absence of free air does not exclude perforation (~30% have none) → CECT is the most sensitive test.
- Small bowel obstruction: valvulae conniventes cross the full lumen diameter, central loops, step-ladder/concertina pattern. Large bowel obstruction: haustra are irregular and do not cross the full width, peripheral loops.
- Beware CHILAIDITI sign — colon interposed between liver and diaphragm mimicking free air (haustra visible within the “gas”).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “All of the following cause this X-ray appearance except” with obstruction as the answer; or an image of gas under both domes asking the diagnosis or the next step (erect CXR / laparotomy).

**REMEMBER:** *Obstruction fills the bowel; perforation empties it into the peritoneum.*

### 2. Surgical Wound Classification and Surgical Site Infection

**PEARL:** Class I clean — no viscus opened, no inflammation (hernia repair, breast biopsy). Class II clean-contaminated — a viscus opened under controlled conditions without significant spillage (elective cholecystectomy, elective colon resection). Class III contaminated — fresh accidental wounds, a major break in sterile technique, gross GI spillage, or incision through inflamed but non-purulent tissue — open cardiac massage and penetrating abdominal trauma belong here. Class IV dirty — pus, perforated viscus, or old traumatic wounds with devitalised tissue.

**UNDERSTAND:** The classification predicts the expected bacterial load at the time of closure, which determines infection risk and antibiotic strategy. Open cardiac massage is class III because emergency thoracotomy is performed in uncontrolled conditions with an inevitable breach of sterility — the classification grades circumstances, not anatomy.

**MUST KNOW:**

- SSI rates: clean 1–2% (unchanged by prophylaxis); clean-contaminated 6–9% → 3% with prophylaxis; contaminated 13–20% → 6%; dirty 40% → 7%.
- Antibiotic prophylaxis: a single dose within 60 minutes before the incision (120 minutes for vancomycin/fluoroquinolones), redosed for long operations or major blood loss. It is prophylaxis, not treatment — continuing beyond 24 hours does not reduce SSI and breeds resistance. Dirty wounds need therapeutic antibiotics.
- Measures that genuinely reduce SSI: normothermia, glycaemic control, supplemental oxygen, hair removal with CLIPPERS not razors (and only if necessary), chlorhexidine–alcohol skin preparation, and not closing a dirty wound primarily (delayed primary closure at 4–5 days).
- SSI classification: superficial incisional (skin and subcutaneous), deep incisional (fascia and muscle), organ/space — all within 30 days of surgery, or 90 days if an implant is placed.
- SOUTHAMPTON wound grading: 0 normal; I erythema only; II erythema plus other inflammatory signs; III serous or serosanguinous discharge; IV pus; V deep or severe infection.
- Patient risk factors: diabetes, smoking, obesity, malnutrition/hypoalbuminaemia, immunosuppression, remote infection, prolonged preoperative stay — the basis of the NNIS risk index (ASA >2, contaminated/dirty wound, operation exceeding the 75th percentile duration).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Match the scenario to the class. Emergency procedures in uncontrolled settings (open cardiac massage, penetrating trauma) are contaminated (III), not dirty — dirty requires pus or established perforation.

**REMEMBER:** *Controlled entry is clean-contaminated; uncontrolled entry is contaminated; pus is dirty.*

### 3. Wound Healing Timeline

**PEARL:** In the first week the wound is held by sutures, the haemostatic plug and weak type III collagen — not type I. Type I collagen replaces type III during weeks 2–3, and final tensile strength reaches only 60–80% of normal skin.

**UNDERSTAND:** Early wounds have almost no intrinsic strength because type III collagen is thin and poorly cross-linked — laid down fast for scaffolding, not strength. Remodelling substitutes thick, cross-linked type I collagen and realigns the fibres along lines of stress. Strength comes from the weave and cross-linking, not the quantity of collagen, which is why the scar never regains normal strength.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Phases: haemostasis (immediate) → inflammation (days 1–5, neutrophils then macrophages) → proliferation (days 5–21: fibroblasts, angiogenesis, granulation tissue, epithelialisation) → remodelling (3 weeks to 1–2 years).
- Strength milestones: <10% at 1 week; ~30% at 3 weeks; ~60–80% at 3 months, then a plateau. Skin sutures come out at 5 days (face) to 10–14 days (back and limbs).
- Vitamin C, zinc and oxygen are essential for collagen cross-linking; steroids, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, diabetes, malnutrition, jaundice, uraemia, infection and smoking impair healing. Vitamin A reverses the inhibitory effect of steroids.
- Negative pressure wound therapy (VAC): intermittent −125 mmHg hastens debridement and granulation. Contraindicated with exposed vessels/organs/grafts, untreated infection, inadequately debrided necrotic tissue, and malignancy (Marjolin ulcer).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Which is false” → the statement crediting type I collagen in the first week.

**REMEMBER:** *Type three first and weak; type one later and strong.*

### 4. Surgical Knots and Suture Materials

**PEARL:** The surgeon’s knot is a reef (square) knot whose first throw has a double wrap, which stops the first throw slipping while the second is tied. A granny knot has both throws in the same direction (it slips); a reef knot has them in opposite directions (the standard secure knot); a half-hitch is a single throw. For vascular anastomosis use polypropylene (Prolene) — non-absorbable, monofilament, minimally thrombogenic.

**UNDERSTAND:** Knot security depends on friction versus tension. In a reef knot the two throws lie in opposite directions so they lock against each other; in a granny knot they lie the same way and simply slide — which is why a granny knot is used deliberately when you *want* to tighten tissue, then locked with a reef throw. The surgeon’s knot adds a second wrap in the first throw to multiply the friction, holding tissue apposed during the vulnerable moment before the second throw is laid. Suture choice follows the same logic of matching material to task: an artery must hold for a lifetime and must neither harbour bacteria nor provoke thrombus, so it needs a permanent, smooth, monofilament suture.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Every knot must be laid square and tightened without sawing or “cheese-wiring”. Monofilament sutures (nylon, polypropylene) have memory and low friction, so they need more throws (5–6) than braided sutures (3–4).
- Absorbable: catgut (obsolete), POLYGLACTIN/Vicryl (braided, 60–90 days), POLYDIOXANONE/PDS (monofilament, ~6 months), poliglecaprone/Monocryl. NON-absorbable: polypropylene (vascular, hernia mesh, skin), nylon, silk (braided, high tissue reaction, still used for drains and ties), steel (sternum), linen.
- Braided sutures harbour bacteria in their interstices → avoid in contaminated fields and in vessels.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The double first throw identifies the surgeon’s knot; Prolene for vessels is asked repeatedly.

**REMEMBER:** *Double the first throw so it cannot slip; arteries are sewn with Prolene.*

### 5. Named Surgical Incisions

**PEARL:** Kocher — subcostal (open cholecystectomy). CHEVRON/rooftop — bilateral subcostal. LANZ — transverse, cosmetic appendicectomy. GRIDIRON/McBURNEY — oblique appendicectomy. PFANNENSTIEL — suprapubic transverse. Battle — lower right paramedian. Rutherford MORISON — muscle-cutting iliac fossa. Gibson — oblique/curvilinear iliac fossa incision for renal transplant and distal ureteric access.

**UNDERSTAND:** Gibson is the standard transplant incision because it gives extraperitoneal access to the iliac vessels, lower ureter and bladder with low morbidity — the graft sits retroperitoneally in the iliac fossa, so the peritoneum need never be opened.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Image-based incision identification.

### 6. Surgical Risk Scores

**PEARL:** Thoracoscore predicts operative mortality in thoracic surgery from 9 preoperative variables — complications of surgery are not among them (they are outcomes, not predictors).

**UNDERSTAND:** A risk score must be computable before the operation, so every variable has to be a preoperative patient or procedure characteristic. Including a postoperative complication would make the score circular and useless for decision-making — which is exactly why the distractor is wrong on principle, without needing the list memorised.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Thoracoscore variables: age, sex, ASA grade, performance status, dyspnoea score, priority of surgery (elective vs urgent), extent of surgery, malignant diagnosis, composite comorbidity score.
- Other scores: ASA — preoperative physical status; POSSUM/P-POSSUM — morbidity and mortality in general surgery; APACHE II — critical illness and pancreatitis; Ranson and BISAP — pancreatitis; child–Pugh and MELD — liver reserve; Glasgow-Blatchford and Rockall — upper GI bleeding; ALVARADO — appendicitis; SIRS/qSOFA — sepsis.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Not a component” → complication of surgery.

**REMEMBER:** *A preoperative score can only contain preoperative facts.*

### 7. Intra-abdominal Abscess — Commonest Site

**PEARL:** The right subhepatic space (Rutherford Morison’s pouch) is the commonest site of an intra-abdominal abscess.

**UNDERSTAND:** It is the most dependent part of the peritoneal cavity in the supine patient, so infected fluid from appendicitis, a perforated duodenal ulcer or cholecystitis gravitates there. Its position downstream of the right paracolic gutter, which channels fluid from the pelvis and right iliac fossa, makes it the natural collecting point.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Boundaries: liver and diaphragm above and to the right, transverse colon and hepatic flexure below, foramen of Winslow medially, duodenum inferiorly, right kidney posteriorly.
- Common sources: appendicitis, perforated duodenal ulcer, cholecystitis, postoperative upper abdominal surgery.
- Other sites: subphrenic (right and left), left subhepatic (lesser sac), paracolic gutters, and the pelvis (pouch of Douglas — the most dependent site overall in the erect/semi-recumbent patient).
- Presentation: swinging fever, tachycardia, shoulder tip pain — “pus somewhere, pus nowhere, pus under the diaphragm”; investigate with ultrasound or CT.
- Treatment: image-guided percutaneous (pigtail) drainage plus antibiotics; surgery only if drainage fails or the collection is multiloculated.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Labelled anatomical diagram of the peritoneal spaces.

**REMEMBER:** *Fluid runs down the right gutter and pools under the liver.*

### 8. Gossypiboma

**PEARL:** A retained surgical sponge/swab causing a foreign body reaction.

**MUST KNOW:** Two responses — an exudative reaction (early abscess/fistula) or a fibrinous reaction (late mass mimicking a tumour). Imaging: a whorled mass with a radio-opaque marker and a “spongiform” gas pattern. Prevention is swab and instrument counts; it is a classic medicolegal (res ipsa loquitur) case.

**REMEMBER:** *Gossypiboma = the sponge you left behind — count them.*

## Fluids, Nutrition, Haemorrhage and Transfusion

### 9. Classification of Surgical Haemorrhage

**PEARL:** Primary haemorrhage = during the operation. Reactionary = within 24 hours (typically 4–6 hours), as the blood pressure rises and clots or ligatures are dislodged — this is what causes post-thyroidectomy airway obstruction. Secondary = 7–14 days, from infection or erosion of a vessel wall.

**UNDERSTAND:** The timing is a direct consequence of the mechanism. Intraoperative hypotension and vasospasm hide small bleeders; when the patient is warmed, resuscitated and normotensive again — within hours — those vessels reopen. Later bleeding cannot be mechanical, because clots are organised by then; it takes days of infection to digest a vessel wall, hence the 7–14 day window.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Reactionary haemorrhage occurs within 24 hours — the stem’s favourite false statement is “24–48 hours”.
- Post-thyroidectomy haematoma is the classic reactionary bleed: tense neck swelling and stridor within hours. The airway obstruction is from venous and lymphatic congestion of the larynx, not direct compression — so open the wound AT the bedside immediately (remove clips/sutures, evacuate the clot) before attempting intubation. Every thyroidectomy bed keeps a clip/suture-remover set.
- Early post-thyroidectomy problems by timing: immediate hoarseness → recurrent laryngeal nerve; hours → haematoma; 24–48 hours → thyroid storm; 2–3 days → hypocalcaemia.
- Secondary haemorrhage: manage the cause — antibiotics, control sepsis, proximal control of the vessel; local suturing in infected tissue fails.
- Classes of haemorrhagic shock (ATLS): I <15% (normal vitals); II 15–30% (tachycardia, narrowed pulse pressure, anxiety); III 30–40% (hypotension, confusion); IV >40% (lethargy, minimal urine output). The earliest sign is a narrowed pulse pressure with tachycardia — hypotension is late, especially in the young.
- ATLS 11th edition (2025) changed the primary survey to “xABCDE” — an “x” PREFIXED for immediate control of exsanguinating external haemorrhage (tourniquet, wound packing) before the airway. The edition is built on damage-control resuscitation: minimal crystalloid, early balanced 1:1:1 blood component therapy, permissive hypotension until bleeding is controlled, routine point-of-care ultrasound, and “spine motion restriction” replacing rigid full immobilisation.[^1]

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Identify the incorrect statement” — the false option is the timing of reactionary haemorrhage. Learn the three windows: during, <24 h, 7–14 days.

**REMEMBER:** *Primary now, reactionary when the pressure comes back, secondary when the infection eats through.*

### 10. Massive Transfusion: The Metabolic Consequences

**PEARL:** Massive transfusion causes HYPOcalcaemia (not hyper), hypothermia, hypokalaemia or hyperkalaemia, metabolic alkalosis (late, from citrate metabolism) and coagulopathy. Hypercalcaemia IS not A complication.

**UNDERSTAND:** Stored blood is anticoagulated with citrate, which chelates ionised calcium — so calcium falls the moment large volumes are infused. That same citrate is then metabolised by the liver to bicarbonate, which is why the picture evolves from early acidosis (shock plus the stored blood’s own acid load) to a late metabolic alkalosis. Potassium leaks from stored red cells (hyperkalaemia on rapid transfusion of old blood), but the alkalosis and catecholamines subsequently drive potassium into cells, giving late hypokalaemia. Every complication traces back to the storage lesion or the citrate.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Definition: ≥10 units of red cells in 24 hours, or ≥4 units in 1 hour with ongoing bleeding, or replacement of one blood volume.
- Ratio 1:1:1 red cells : FFP : platelets — balanced resuscitation reduces mortality (PROPPR). Add tranexamic acid within 3 hours of trauma (crash-2), and cryoprecipitate to keep fibrinogen >1.5 g/L.
- The lethal triad: hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy — each worsens the others. Use warmed fluids and blood.
- Give calcium empirically during massive transfusion — hypocalcaemia causes hypotension and arrhythmia and worsens coagulopathy (the “diamond of death” when added to the triad).
- Damage-control resuscitation: permissive hypotension (SBP ~90, unless head injury), minimal crystalloid, early blood products, rapid haemorrhage control.
- Other transfusion complications: TRALI (donor antibodies, non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema within 6 hours), TACO (volume overload), acute haemolytic reaction (ABO error — the commonest fatal cause is clerical), febrile non-haemolytic reaction, citrate toxicity, and 2,3-DPG depletion shifting the oxygen dissociation curve left (impaired oxygen unloading).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Which is not an adverse effect” — the answer inverts a real derangement. Calcium goes down, temperature goes down.

**REMEMBER:** *Citrate steals the calcium and later leaves behind bicarbonate.*

### 11. Nutrition — Parenteral versus Enteral Complications

**PEARL:** Aspiration pneumonia is a complication of enteral feeding, not of parenteral nutrition. Central-line TPN causes pneumothorax, line sepsis, refeeding syndrome, metabolic derangement, hepatic steatosis and metabolic bone disease.

**UNDERSTAND:** The complication follows the route. TPN bypasses the gut entirely, so it cannot cause aspiration — but it requires a central line (mechanical and infective complications) and delivers nutrients unphysiologically. Enteral feeding uses the gut, so its problems are tube-related and gastrointestinal, chief among them regurgitation and aspiration.

**MUST KNOW:**

- “If the gut works, use it” — enteral nutrition preserves mucosal integrity, reduces bacterial translocation, and is cheaper and safer.
- Enteral complications: tube-related (commonest — malposition, blockage, displacement, nasal erosion), gastrointestinal (diarrhoea, bloating, aspiration), metabolic and infective. Prevent aspiration by semi-recumbent (30–45°) positioning.
- Refeeding syndrome affects both routes: reintroducing carbohydrate to a severely malnourished patient drives phosphate, potassium and magnesium intracellularly → hypophosphataemia (the hallmark), arrhythmias, heart failure, seizures, rhabdomyolysis. Prevent by starting at 10 kcal/kg/day, giving thiamine before feeding, and monitoring electrolytes daily.
- TPN-specific: central line sepsis (commonest serious complication), pneumothorax, thrombosis, hyperglycaemia, cholestasis and hepatic steatosis, trace element deficiencies.
- Ringer’s lactate (Hartmann’s): Na 131, Cl 111, K 5, Ca 2, lactate 29 mEq/L; NO glucose. Do not run it in the same line as blood — its calcium chelates the citrate anticoagulant and causes clotting. Avoid in liver failure (lactate not metabolised) and in metabolic alkalosis.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Not a complication of this method” — identify from the image whether the route is central (TPN) or enteral, then pick the complication belonging to the other route.

**REMEMBER:** *Only food that passes the throat can be aspirated.*

### 12. Intraosseous Access

**PEARL:** When intravenous access fails in an emergency, place an intraosseous needle — first choice the anterior proximal tibia, 1–2 cm below and medial to the tibial tuberosity — if IV access is not obtained within 1 minute.

**UNDERSTAND:** The medullary cavity of a long bone is a non-collapsible venous plexus draining via emissary and nutrient veins directly into the central circulation. Unlike peripheral veins, it does not collapse in shock or cardiac arrest — which is precisely why it remains cannulable when every peripheral vein has shut down.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Any drug or fluid that can be given intravenously can be given intraosseously, at the same dose — including blood, inotropes and anaesthetic agents. Flow improves with a pressure bag.
- Other sites: distal tibia (above the medial malleolus), distal femur, proximal humerus, sternum, iliac crest.
- Contraindications: fracture of the target bone, previous IO attempt in the same bone, infection or burn over the site, osteogenesis imperfecta or osteopetrosis, prosthesis.
- Complications: extravasation and compartment syndrome, osteomyelitis (rare), fracture, growth plate injury — avoid the physis in children. Remove the needle within 24 hours once other access is secured.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Image recognition of a needle in the proximal tibia. The purpose — vascular access for fluids/drugs, not marrow sampling — is the discriminator.

**REMEMBER:** *Marrow is a vein that cannot collapse.*

## Trauma

### 13. Penetrating Abdominal Trauma — Who Goes Straight to Laparotomy

**PEARL:** Evisceration mandates emergency laparotomy even in a haemodynamically stable patient. A stable stab wound without these features is investigated (local wound exploration / CT / serial examination), not opened.

**UNDERSTAND:** The decision is never “stab wound = laparotomy”. It is: *is there already proof of significant intraperitoneal injury?* Instability, peritonitis, evisceration and gunshot wounds are each independent proof — further imaging only delays a certainty. Everything else is uncertain, and up to half of anterior abdominal stab wounds do not even breach the peritoneum, so operating on all of them produces a large non-therapeutic laparotomy rate.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Mandatory laparotomy: haemodynamic instability, peritonitis, evisceration, gunshot wound (high kinetic energy, ~90% visceral injury), impalement, blood on NG/PR.
- Stable anterior stab wound: local wound exploration → if the anterior fascia is not breached, discharge; if breached → CT with triple contrast and serial abdominal examination.
- FAST is useful when positive (guides to theatre), but a negative FAST does not exclude hollow-viscus injury — its real strength is blunt trauma and pericardial blood.
- Diagnostic peritoneal lavage is largely historic but still appears as the answer for the unstable blunt-trauma patient where FAST is unavailable or equivocal.
- Retroperitoneal haematoma zones: zone 1 (central — aorta, IVC, pancreaticoduodenal) → always explore, blunt or penetrating. Zone 2 (lateral/perinephric) → explore only if expanding, pulsatile or penetrating. Zone 3 (pelvic) → do not explore in blunt trauma (pack and embolise); explore if penetrating. The MATTOX manoeuvre mobilises the left colon medially to expose the aorta; the CATTELL–BRAASCH manoeuvre mobilises the right colon to expose the IVC.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Two near-identical stems differing in one detail — one shows omentum protruding (→ laparotomy), the other a clean stable stab (→ investigate). Read the image description, not just the vitals.

**REMEMBER:** *Unstable, peritonitic, eviscerated, or shot — open. Otherwise, look first.*

### 14. Liver Trauma: Penetrating versus Blunt

**PEARL:** Penetrating liver injury with haemodynamic instability → operative management (laparotomy). Non-operative management with angioembolisation is for the haemodynamically stable blunt injury — instability is the universal veto.

**UNDERSTAND:** Non-operative management works only when two conditions hold: the bleeding is self-limiting (the liver’s low-pressure parenchymal bleeding usually tamponades within Glisson’s capsule) and there is no hollow-viscus injury to leak. A penetrating wound violates both assumptions — the tract crosses whatever lies in its path — and hypotension proves the bleeding is not self-limiting.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Non-operative criteria (blunt): haemodynamically stable or a responder, no peritonitis, CT-defined injury, ICU monitoring available, blood on standby, and angioembolisation available for a contrast blush. Success >85% — the liver is the organ most often managed non-operatively.
- Operative indications: instability, peritonitis, ALL gunshot wounds, evisceration, ongoing transfusion requirement, associated hollow-viscus injury.
- Damage-control surgery: PERIHEPATIC packing for uncontrollable bleeding — pack, close temporarily, correct the lethal triad in ICU, re-explore at 24–48 hours.
- Pringle manoeuvre — clamping the portal triad in the free edge of the lesser omentum (portal vein + hepatic artery). If bleeding continues despite it, the source is the hepatic veins or RETROHEPATIC IVC — the most lethal liver injuries.
- AAST grading I–VI; grade VI = hepatic avulsion, essentially fatal.
- Late complications: biloma, HAEMOBILIA (QUINCKE triad — jaundice, biliary colic, upper GI bleed) treated by angioembolisation, abscess, post-traumatic pseudoaneurysm.
- The spleen is the most commonly injured organ in blunt abdominal trauma; the liver is the most commonly injured in penetrating trauma and the one most often responsible for death.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Read the vital signs before the imaging grade. BP 80/50 → theatre, whatever the CT shows.

**REMEMBER:** *Unstable or penetrating means open; stable and blunt means embolise.*

### 15. Vascular Trauma: Choosing the Conduit

**PEARL:** A 3.5 cm longitudinal tear of the brachial artery cannot be repaired primarily — it needs an interposition graft using the reversed great saphenous vein harvested from the uninjured leg. Prosthetic grafts are a last resort.

**UNDERSTAND:** Primary end-to-end anastomosis is possible only when the defect is short enough (<2 cm) to close without tension — tension causes disruption or stenosis. The great saphenous vein is preferred because it matches the artery in calibre, has comparable elastic properties allowing pulsatile flow, is non-thrombogenic, and resists infection in a contaminated traumatic field — where a synthetic graft would almost certainly become infected. It is taken from the uninjured limb so venous drainage of the damaged limb is preserved, and it is reversed so its valves do not obstruct arterial flow.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Alternatives: cephalic and lesser saphenous vein (harder to harvest, and the cephalic vein is needed for future venous access); PTFE/Dacron only when no vein is available and the field is clean.
- Hard signs of vascular injury (→ immediate exploration): pulsatile bleeding, an expanding or pulsatile haematoma, a palpable thrill or audible bruit, absent distal pulses, distal ischaemia (the 6 Ps). Soft signs (history of bleeding, small stable haematoma, proximate nerve deficit) → injured extremity index (<0.9 abnormal) or CT angiography.
- Sequence in a mangled limb: control haemorrhage → restore perfusion (shunt if necessary) → skeletal fixation → definitive vascular repair → soft-tissue cover. A temporary intravascular shunt buys time when bone must be fixed first.
- Repair principles: proximal and distal control, heparinised saline, SPATULATED end-to-end anastomosis or interposition reversed vein graft, systemic heparin if there is no other bleeding source, and completion angiography.
- Fasciotomy for prolonged ischaemia (>4–6 hours) or after revascularisation — reperfusion causes compartment syndrome, and delay costs the limb. Watch for rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalaemia and acute kidney injury.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The length of the defect is the discriminator — a long tear rules out primary anastomosis and a patch. Interposition reversed saphenous vein graft is the answer.

**REMEMBER:** *Short gap, sew it; long gap, bridge it with the patient’s own vein.*

### 16. Traumatic Diaphragmatic Hernia and Chest Drains

**PEARL:** A post-traumatic chest X-ray showing an elevated hemidiaphragm with bowel or stomach gas in the thorax (± the NG tube coiled in the chest) is a traumatic diaphragmatic hernia — chest tube insertion IS contraindicated, because the drain will perforate herniated viscus.

**UNDERSTAND:** A chest drain is placed blindly through the pleural space on the assumption that only lung lies beneath. When bowel or stomach has migrated into the hemithorax that assumption is false — the trocar or finger enters hollow viscus, converting a repairable hernia into faecal or gastric contamination of the pleura. This is why a finger sweep before insertion is mandatory in every chest drain, and why the film must be read before the procedure.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Left side in ~75% (the liver shields the right); a right-sided rupture implies far greater force and worse associated injuries.
- Diagnosis: chest X-ray (an NG tube curling above the diaphragm is pathognomonic), CT with sagittal/coronal reformats. Often missed initially, presenting late with obstruction or strangulation.
- Repair via laparotomy in the acute phase (to deal with associated intra-abdominal injuries); thoracotomy/thoracoscopy for chronic presentations, where adhesions to the lung must be divided.
- Chest drain: the “triangle of safety” — 5th intercostal space, anterior to the mid-axillary line, bounded by the lateral border of pectoralis major, the lateral border of latissimus dorsi and a line at the nipple — passing over the upper border of the rib to avoid the neurovascular bundle.
- Indications: pneumothorax (tension pneumothorax gets immediate needle decompression first, then a drain), haemothorax, empyema, chylothorax, large effusion. Cautions: diaphragmatic hernia, dense pleural adhesions, coagulopathy.
- Immediate drainage of >1500 mL of blood, or >200 mL/hour for 2–4 hours, indicates thoracotomy.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Which should be avoided?” — read the X-ray description first. Bowel in the chest rules out a chest drain; NG tube, log roll and analgesia are all safe.

**REMEMBER:** *Never put a drain into a chest full of bowel.*

### 18. Skin Grafts: Primary versus Secondary Contracture

**PEARL:** Primary contracture (immediate, elastic recoil at harvest) is greater in full-thickness grafts. Secondary contracture (late, myofibroblast-driven shrinkage after grafting) is greater in split-thickness grafts.

**UNDERSTAND:** The two contractures have entirely different mechanisms, which is why they run in opposite directions. Primary contracture is passive — the elastin in the dermis recoiling the moment the graft is cut free, so the more dermis harvested, the more it shrinks immediately. Secondary contracture is active — the wound bed’s myofibroblasts pulling the graft inward over weeks — and the dermis is what suppresses that process. A split graft carries little dermis, so it cannot restrain the bed and contracts markedly over time.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Full-thickness grafts are used where contracture would be disastrous — the face, eyelids, hands and across flexion creases (better colour match, less late contracture, but they need a well-vascularised bed and a donor site closed primarily). Split grafts cover large areas, can be MESHED to expand, and the donor site re-epithelialises spontaneously from adnexal remnants — but they contract badly and match colour poorly.
- Graft take: PLASMATIC imbibition (0–48 h) → inosculation (2–3 days) → revascularisation (4–7 days).
- The commonest cause of graft failure is haematoma (then seroma, infection — especially *Streptococcus pyogenes* — shear/movement, and a poorly vascularised bed). Grafts will not take on bare bone, bare cartilage, bare tendon or irradiated tissue — those need a flap.
- Donor sites: split — thigh, buttock; full-thickness — postauricular, supraclavicular, upper eyelid, groin. Meshing increases coverage and lets exudate escape, at the cost of a permanent lattice scar.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Two-statement selection with the contractures deliberately swapped. Anchor: more dermis → more primary, less secondary.

**REMEMBER:** *Thick grafts shrink at once and then behave; thin grafts behave at once and then shrink.*

### 19. Burn Depth, Dressings and Resuscitation

**PEARL:** A pink, oozing, exquisitely painful burn (sensitive to moving air and touch) is a superficial partial-thickness burn — dress it with a biological dressing (collagen sheet or amniotic membrane). Excision and grafting is for full-thickness burns, and silver sulphadiazine is reserved for deep dermal, full-thickness, infected or neglected burns.

**UNDERSTAND:** Pain is the depth test. Superficial burns hurt intensely because the sensory nerve endings survive; a full-thickness burn is painless and insensate because the nerves are destroyed, and it looks dry, leathery and white or charred with no capillary refill. Since a superficial partial-thickness burn has intact dermal appendages and will re-epithelialise in 2–3 weeks, the goal is simply a moist, sterile, non-adherent cover — which is what collagen provides; grafting it would be unnecessary surgery. Silver sulphadiazine forms a pseudo-eschar, delays epithelialisation and selects resistance, so it is not first choice for a clean superficial burn.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Depth: epidermal (erythema only, no blister — sunburn); superficial partial (blisters, moist, pink, blanches, very painful — heals in 2 weeks without scarring); deep partial (mottled red/white, sluggish or absent blanching, reduced sensation — heals slowly with scarring, often grafted); full thickness (white/waxy or charred, dry, leathery, insensate, no blanching — needs excision and grafting).
- PARKLAND formula: 4 mL × weight (kg) × %TBSA of Ringer’s lactate in the first 24 hours, half in the first 8 hours from the time of injury, titrated to urine output 0.5 mL/kg/hr in adults, 1 mL/kg/hr in children. Rule of nines for area; the patient’s palm ≈ 1%. Superficial epidermal burns are not counted in TBSA.
- ESCHAROTOMY for circumferential full-thickness burns of a limb (vascular compromise) or the chest (ventilatory restriction).
- Suspect inhalational injury with facial burns, singed nasal hair, soot in sputum, hoarseness or a closed-space fire — intubate early, before oedema closes the airway.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The pain and colour give the depth, and the depth gives the dressing.

**REMEMBER:** *If it hurts, it will heal — cover it. If it is numb and leathery, cut it out and graft it.*

### 20. Marjolin’s Ulcer and the Edges of Ulcers

**PEARL:** Marjolin’s ulcer = a well-differentiated (low-grade) squamous cell carcinoma arising in a chronic scar or ulcer. It is painless, slow-growing, radioresistant and does not metastasise via lymphatics — because scar tissue is avascular and has no nerves or lymphatics.

**UNDERSTAND:** Every feature follows from the avascular, denervated, alymphatic nature of scar tissue. No nerves means no pain; no lymphatics means no nodal spread (until the tumour breaches the scar into normal tissue); poor blood supply means slow growth and radioresistance (radiotherapy needs oxygen to generate free radicals). So the answer to “highly vascular” is emphatically no.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Arises in post-burn scars (classically after 10–30 years), chronic venous ulcers, chronic osteomyelitis sinuses, post-radiation ulcers and pressure sores.
- Signs of malignant change in a chronic ulcer: everted edges, exuberant cauliflower-like granulation tissue, foul discharge, bleeding on touch, increase in size, and change from painless to painful.
- Treatment: wide local excision with a 1 cm margin ± skin grafting; amputation for extensive limb disease. Node dissection only if nodes are clinically involved (once the tumour has escaped the scar).
- Ulcer edges: sloping — healing/venous; punched-out — trophic, syphilitic, ischaemic; undermined — tuberculous; rolled/beaded — basal cell carcinoma; everted — squamous cell carcinoma.
- PILONIDAL sinus — a natal cleft sinus in a hirsute young man, classically a jeep/lorry driver. The hair in the sinus is dead hair drawn in by shearing forces — hair follicles are essentially never found in the sinus wall. Recurrence is common; treatment ranges from excision with primary or Karydakis/Bascom flap closure to laying open.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “All are true except” → highly vascular. Every other stated feature is a consequence of avascularity.

**REMEMBER:** *Scar tissue has no vessels, nerves or lymphatics — so neither does the tumour in it.*

## Oesophagus and Stomach

### 21. Oesophageal Perforation and Boerhaave Syndrome

**PEARL:** Boerhaave syndrome (spontaneous, full-thickness rupture after forceful vomiting) requires operative management, whereas cervical and small iatrogenic (endoscopic) perforations without sepsis or mediastinal contamination may be managed conservatively with nil by mouth, antibiotics, nutritional support and drainage. The commonest cause of oesophageal perforation overall IS iatrogenic (endoscopy/dilatation), not Boerhaave.

**UNDERSTAND:** Oesophageal perforation is deadly because the oesophagus has NO serosa, so contamination spreads unchecked into the mediastinum, producing fulminant mediastinitis with mortality rising sharply after 24 hours. That single anatomical deficiency explains why the time from perforation to treatment is the strongest predictor of survival, and why an extensive thoracic rupture with soiling cannot be managed by antibiotics alone.

**MUST KNOW:**

- MACKLER’S triad: vomiting, lower chest pain, subcutaneous emphysema. Usually a left posterolateral distal tear. Chest X-ray shows pneumomediastinum, pleural effusion and the “V sign of NACLERIO”; confirm with a water-soluble contrast swallow or CT.
- Pleural fluid with A low pH and high amylase, OR containing food particles, IS diagnostic.
- Treatment: resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics and antifungals, and primary repair with drainage — or oesophageal stenting/diversion in late or unfit cases.
- A Mallory–Weiss tear is the contrast: a mucosal tear at the gastro-oesophageal junction after retching, presenting with haematemesis and usually self-limiting — Boerhaave presents with chest pain and surgical emphysema rather than haematemesis.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Oesophageal perforation needing operative management → Boerhaave syndrome. Haematemesis after binge drinking and retching → Mallory–Weiss tear.

**REMEMBER:** *No serosa means no barrier — a ruptured oesophagus contaminates the mediastinum within hours.*

### 22. GERD, Barrett Oesophagus and Hiatus Hernia

**PEARL:** When GERD symptoms fail to respond to A proton pump inhibitor, the next step IS upper GI endoscopy (oesophagitis, Barrett’s, stricture, malignancy) — pH studies and manometry come later, mainly before surgery. Regurgitation responds poorly to PPIs (they suppress acid, not reflux). Barrett’s oesophagus = salmon-pink columnar mucosa extending above the gastro-oesophageal junction, with intestinal metaplasia on biopsy. The “mixed” (type III) hiatus hernia involves upward migration of the gastro-oesophageal junction together with a paraoesophageal component.

**UNDERSTAND:** The distinction between acid and volume explains the whole algorithm. PPIs make the refluxate less acidic but do not stop it refluxing, so heartburn improves while regurgitation, cough, aspiration and laryngitis persist — and those “volume” symptoms are the ones that respond to a mechanical solution, fundoplication. Likewise a sliding (type I) hernia predisposes to GERD by disrupting the sphincter mechanism, while a rolling/paraoesophageal hernia keeps the sphincter in place and presents instead with obstructive symptoms and the risk of gastric volvulus.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Extra-oesophageal GERD: chronic cough, laryngitis, hoarseness, asthma, dental erosions. Alarm features demanding prompt endoscopy: dysphagia, weight loss, anaemia, GI bleeding, persistent vomiting, a mass, and age >45–50 with new symptoms.
- Barrett’s is a risk factor for adenocarcinoma (~0.5% per year) — high-dose PPI and surveillance endoscopy; low- or high-grade dysplasia is treated by endoscopic radiofrequency ablation or endoscopic mucosal resection.
- Rolling/paraoesophageal hernias are repaired even when mildly symptomatic because of incarceration and gastric volvulus; CT with oral contrast is the best imaging (endoscopy under-detects hernias).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** GERD not responding to PPI → endoscopy; “incorrect about GERD” → that regurgitation is well controlled by PPIs; salmon-coloured mucosa with reflux → Barrett oesophagus; “correct about hiatus hernia” → mixed type involves upward migration of the GE junction.

**REMEMBER:** *PPIs change the acid, surgery changes the anatomy — regurgitation needs the anatomy fixed.*

### 23. Achalasia and Oesophageal Webs (Plummer–Vinson)

**PEARL:** Dysphagia to both solids and liquids from the outset, with a bird-beak tapering and dilated oesophagus on barium, is achalasia → confirm with oesophageal manometry (gold standard) and perform upper GI endoscopy to exclude PSEUDOACHALASIA (malignancy at the cardia). The definitive treatment is HELLER’S CARDIOMYOTOMY (with a partial fundoplication) or POEM. By contrast, an oesophageal web (Plummer–Vinson) is treated by dilatation with bougies — not myotomy — and correcting the iron deficiency alone often relieves the dysphagia.

**UNDERSTAND:** Dysphagia to liquids as early as to solids is the signature of a motility disorder, because peristaltic failure affects all consistencies equally; a mechanical obstruction (web, stricture, carcinoma) blocks solids first. Manometry is needed because it measures the two defining abnormalities directly — failure of the lower oesophageal sphincter to relax and absent peristalsis — and detects them before the oesophagus dilates enough to show on barium. Endoscopy is mandatory because a tumour infiltrating the cardia reproduces every feature. And because a web is a mechanical shelf, not a muscle problem, cutting muscle achieves nothing — you dilate it.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Manometry: raised integrated relaxation pressure, absent peristalsis, ± raised resting LOS pressure. CHICAGO classification: Type I (classic); Type II (pan-oesophageal pressurisation — best response); Type III (spastic — worst response, may need a long myotomy).
- Pathology: loss of inhibitory (NO- and VIP-producing) neurons in the myenteric (Auerbach) plexus, leaving excitatory cholinergic tone unopposed. Chagas disease (*Trypanosoma cruzi*) produces an identical picture.
- Treatment options: PNEUMATIC balloon dilatation; laparoscopic HELLER’S myotomy with A partial (DOR/TOUPET) fundoplication — to prevent the reflux the myotomy would otherwise cause; POEM (especially type III). Botulinum toxin and nitrates/calcium channel blockers are for the unfit (temporary). RAMSTEDT’S pyloromyotomy IS for infantile pyloric stenosis — a classic distractor.
- Achalasia IS premalignant — squamous cell carcinoma (chronic stasis and mucosal irritation), latency 15–20 years.
- Barium features: “bird’s beak”/“rat tail” tapering, a dilated tortuous “sigmoid” oesophagus with an air-fluid level, and an absent gastric air bubble.
- Plummer–Vinson: post-cricoid web + iron-deficiency anaemia + glossitis (also angular stomatitis, koilonychia), classically in middle-aged women. Premalignant for post-cricoid squamous carcinoma — these patients need follow-up.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The stem gives the barium picture and asks what else is needed — the pair is endoscopy (rule out cancer) + manometry (confirm and subtype). “Incorrect about Plummer–Vinson” → that myotomy is used to dilate the webs.

**REMEMBER:** *Liquids and solids together means the muscle, not the lumen. Web = dilate plus iron; achalasia = myotomy.*

### 24. Peptic Ulcer — Bleeding and Perforation

**PEARL:** An actively bleeding peptic ulcer IS treated endoscopically first — dual therapy with adrenaline injection plus A second modality (clips OR thermal coagulation) — followed by high-dose PPI; surgery is for failed endotherapy. IN suspected perforation the first investigation IS an erect chest/abdominal X-ray looking for free gas (positive in ~70%), with CECT if negative but suspicion persists. GRAHAM’S omental patch repair IS for perforation, not for bleeding.

**understand:** The anatomy predicts the complication: a posterior duodenal ulcer erodes the GASTRODUODENAL artery and bleeds; an anterior duodenal ulcer, with only peritoneum beyond it, perforates. So the site described in a stem tells you which emergency you face — and why a posterior ulcer bleeding massively may need the artery under-run surgically (with risk to the common bile duct).

**MUST KNOW:**

- FORREST classification grades endoscopic stigmata and predicts rebleeding: Ia spurting, Ib oozing, IIa visible vessel, IIb adherent clot, IIc flat pigmented spot, III clean base. Classes Ia–IIb require endoscopic therapy; class III needs none.
- Always test for and eradicate *H. Pylori*, STOP NSAIDs, and repeat endoscopy for gastric ulcers to exclude malignancy.
- Perforation management: resuscitation, nasogastric decompression, IV PPI and antibiotics, then laparoscopic OR open omental (GRAHAM) patch repair with peritoneal lavage. Non-operative management is reserved for a sealed perforation in a stable patient. Endoscopy IS contraindicated in suspected perforation — insufflation worsens the leak — and CT is not required once free gas is seen.
- Post-gastrectomy syndromes: early dumping (15–30 min after a meal — colic, diarrhoea, flushing, palpitations, from hyperosmolar chyme and fluid shift) and late dumping (1–3 hours — reactive hypoglycaemia with light-headedness and syncope). Management: small, frequent, dry meals (not fewer larger meals), avoid high-glycaemic-index carbohydrate, separate fluids from solids, lie down after eating, add acarbose or octreotide; revisional surgery (Roux-en-Y) is a last resort.
- Reconstructions: BILLROTH I — gastroduodenostomy; BILLROTH II — gastrojejunostomy with a blind duodenal loop (afferent loop syndrome and bile reflux, reduced by a Braun anastomosis); Roux-EN-Y — the best protection against bile reflux.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Actively bleeding duodenal ulcer → adrenaline injection with clips; suspected perforated ulcer → erect X-ray abdomen; “not useful in dumping syndrome” → fewer but larger meals.

**REMEMBER:** *Anterior ulcers perforate, posterior ulcers bleed — the wall behind decides the emergency.*

### 25. Endoscopy, GIST and Oesophageal Cancer Palliation

**PEARL:** Upper GI endoscopy visualises to the second part of the duodenum, IS the GOLD standard for mucosal disease and allows variceal banding — but IT IS poor for diagnosing A GIST, which is a submucosal lesion requiring endoscopic ultrasound (with biopsy) or CT. The commonest complication of oesophageal stenting for palliation IS stent migration.

**understand:** The GIST point illustrates a general principle: the scope sees only the mucosa. A submucosal tumour appears as a smooth bulge with normal overlying mucosa, so ordinary forceps biopsies are usually negative and EUS — which images the wall in layers and permits fine-needle aspiration — is the correct tool. The same reasoning explains why EUS, not endoscopy, provides the T stage in oesophageal and gastric cancer.

**MUST KNOW:**

- GIST: arises from the interstitial cells of Cajal; c-kit (CD117) and DOG1 positive; graded by size and mitotic rate; treated by resection without routine lymphadenectomy (spread is haematogenous), with IMATINIB for high-risk, unresectable or metastatic disease. Biopsy of A resectable GIST IS avoided — risk of rupture and seeding.
- Oesophageal cancer palliation: self-expanding metal stents for dysphagia (complications — migration, tumour ingrowth/overgrowth, pain, perforation, reflux with a stent across the GOJ), plus radiotherapy/brachytherapy, laser or argon plasma ablation, and nutritional support.
- Endoscopic adjuncts: narrow band imaging uses filtered blue-green light to enhance mucosal vasculature and detect dysplasia; CHROMOENDOSCOPY uses dyes — Lugol’s iodine for squamous dysplasia leaves abnormal mucosa unstained; also methylene blue, acetic acid, indigo carmine. Indocyanine green fluorescence assesses perfusion and maps lymphatics intraoperatively.
- Therapeutic capability: haemostasis, variceal banding, polypectomy, EMR/ESD, stenting, dilatation, peg placement, foreign body retrieval.
- Complications of diagnostic endoscopy: perforation, bleeding, aspiration and sedation-related cardiorespiratory events.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Incorrect about the upper GI endoscope” → that it is useful for diagnosing GIST; commonest complication of oesophageal stenting → migration.

**REMEMBER:** *The scope sees mucosa only — a submucosal lump needs ultrasound, not forceps.*

### 26. Gastric Cancer

**PEARL:** The commonest site IS the antrum worldwide, but the proximal stomach IN Western countries (rising with GERD and obesity, while distal cancers fall with *H. Pylori* eradication). D1 lymphadenectomy removes perigastric nodes (stations 1–6); D2 adds the nodes along the major arterial trunks (7–11), so D2 = stations 1–11. Siewert type II — from 1 cm above to 2 cm below the gastro-oesophageal junction — IS the true junctional tumour (type I: 1–5 cm above; type III: 2–5 cm below). Dukes staging was never used for stomach cancer — it is colorectal.

**UNDERSTAND:** The east–west difference in nodal surgery makes sense once you see what D2 adds: gastric cancer spreads early along lymphatics accompanying the coeliac axis branches, so removing only perigastric nodes understages and under-treats. D2 was pioneered in Japan and initially showed higher mortality in Western trials only because it included splenectomy and distal pancreatectomy — with those omitted, spleen- and pancreas-preserving D2 IS now the global standard.

**MUST KNOW:**

- T staging: T1 mucosa/submucosa, T2 muscularis propria, T3 subserosa, T4 perforates serosa (T4a) or invades adjacent structures (T4b). RETROPANCREATIC, mesenteric and para-aortic nodes are NON-regional and count AS M1.
- Gastric polyps: metaplastic/hyperplastic — associated with *H. Pylori* and chronic gastritis (regress after eradication); fundic gland — PPI use and FAP; adenomatous — genuine malignant potential. IT IS the fundic gland polyp, not the hyperplastic polyp, that IS linked to FAP.
- Gastric lymphoma: the commonest extranodal lymphoma site; B-cell, derived from MALT, arising most often in the antrum (not the fundus); NON-Hodgkin. Early low-grade MALToma regresses with *H. Pylori* eradication alone (unless t(11;18)-positive).
- Risk factors: *H. Pylori*, atrophic gastritis and pernicious anaemia, smoked/salted food and nitrosamines, smoking, blood group A, previous gastric surgery, MÉNÉTRIER disease, hereditary diffuse gastric cancer (CDH1).
- LAUREN classification: intestinal (type I) — environmental, arising in gastric atrophy and intestinal metaplasia; MEN > women, older, gland-forming, well differentiated, haematogenous spread; APC mutations and microsatellite instability. Diffuse (type II) — familial, blood group A, women > MEN, younger, signet ring cells, poorly differentiated, transmural and lymphatic spread, loss of E-cadherin.
- Early gastric cancer (confined to mucosa/submucosa regardless of nodes) has >90% five-year survival and may be treated by endoscopic submucosal dissection — the reason Japanese screening matters.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Commonest site in Western countries → proximal stomach; D2 → stations 1–11; true GE junction tumour → Siewert type II; “inaccurate about gastric TNM” → that Dukes staging was used; “incorrect about gastric polyps” → that metaplastic polyps are strongly associated with FAP; “incorrect about gastric lymphoma” → that the fundus is the commonest site.

**REMEMBER:** *Perigastric nodes are D1; add the arterial trunks and you have D2.*

### 27. Upper GI Bleeding Scores

**PEARL:** The Rockall score uses age, shock (systolic BP and pulse), comorbidity, endoscopic diagnosis and stigmata of recent haemorrhage — IT does not include haemoglobin. The Glasgow–Blatchford score does include haemoglobin and urea but NO endoscopic findings.

**UNDERSTAND:** The two scores answer different questions at different moments. Blatchford IS A pre-endoscopy triage tool — computed from bedside observations and blood tests, it identifies the very-low-risk patient (score 0–1) who can be discharged for outpatient endoscopy. Rockall IS A post-endoscopy prognostic tool — it predicts rebleeding and mortality, and therefore requires endoscopic information. Choosing the wrong one is choosing the wrong question.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Haemoglobin IS A poor early marker of acute blood loss — it falls only after haemodilution. A normal haemoglobin never excludes A major bleed IN the first hours; resuscitation is guided by pulse, blood pressure, urine output and lactate.
- Management: resuscitate first (two wide-bore cannulae, cross-match, restrictive transfusion to ~7–8 g/dL), correct coagulopathy, give a PPI, and add terlipressin and prophylactic antibiotics IF variceal bleeding IS suspected. Endoscopy within 24 hours (within 12 hours if unstable or variceal).
- Restrictive transfusion improves survival, particularly IN cirrhosis, because over-transfusion raises portal pressure and provokes rebleeding.
- Variceal bleeding: band ligation first-line (sclerotherapy if not feasible); balloon tamponade is a temporary bridge; tipss is the rescue for uncontrolled or early rebleeding; secondary prophylaxis is banding plus a non-selective beta-blocker.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “not included in the Rockall score” → haemoglobin.

**REMEMBER:** *Blatchford before the scope (bloods); Rockall after it (findings).*

## Small Intestine and Appendix

### 28. Acute Appendicitis

**PEARL:** Luminal obstruction (faecolith, lymphoid hyperplasia, stricture, or *Enterobius VERMICULARIS*) IS the initiating event. The organisms are mixed aerobic and anaerobic (*E. Coli* and *Bacteroides FRAGILIS*) — not purely anaerobic. Murphy’s triad = periumbilical pain migrating to the right iliac fossa, vomiting, and (low-grade) fever — guarding and rigidity are not part of IT. Risk factors for perforation: faecolith, extremes of age, immunosuppression, delayed presentation — a retrocaecal position is not one (it causes atypical, milder signs, but is not itself a perforation risk).

**understand:** The classic pain migration is a lesson in innervation: early distension produces visceral pain referred to the T10 dermatome (periumbilical), and only when inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum does the pain become somatic, sharp and localised at McBurney’s point. The natural history follows the obstruction: distension → venous congestion → mucosal ischaemia → bacterial invasion → gangrene → perforation, typically within 24–72 hours.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Signs: McBURNEY’S point tenderness, ROVSING’S sign, psoas sign (retrocaecal), obturator sign (pelvic), rebound tenderness. ALVARADO (“MANTRELS”) score aids decision-making; ultrasound is first-line in children and pregnancy, CT in adults (highest accuracy), MRI in pregnancy where available.
- Postoperative complications by timing: wound infection days 4–5; intra-abdominal/pelvic abscess around day 5–7 with spiking fever, malaise and diarrhoea (a pelvic abscess irritates the rectum); portal PYAEMIA (rare — jaundice, rigors, liver abscesses); faecal fistula; later adhesive obstruction.
- Appendiceal MASS (a phlegmon after 3–5 days) IS managed conservatively (Ochsner–SHERREN regimen) with antibiotics and interval appendicectomy or colonic evaluation; an abscess IS drained.
- Antibiotics-first is now an accepted alternative in uncomplicated appendicitis (CODA trial): non-inferior to appendicectomy for 30-day outcomes, but ~40% come to appendicectomy within 4 years, and an appendicolith strongly predicts failure — so a faecolith, perforation, abscess or sepsis still means surgery.[^2]
- Appendiceal neoplasms: neuroendocrine (carcinoid) tumours are commonest and usually incidental. Size IS the best predictor: <1 cm at the tip — appendicectomy suffices; >2 cm, or base involvement, mesoappendiceal invasion or nodal disease — right hemicolectomy; 1–2 cm — individualised. Mucinous neoplasms can cause pseudomyxoma peritonei.
- Eponymous abdominal signs: BLUMBERG = rebound tenderness (peritoneal irritation); ROVSING = left iliac fossa pressure causes right iliac fossa pain; Murphy = arrest of inspiration on RUQ palpation (cholecystitis, not appendicitis); psoas and obturator = retrocaecal and pelvic appendix respectively.
- IN pregnancy, appendicitis IS the commonest NON-obstetric surgical emergency, the appendix is displaced upward, and delay is far more dangerous to the fetus than surgery.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “not true about aetiology” → that the organisms are anaerobic; “not part of Murphy’s triad” → guarding and rigidity; “not a perforation risk” → retrocaecal position; spiking fever with diarrhoea on day 6 → pelvic abscess.

**REMEMBER:** *Obstruction starts it, visceral pain announces it, parietal pain localises it.*

### 29. Meckel’s Diverticulum

**PEARL:** A true diverticulum (containing ALL three layers) — a persistent remnant of the VITELLO-intestinal duct on the antimesenteric border of the ileum. About 20% contain ectopic gastric (commonest) or pancreatic mucosa. IN children the commonest presentation IS painless per-rectal bleeding, not an appendicitis-like picture (diverticulitis mimicking appendicitis is the adult presentation). A Tc-99m pertechnetate scan detects the ectopic gastric mucosa.

**UNDERSTAND:** The ectopic gastric mucosa IS the key to everything. It secretes acid onto adjacent ileal mucosa that has no defence against it, causing peptic ulceration and painless bleeding; it is also what makes the diverticulum visible on a pertechnetate scan, since the tracer is taken up by gastric mucosa wherever it lies. A diverticulum without gastric mucosa neither bleeds nor shows on the scan — which is why a negative scan does not exclude a Meckel’s causing obstruction.

**MUST KNOW:**

- The “rule of 2s”: 2% of the population, 2 feet (60 cm) from the ileocaecal valve, 2 inches long, 2 types of ectopic mucosa, symptomatic usually before age 2, male:female 2:1.
- Complications in order: haemorrhage (children); intestinal obstruction via a persistent fibrous band, volvulus or intussusception (the commonest lead point in children, and the commonest complication in adults); diverticulitis and perforation; rarely neoplasia (carcinoid).
- Treatment: DIVERTICULECTOMY or wedge resection — segmental resection and anastomosis if the base is broad, the ulcer is in the adjacent ileum, or there is perforation. Resect any symptomatic diverticulum; incidental resection is considered in the young, with a narrow base, palpable ectopic tissue, or a long diverticulum — an incidental broad-based, non-thickened diverticulum may be left alone.
- Other vitello-intestinal duct anomalies: umbilical sinus, fistula (faecal discharge from the umbilicus), a fibrous band, enterocystoma.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Incorrect about Meckel’s” → that it is a false diverticulum, or that in children it presents mimicking appendicitis (it presents with painless bleeding).

**REMEMBER:** *Gastric mucosa in the wrong place — it bleeds, and it glows on the scan.*

### 30. Intestinal Obstruction — Principles, Radiology and Strangulation

**PEARL:** Colicky pain IS the earliest symptom, followed by vomiting, distension and absolute constipation. The 3-6-9 rule: small bowel <3 cm, large bowel <6 cm, caecum <9 cm (and appendix <6 mm) — dilatation beyond these is significant. The commonest cause of small bowel obstruction IS adhesions (then hernia, then malignancy); of large bowel obstruction IT IS carcinoma.

**UNDERSTAND:** The symptom sequence is dictated by where the obstruction is. High (proximal): early, profuse vomiting with rapid dehydration and minimal distension. Low (distal): pain and distension predominate, with late, feculent vomiting.

**MUST KNOW:**

- X-ray triad of SBO: dilated small bowel loops >3 cm, air–fluid levels on the erect film, and a paucity of gas IN the colon. Small bowel — central loops with valvulae conniventes crossing the full width; large bowel — peripheral loops with haustra that do not cross fully. CT is the investigation of choice — transition point, cause and ischaemia.
- Signs demanding immediate surgery: peritonism, continuous (rather than colicky) pain, tachycardia, fever, leucocytosis, acidosis, a tender irreducible hernia, or CT features of strangulation (bowel wall thickening, poor enhancement, mesenteric fat stranding, free fluid, pneumatosis, portal venous gas).
- Strangulation principles: venous outflow IS occluded before arterial inflow (congestion, then infarction); a closed-loop obstruction strangulates rapidly; Richter’s hernia (only part of the circumference caught) strangulates without complete obstruction — so absolute constipation IS not A feature.
- IN large bowel obstruction with A competent ileocaecal valve IT IS the caecum (thinnest wall, greatest diameter — Laplace’s law), not the splenic flexure, that IS most prone to ischaemia and perforation. Caecal diameter >9–12 cm risks perforation.
- Management: nil by mouth, nasogastric decompression, IV fluids and electrolyte correction, analgesia, catheter, close observation. Water-soluble contrast (Gastrograffin) IS both prognostic and therapeutic in adhesive obstruction — contrast reaching the colon within 24 hours predicts resolution.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** First symptom → colicky pain; significant small bowel dilatation → >3 cm; “incorrect about strangulation” → that the splenic flexure is most prone to ischaemia in LBO; obstipation not a necessary feature → Richter’s hernia.

**REMEMBER:** *Vomit early means high, distend early means low — and the caecum bursts first. 3 small, 6 large, 9 caecum.*

### 31. Adhesive Small Bowel Obstruction

**PEARL:** Colicky pain, bilious vomiting and hyperperistalsis years after laparotomy = adhesive small bowel obstruction. The next step is CECT abdomen, not immediate laparotomy — it identifies the transition point and, critically, signs of strangulation.

**UNDERSTAND:** CT comes first because most adhesive obstructions settle non-operatively, while a strangulating obstruction must be operated on within hours. Only CT separates them: it shows the transition zone, bowel wall thickening, reduced or absent mural enhancement, mesenteric fat stranding, a closed-loop configuration and free fluid. Operating on everyone creates the adhesions that cause the next episode.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Initial management (“drip and suck”): NBM, nasogastric decompression, IV fluids, correct electrolytes, catheterise and monitor output, serial examination.
- Operate immediately for: peritonitis, strangulation/ischaemia on CT, closed-loop obstruction, failure to resolve in 48–72 hours, or a VIRGIN abdomen (no previous surgery → a mechanical cause needing definitive treatment).
- Gastrograffin challenge is both diagnostic and therapeutic — contrast reaching the colon within 24 hours predicts non-operative resolution and speeds it.
- Other causes of SBO: hernia (commonest worldwide and in the un-operated abdomen), malignancy, Crohn’s stricture, intussusception, gallstone ileus, bezoar, TB.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The stem pairs a diagnosis with a next step — the answer must contain both correctly. Adhesive obstruction + CECT, not straight to laparotomy in a stable patient without peritonitis.

**REMEMBER:** *Drip and suck while the CT decides whether to cut.*

### 32. Postoperative Ileus versus Mechanical Obstruction

**PEARL:** Distension with bilious vomiting after starting oral feeds on day 4 and a non-tender abdomen is postoperative ileus (or early adhesive obstruction) → stop orals, insert an NG tube, check electrolytes (especially potassium) and get an abdominal X-ray. Nasojejunal feeding is the wrong answer — you do not feed a gut that is not working.

**UNDERSTAND:** The intolerance of feeding *is* the sign that transit has failed; delivering nutrition beyond the pylorus does nothing about the distended, atonic stomach and small bowel proximal to it, and risks aspiration. The priorities are decompression and correction of the reversible causes — of which hypokalaemia is the most important and most commonly missed, since potassium is essential for smooth-muscle depolarisation.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Normal return of motility: small bowel within hours, stomach 24–48 hours, colon 48–72 hours — ileus is diagnosed when this fails or reverses.
- Causes to correct: hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hyponatraemia, opioids, anticholinergics, sepsis/intra-abdominal collection or anastomotic leak, retroperitoneal haematoma, prolonged bowel handling.
- Ileus vs mechanical obstruction: ileus is painless with absent/quiet bowel sounds and gas distributed throughout including the colon and rectum; mechanical obstruction has colic, high-pitched hyperactive sounds and a GASLESS distal bowel with a transition point on CT.
- Management: NBM, NG decompression, IV fluids, correct electrolytes, stop/minimise opioids (epidural or NSAID-based analgesia), mobilise, and CT if it persists beyond 5–7 days — an unresolving “ileus” after bowel surgery is an anastomotic leak until proven otherwise.
- Prevention (eras): minimally invasive surgery, avoidance of fluid overload, opioid-sparing analgesia, chewing gum, early feeding and early mobilisation.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “All of the following except” — the exception is always the option that continues feeding.

**REMEMBER:** *Decompress and check the potassium — never feed a silent gut.*

### 33. Small Bowel Anatomy, Malrotation and Ileocaecal Tuberculosis

**PEARL:** The jejunum has A wider lumen, thicker wall, longer vasa recta and fewer arcades; the ileum makes up about 60% of small bowel length; the ligament of Treitz marks the duodenojejunal flexure. Ileal resection IS tolerated worse than jejunal resection. IN malrotation the caecum and appendix lie ON the left; Ladd’s procedure divides Ladd’s bands, broadens the mesentery, performs an incidental appendicectomy, and places the small bowel ON the right and the colon ON the left — the caecum IS deliberately left ON the left, not fixed to the right.

**understand:** Ladd’s procedure looks paradoxical until you see its aim: it does not restore normal anatomy — it creates a NON-rotated anatomy with the widest possible mesenteric base, because volvulus happens when the midgut hangs from a narrow pedicle. Broadening that base prevents recurrence; the appendix is removed because it will lie in the left upper quadrant and would confuse any future presentation. Similarly, the ileum is irreplaceable because it alone absorbs B12 and bile salts and contains the ileocaecal valve — the jejunum cannot take over those functions, though the ileum can adapt to jejunal loss.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Short bowel syndrome: ileal resection causes B12 deficiency, bile-salt diarrhoea, steatorrhoea, gallstones and oxalate renal stones. Loss of the ileocaecal valve worsens outcome by allowing bacterial overgrowth and rapid transit.
- Ileocaecal tuberculosis: anti-TUBERCULAR chemotherapy cures most cases. Surgery IS reserved for complete/acute obstruction, perforation, massive bleeding, or an unresolving mass with diagnostic doubt — ulceration, a hyperplastic mass and diarrhoea are all managed medically. Beware paradoxical worsening and stricture formation during treatment.
- ENTEROSCOPY/capsule endoscopy and CT enterography are the modern tools for obscure small bowel disease.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “False about small bowel anatomy” → that ileal resections are better tolerated; “incorrect about malrotation” → that the caecum is fixed to the right; when to operate in ileocaecal TB → acute obstruction.

**REMEMBER:** *Ladd’s does not straighten the gut — it widens the mesentery so it cannot twist.*

### 34. Enterocutaneous Fistula — What Closes and What Doesn’t

**PEARL:** A fistula tract longer than 2.5 cm is likely to close spontaneously; <2.5 cm is not. Everything else in the friend list *prevents* closure.

**UNDERSTAND:** A long tract behaves like a narrow tunnel — flow resistance is high, the tract collapses and granulates. A short tract is effectively a direct stoma to skin, and epithelium creeps across it, permanently lining the communication so it can never granulate shut. This is why epithelialisation is a bar to closure, not an aid.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Friend — factors preventing spontaneous closure: Foreign body, Radiation, Inflammation/IBD, Epithelialisation, Neoplasm, Distal obstruction. (Add: high output, sepsis, short tract, malnutrition.)
- Output: high >500 mL/day, intermediate 200–500, low <200 mL/day. High output = less likely to close, more electrolyte loss.
- A duodenal fistula causes the most malnutrition — proximal, *plus* it loses an extra 1–2 L/day of pancreatic and biliary secretions on top of gastric losses. Distal ileal and colonic fistulae are easiest to manage.
- Snap management: Sepsis control + Skin protection → Nutrition (TPN) → Anatomy (fistulogram/CT) → Planned definitive surgery.
- Wait 6 weeks–3 months before definitive surgery; octreotide reduces output but does not improve closure rates.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Either “which factor aids spontaneous closure” (answer: the long tract — everything else listed is a friend) or “which fistula causes maximum malnutrition” (duodenal).

**REMEMBER:** *Long tract closes; short, lined, fed-by-a-foreign-body tract never will.*

### 35. Superior Mesenteric Artery (Wilkie) Syndrome

**PEARL:** Compression of the third part of the duodenum between the SMA and the aorta when the aortomesenteric angle narrows to less than 25° (normal 38–65°) — so “angle more than 70 degrees” is the false statement. Treatment is nutritional restoration first; DUODENOJEJUNOSTOMY if that fails.

**UNDERSTAND:** The duodenum’s third part is the only bowel passing through a fixed bony-vascular tunnel, and what holds that tunnel open is the retroperitoneal fat PAD at the SMA origin. Anything that strips that fat — profound weight loss, cachexia, malabsorption, burns, anorexia nervosa — collapses the angle onto the duodenum, creating a vicious cycle: obstruction → vomiting → further weight loss → a narrower angle. Restoring the fat pad by feeding beyond the obstruction reverses the mechanism, which is why nutrition is the treatment, not merely supportive care.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Presentation: postprandial epigastric pain and bilious vomiting, early satiety, weight loss — classically relieved by the prone, knee–chest or left lateral position (which lets the SMA fall away from the aorta).
- Precipitants: adolescent growth spurt, severe weight loss of any cause, prolonged bed rest, spinal surgery or a body cast for scoliosis, and after bariatric surgery.
- Investigation of choice: CT angiography with the aortomesenteric angle and distance (normal 10–28 mm; SMA syndrome <8 mm); barium shows an abrupt vertical cut-off in the third part with to-and-fro peristalsis.
- DUODENOJEJUNOSTOMY is the operation of choice (better than gastrojejunostomy or Strong’s procedure).
- Related compression syndromes: nutcracker (left renal vein between SMA and aorta — haematuria, flank pain, varicocele); median arcuate ligament (coeliac artery — postprandial pain, epigastric bruit); may–THURNER (left common iliac vein compressed by the right common iliac artery → left-sided DVT).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Which is incorrect?” — the false option inverts the angle. Anchor the number: narrow (<25°) obstructs.

**REMEMBER:** *Lose the fat pad, close the angle, trap the duodenum.*

## Colon, Rectum and Anal Canal

### 36. Familial Adenomatous Polyposis and Lynch Syndrome

**PEARL:** The first-line screening test for an AT-risk child IN an FAP family IS genetic testing for the APC mutation (from age 10–12), with annual flexible sigmoidoscopy if positive or if testing is unavailable. Extracolonic manifestations of FAP: duodenal/periampullary adenomas and carcinoma, gastric fundic gland polyps, hepatoblastoma, thyroid carcinoma, epidermoid cysts, pilomatrixoma, CHRPE, desmoid tumours, osteomas and dental anomalies — renal cell carcinoma IS not one. In Gardner syndrome the commonest facial abnormality is multiple osteomas (mandible commonest). The Amsterdam II criteria (for Lynch, not FAP) require AT least 2 successive generations affected.

**UNDERSTAND:** Screening an FAP family starts with genetics rather than endoscopy because a negative APC test in a family with a known mutation completely releases that child from a lifetime of colonoscopies, while a positive test justifies surveillance in childhood and planning prophylactic colectomy in the teens or twenties — before cancer, which is otherwise inevitable (100% by age 40). Genetic testing is thus not academic but the decision that shapes the child’s next twenty years.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Amsterdam II (“3-2-1”) criteria: ≥3 relatives with a Lynch-associated cancer (colorectal, endometrial, small bowel, ureter or renal pelvis), one a first-degree relative of the other two; ≥2 successive generations; ≥1 diagnosed before age 50; FAP excluded; tumours verified pathologically (not radiologically). The Bethesda guidelines determine who gets tumour MSI/IHC testing.
- FAP is autosomal dominant, APC on chromosome 5q21. Variants: Gardner (osteomas, desmoids, epidermoid cysts) and Turcot (CNS tumours). CHRPE is an early clue.
- Surgery: PROCTOCOLECTOMY with ileal pouch–anal anastomosis (or colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis if rectal polyps are few, with continued rectal surveillance). Desmoid tumours and periampullary carcinoma become the leading causes of death after colectomy — upper GI surveillance continues lifelong.
- The adenoma–carcinoma sequence: APC loss initiates the adenoma (earliest event); KRAS activation drives its growth; SMAD4/DCC loss and — crucially — p53 mutation marks the transition from adenoma to invasive carcinoma.
- Lynch syndrome (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, EPCAM): right-SIDED, poorly differentiated/mucinous tumours, synchronous and metachronous cancers, endometrial cancer in women, and MSI-high tumours that respond exceptionally to checkpoint inhibitors.
- MUTYH-associated polyposis is the autosomal recessive mimic of attenuated FAP.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Screening children in an FAP family → APC genetic testing; “not an extracolonic manifestation” → renal cell carcinoma; commonest facial abnormality in Gardner → multiple osteomas; “correct about Amsterdam II” → at least 2 successive generations; marker of transition to invasive carcinoma → p53.

**REMEMBER:** *Test the gene first — a negative result frees the child, a positive one starts the clock.*

### 38. Colorectal Cancer — Screening, Presentation and Spread

**PEARL:** Colonoscopy IS the GOLD-standard diagnostic test but not the population screening test — screening is by faecal IMMUNOCHEMICAL testing (fit)/faecal occult blood, with colonoscopy for positives. Screening now starts AT 45, not 50 (average risk, to age 75).[^3] the most important prognostic determinant IS tumour stage, especially lymph node status. The earliest and commonest symptom of rectal cancer IS painless bleeding per rectum. Rectal cancer drains upward to the mesenteric/para-aortic nodes and does not normally go to the inguinal nodes.

**UNDERSTAND:** The screening test and the diagnostic test answer different questions. A screening test must be cheap, safe, acceptable and applicable to millions of asymptomatic people — hence fit. Colonoscopy is the best *diagnostic* and therapeutic tool (it both finds and removes adenomas) but is invasive, expensive and carries a perforation risk, so it is reserved for symptomatic patients, positive screens and high-risk groups. The nodal question is settled by embryology: the watershed is the dentate line — above it drains upward along the superior rectal vessels; below it (anal canal, penis/scrotum, lower limb) drains to the superficial inguinal nodes.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Presentation by site: right-SIDED — iron-deficiency anaemia, weight loss, a vague mass (the caecum is capacious and stool liquid, so obstruction is late); left-SIDED — altered bowel habit, obstruction, tenesmus and bleeding. Any iron-deficiency anaemia IN A man OR postmenopausal woman needs colonic evaluation.
- Neoplasm IS the commonest cause of large bowel obstruction IN adults (adhesions are commonest for small bowel); volvulus, diverticular stricture and hernia follow.
- Liver metastases may be resectable with ~40% 5-year survival. Modern protocols do permit mechanical bowel preparation with oral antibiotics, which reduces surgical site infection, anastomotic leak and reoperation.
- Treatment: right/left hemicolectomy or anterior resection with total mesorectal excision; ABDOMINOPERINEAL resection for very low rectal tumours; neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy for locally advanced rectal cancer; adjuvant chemotherapy (FOLFOX) for node-positive disease; metastasectomy for resectable liver or lung disease. CEA is for follow-up, not diagnosis.
- Proctoscopy visualises the anal canal, anorectal junction and lower rectum (~10 cm); the rigid sigmoidoscope reaches ~25 cm, the flexible sigmoidoscope 60 cm.
- Current screening menu (ACS 2026): preferred — annual fit, multitarget stool DNA every 3 years, the new multitarget stool RNA test, colonoscopy every 10 years, CT colonography or flexible sigmoidoscopy every 5 years. Blood-based cell-free DNA testing (Shield) is now an option but is not preferred — it misses advanced precancerous lesions and stage I cancers, and a positive result still mandates colonoscopy.[^3]
- Colonic nodal groups, in order: EPICOLIC (on the bowel wall) → paracolic (along the marginal artery of Drummond, the anastomotic vessel running along the mesenteric border) → intermediate (along the named colic arteries) → principal/PREAORTIC (at the origin of the SMA/IMA).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Incorrect about colorectal cancer” → that colonoscopy is the screening test of choice; commonest cause of colonic obstruction → neoplasm; least likely to cause inguinal lymphadenopathy → rectum; earliest symptom of rectal cancer → bleeding per rectum; length seen at proctoscopy → 10 cm.

**REMEMBER:** *Screen the population with stool, investigate the patient with a scope. Above the dentate line drains up; below it drains to the groin.*

### 39. Rectal Cancer — Staging and Operations

**PEARL:** Dukes staging: A — confined to the bowel wall (>90% 5-year survival); B — through the wall into extra-rectal tissue but node-negative (~70%); C — regional nodes involved (C1 pararectal only, C2 up to the vessel origin, ~40%); D — distant metastases. Hartmann’s operation (resection with an end colostomy and closure of the rectal stump) IS used when an anastomosis IS unsafe OR sphincter function IS poor — not when sphincter function is good.

**UNDERSTAND:** The whole surgical decision is how far the tumour IS from the anal sphincter. If a distal margin can be obtained while preserving the sphincter complex, perform an anterior resection with a stapled colorectal or coloanal anastomosis (usually with a defunctioning ileostomy). If not, the sphincter must be sacrificed — APR with a permanent colostomy. Hartmann’s sits between them: a resection performed when the patient is too unwell, obstructed or contaminated for an anastomosis to heal.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Total mesorectal excision (TME) IS the oncological standard — sharp dissection along the mesorectal fascial plane, which reduced local recurrence from ~30% to <10%. MRI pelvis defines the circumferential resection margin and selects patients for neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy.
- ENDOLUMINAL stenting relieves obstruction as a bridge to surgery or for palliation.
- Postoperative issues: anastomotic leak (the most feared — fever, tachycardia, ileus and pain around days 5–7), low anterior resection syndrome (urgency, frequency, clustering), and stoma complications.
- The modern staging system is TNM; Dukes remains examined because it encapsulates the prognostic logic — wall, then nodes, then distant spread.
- “Watch and wait” is now an option after a complete clinical response to chemoradiotherapy in selected rectal cancers.
- Surveillance after resection: colonoscopy at 1 year, CEA and CT for 5 years.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Tumour through the wall without nodes → Dukes B; “false about rectal cancer surgery” → that Hartmann’s is used when sphincter function is good.

**REMEMBER:** *Distance from the sphincter decides the operation; nodes decide the prognosis.*

### 40. Inflammatory Bowel Disease — Surgical Indications

**PEARL:** Indications for surgery IN ulcerative colitis: failure of medical therapy/steroid dependence, acute severe colitis or toxic megacolon, perforation, uncontrollable haemorrhage, dysplasia OR carcinoma, growth retardation in children, and intractable extra-intestinal manifestations — but not simply having left-SIDED colitis, which is a disease distribution, not an indication. The commonest extra-intestinal manifestation of UC IS arthropathy (~15%). In Crohn disease, symptomatic relapse after resection is not related to whether disease was present at the resection margins.

**UNDERSTAND:** Colectomy cures ulcerative colitis but not Crohn disease — UC is confined to the colon and rectum, whereas Crohn is a panintestinal, transmural immunological disorder that can recur anywhere. That single fact governs surgical philosophy: in UC surgery is definitive and even prophylactic (against cancer); in Crohn it is reserved for complications and is AS conservative AS possible, because wider margins buy nothing while repeated resections cause short bowel syndrome.

**MUST KNOW:**

- UC surgery: total PROCTOCOLECTOMY with ileal pouch–anal anastomosis is the standard elective operation; in the acute severe/toxic setting, SUBTOTAL colectomy with end ileostomy is safest, with reconstruction later. POUCHITIS is the commonest late complication (metronidazole/ciprofloxacin).
- Extra-intestinal manifestations that track disease activity: peripheral arthropathy, erythema nodosum, episcleritis, aphthous ulcers. Those that do not: ankylosing spondylitis/sacroiliitis (HLA-B27), primary sclerosing cholangitis, uveitis, pyoderma gangrenosum. PSC runs an independent course, persists after colectomy, carries cholangiocarcinoma risk and multiplies the colorectal cancer risk — surveillance is annual from diagnosis.
- Acute severe colitis: TRUELOVE and WITTS criteria; IV steroids, VTE prophylaxis, daily review; assess response at day 3 (TRAVIS criteria) and rescue with infliximab or ciclosporin — surgery IF NO response by day 5–7. Delaying colectomy IN A deteriorating patient IS the classic avoidable cause of death.
- Toxic megacolon: transverse colon >6 cm with systemic toxicity — avoid antimotility agents, opioids and colonoscopy.
- Crohn: the terminal ileum is the commonest site; discontinuous “skip lesions”; transmural inflammation, non-caseating granulomas, cobblestone mucosa, deep linear and aphthous ulcers, fat wrapping, fistulae, fissures, strictures and perianal disease. Surgery for stricture, fistula, abscess, perforation, haemorrhage or failure of medical therapy — with STRICTUROPLASTY preferred over resection. A Crohn’s abscess needs drainage plus antibiotics — antibiotics alone are insufficient. Smoking worsens Crohn but protects against UC.
- UC contrast: mucosal, continuous from the rectum, crypt abscesses and pseudopolyps.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “not an indication for surgery in UC” → left-sided colitis; commonest extra-intestinal manifestation → arthropathy. In the Crohn stem, three options state the opposite of a true fact (proximal ileum, continuous lesions, antibiotics alone) — the answer is the counter-intuitive one about margins.

**REMEMBER:** *Colectomy cures colitis but never cures Crohn — so cut generously in one and sparingly in the other.*

### 41. Diverticular Disease and Lower GI Bleeding

**PEARL:** Hinchey classification of perforated diverticulitis: I — pericolic/mesenteric abscess; II — pelvic or retroperitoneal abscess; III — generalised purulent peritonitis; IV — generalised faecal peritonitis. DIVERTICULOSIS IS the commonest cause of massive lower GI bleeding. In a patient over 60 with painless, sometimes massive lower GI bleeding and a recent normal screen, colonoscopy showing dilated tortuous mucosal and submucosal veins = angiodysplasia (classically right-sided; associated with aortic stenosis — HEYDE syndrome).

**UNDERSTAND:** Hinchey grading exists because it dictates the operation: I–II are managed with antibiotics ± percutaneous drainage; III–IV require surgery — traditionally Hartmann’s procedure, with primary anastomosis ± diverting ileostomy or laparoscopic lavage in selected cases. The purulent/faecal distinction matters because faecal contamination carries much higher mortality and effectively mandates resection.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Colonic diverticula are acquired false (pulsion) diverticula — mucosa and submucosa herniating where the vasa recta penetrate the muscle (hence brisk bleeding). Commonest in the sigmoid in Western populations; right-SIDED (and often true, congenital) in Asians. The rectum IS spared because its outer muscle coat is complete. Caused by a low-fibre diet and raised intraluminal pressure. Meckel’s is the classic true diverticulum.
- Colonoscopy IS contraindicated IN acute diverticulitis (perforation risk) — CT IS the investigation of choice; colonoscopy follows 6–8 weeks later to exclude carcinoma.
- Approach to lower GI bleeding: resuscitate → exclude an upper GI source (nasogastric aspirate or upper endoscopy — brisk upper bleeding can present as fresh red blood) → colonoscopy when stable. For ongoing bleeding: CT angiography (detects ≥0.3 mL/min) then catheter angiography with embolisation; a nuclear-medicine RBC scan for slow intermittent bleeding; surgery last.
- Causes by age: children — Meckel’s, polyps, intussusception; adults — haemorrhoids, fissure, IBD, infective colitis; elderly — DIVERTICULOSIS, angiodysplasia, ischaemic colitis, malignancy.
- Haemorrhoids and fissure are the commonest causes overall — but never attribute rectal bleeding to them IN an older patient without excluding cancer.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Faecal peritonitis from diverticular perforation → Hinchey stage 4; syncope with painless rectal bleeding and a recent normal screen → angiodysplasia.

**REMEMBER:** *Hinchey tells you whether to drain it or resect it. False, acquired, sigmoid — and the rectum is spared.*

### 42. Volvulus

**PEARL:** Caecal volvulus: commoner in women in the fourth-to-fifth decades, a CLOCKWISE twist, “bird-beak” tapering on contrast, and a dilated loop pointing to the left upper quadrant. Caecopexy has high recurrence — right hemicolectomy (or caecostomy in the unfit) IS preferred. Sigmoid volvulus gives the “coffee-bean”/inverted-U loop arising from the pelvis and pointing to the right upper quadrant.

**UNDERSTAND:** Volvulus needs a long mobile loop on a narrow mesenteric base, which is why the sigmoid (chronic constipation, high-fibre diets, Chagas disease, institutionalised elderly) and a congenitally unfixed caecum are the two sites. The therapeutic difference follows anatomy: the sigmoid can be decompressed from below by flexible sigmoidoscopy and a flatus tube, while the caecum cannot be reached — so caecal volvulus is a primarily surgical problem.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Sigmoid volvulus: endoscopic detorsion succeeds in 70–90% and is first-line if there is no peritonitis or gangrene — but recurrence IS ~50%, so elective sigmoid resection IS offered during the same admission. Gangrene, peritonitis, perforation OR failed decompression mean immediate laparotomy (Hartmann’s).
- Bloody effluent OR necrotic mucosa seen AT endoscopic detorsion IS an absolute indication to STOP and operate.
- Other volvuli: gastric (BORCHARDT’S triad — unproductive retching, epigastric pain, inability to pass a nasogastric tube; organoaxial or mesenteroaxial); midgut volvulus in the neonate (malrotation — a surgical emergency); ILEOSIGMOID knotting (a rapidly gangrenous double volvulus, notably reported from India and Africa).
- Volvulus is a closed-loop obstruction and therefore strangulates rapidly — which is why delay is so costly.
- Sigmoid volvulus IS A substantially commoner cause of large bowel obstruction IN India and Africa than IN the west — think of it in an elderly patient with gross distension and few air–fluid levels.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Incorrect about caecal volvulus” → that recurrence rates are low with caecopexy.

**REMEMBER:** *A sigmoid volvulus can be untwisted from below; a caecal one has to be resected.*

### 43. Acute Colonic Pseudo-Obstruction (Ogilvie Syndrome)

**PEARL:** Risk factors are the conditions that reduce colonic motility: recent surgery or trauma, opioids, and psychiatric illness (anticholinergic drugs) — plus electrolyte disturbance, immobility, sepsis and severe medical illness. Adhesions cause mechanical obstruction and fasting causes ileus — neither causes pseudo-obstruction. Treatment is IV neostigmine, not a knife.

**UNDERSTAND:** Ogilvie syndrome is an autonomic imbalance — excess sympathetic and deficient parasympathetic (sacral) input to the distal colon — so the colon dilates without any mechanical block. That is why the transition point is typically at the splenic flexure or descending colon, the watershed where vagal supply ends and sacral supply begins, and why the treatment is a parasympathomimetic: neostigmine is an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor that restores propulsive activity.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Presentation: progressive distension with minimal pain and often preserved flatus, in a medically ill or postoperative patient. X-ray shows massive colonic dilatation with gas extending to the rectum; a water-soluble contrast enema or CT is the key investigation to exclude mechanical obstruction.
- Caecal diameter >12 cm (or a rapid increase) predicts ischaemia and perforation — Laplace’s law again.
- Management ladder: (1) conservative — NBM, nasogastric and flatus tube, correct electrolytes (especially potassium and magnesium), stop opioids and anticholinergics, mobilise; (2) IV neostigmine 2 mg with cardiac monitoring and atropine at hand (bradycardia and asystole risk; contraindicated in mechanical obstruction, asthma, bradycardia); (3) COLONOSCOPIC decompression; (4) surgery (caecostomy/colectomy) for perforation or ischaemia.
- Differentiate from mechanical obstruction (a transition point with collapsed distal bowel on CT) and from toxic megacolon (systemic toxicity with colitis).
- Chronic intestinal pseudo-obstruction is a different entity — a myopathy or neuropathy of the gut (scleroderma, amyloid, paraneoplastic, mitochondrial).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Multi-select of risk factors — include the motility-suppressing ones, exclude the mechanical ones (adhesions).

**REMEMBER:** *The colon is paralysed, not blocked — give neostigmine, but only after excluding a real blockage.*

### 44. Benign Anorectal Disease — Fissure, Abscess and Haemorrhoids

**PEARL:** Severe pain during and after defecation with bright blood Streaking the stool and marked anal spasm = fissure-IN-ANO (posterior midline in 90%). The cryptoglandular theory explains anorectal abscess — infection begins in an intersphincteric anal gland — and the commonest type IS perianal (~60%), then ischiorectal (~30%); drain by cruciate incision under anaesthesia. Urinary retention IS the commonest complication after haemorrhoidectomy (15–30%).

**UNDERSTAND:** Anal fissure is a self-perpetuating ischaemic ulcer: pain causes internal sphincter spasm, spasm reduces blood flow in the posterior midline — the least perfused part of the anal canal — and poor perfusion prevents healing, which causes more pain. That is why treatment targets the spasm rather than the ulcer: stool softeners and fibre, plus topical GTN or diltiazem (chemical sphincterotomy), botulinum toxin, and finally lateral internal sphincterotomy (definitive, with a small incontinence risk).

**MUST KNOW:**

- A fissure that IS lateral, multiple OR painless IS not A simple fissure — think Crohn disease, tuberculosis, syphilis, HIV OR malignancy.
- Do not delay abscess drainage for antibiotics or “ripening” — drainage IS the treatment. Look for diabetes or immunosuppression; the feared progression is Fournier’s gangrene (necrotising fasciitis needing immediate radical debridement). About one-third later develop a fistula-in-ano.
- Haemorrhoids: internal (above the dentate line, painless bleeding, graded I–IV) versus external (below, painful when thrombosed). Treat with fibre and fluids, rubber-band ligation or sclerotherapy for grades I–II, and haemorrhoidectomy or stapled/Doppler-guided procedures for III–IV.
- Post-haemorrhoidectomy: urinary retention is driven by pain, pelvic floor spasm, spinal anaesthesia and excess perioperative IV fluids — manage with catheterisation and good pain control. Other complications: pain (universal), reactionary/secondary haemorrhage (day 7–10), anal stenosis, incontinence.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Pain on defecation with anal spasm → fissure-in-ano; “incorrect about anorectal abscess” → that ischiorectal is the commonest type; commonest complication after haemorrhoidectomy → urinary retention.

**REMEMBER:** *Spasm starves the posterior midline — relax the sphincter and the fissure heals. Perianal beats ischiorectal; cut it open, don’t wait.*

### 45. Fistula-in-Ano: Simple versus Complex

**PEARL:** A single, posterior, intersphincteric (low) fistula with no secondary extensions is a simple fistula → fistulotomy (lay it open). Complex fistulae — high trans-sphincteric, suprasphincteric, extrasphincteric, anterior in a woman, multiple tracts, Crohn-related, or with prior incontinence — require sphincter-preserving procedures (lift, advancement flap, SETON).

**UNDERSTAND:** The whole decision is how much external sphincter the tract crosses. Fistulotomy is the most effective operation (>90% cure) precisely because it removes the tract completely — but it necessarily divides whatever muscle lies over it. Dividing a small amount of distal internal sphincter is safe; dividing a substantial part of the external sphincter causes incontinence. So the operation is chosen by asking how much muscle is at risk — which is why the tract is mapped first.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Parks classification: intersphincteric (~70%, commonest), trans-sphincteric, supra-sphincteric, extra-sphincteric.
- GOODSALL’S rule: an external opening anterior to the transverse anal line tracks in a straight radial line to the nearest crypt; a posterior opening curves to the posterior midline (horseshoe).
- Origin: cryptoglandular infection of the anal glands in the intersphincteric plane — a fistula is the chronic phase of an abscess.
- Sphincter-preserving options: lift (Ligation of the Intersphincteric Fistula Tract), endorectal advancement flap, SETON (cutting or loose/draining), fibrin glue and fistula plug (low success), VAAFT/laser ablation. A draining SETON is the safe first move in sepsis and in Crohn disease.
- Always consider Crohn, tuberculosis, malignancy and HIV in a complex, recurrent or anterior fistula — treat the underlying disease (anti-TNF agents are highly effective in fistulising Crohn) and avoid aggressive surgery, which heals poorly.
- MRI is the imaging gold standard; examination under anaesthesia remains the definitive assessment.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The stem states the tract type and number of openings. Simple and low → fistulotomy; anything high or multiple → a sphincter-sparing option.

**REMEMBER:** *Lay open what crosses little muscle; preserve the sphincter for everything else.*

### 46. Rectal Prolapse

**PEARL:** RIPSTEIN’S procedure is an abdominal operation (anterior mesh rectopexy). The perineal approaches are DELORME’S mucosectomy, ALTEMEIER’S perineal rectosigmoidectomy and THIERSCH’S anal encirclement. In full-thickness rectal prolapse, females are affected about 6 times more often than males (not the reverse), and abdominal rectopexy has lower recurrence than perineal procedures.

**UNDERSTAND:** The choice of route is a trade-off between durability and operative risk. Abdominal procedures fix the rectum to the sacrum, correct the underlying anatomy and have the lowest recurrence — but need a general anaesthetic and laparotomy or laparoscopy. Perineal procedures avoid entering the abdomen, so they suit the frail, elderly, high-risk patient, at the cost of higher recurrence. Recognising which category an eponym belongs to is recognising which patient it was designed for.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Abdominal: Ripstein (anterior sling rectopexy), wells (posterior mesh rectopexy), suture rectopexy, resection rectopexy (FRYKMAN–GOLDBERG — adds sigmoid resection, useful when constipation coexists), and laparoscopic ventral mesh rectopexy (D’Hoore) — the preferred modern operation.
- Perineal: Delorme — mucosal sleeve resection with plication of the muscularis, best for short full-thickness or mucosal prolapse; Altemeier — full-thickness perineal rectosigmoidectomy, for longer prolapse and the emergency of an incarcerated/strangulated prolapse (and with lower recurrence than Delorme); Thiersch — a wire or mesh encircling the anus, purely palliative in the unfit (risks impaction and erosion).
- Full-thickness prolapse shows concentric rings of mucosa; mucosal prolapse and prolapsing haemorrhoids show radial furrows — the bedside discriminator.
- Risk factors: chronic straining, multiparity, anorexia nervosa and childhood malnutrition. Associated findings: a deep pouch of Douglas, redundant sigmoid, lax pelvic floor and a patulous sphincter with incontinence (up to 75%; it often improves after repair but may not fully recover — an important part of consent). Assess with defaecography, anorectal manometry and colonoscopy — always exclude A lead POINT (polyp, carcinoma).
- In children, rectal prolapse is managed conservatively — treat constipation, diarrhoea and malnutrition, and exclude cystic fibrosis and whipworm (*Trichuris*); it almost always resolves without surgery.
- Solitary rectal ulcer syndrome is associated with internal intussusception and is treated by biofeedback, not primarily surgery.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Which is not a perineal approach?” → the abdominal rectopexy (Ripstein or Wells). “Incorrect about full-thickness rectal prolapse” → that men are affected 6 times more than women.

**REMEMBER:** *Ripstein and Wells go through the belly; Delorme, Altemeier and Thiersch stay below.*

### 47. Nerve Injury in Rectal and Pelvic Surgery

**PEARL:** High ligation of the inferior mesenteric artery at its origin injures the sympathetic superior hypogastric plexus → retrograde ejaculation and bladder dysfunction. Parasympathetic (nervi erigentes, S2–S4) injury causes impotence (failure of erection).

**UNDERSTAND:** The autonomic division of labour in the male pelvis is “POINT and shoot”: parasympathetic = Point (erection); sympathetic = Shoot (emission and closure of the bladder neck during ejaculation). The superior hypogastric plexus (L1–L3) lies directly over the aortic bifurcation and the origin of the IMA, so a high ligation predictably sweeps up the sympathetic fibres — abolishing bladder-neck closure, so semen passes backwards into the bladder.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Nerves at risk in rectal surgery: the superior hypogastric plexus (at the sacral promontory/IMA origin — sympathetic); the hypogastric nerves (pelvic sidewall); the nervi erigentes/pelvic splanchnics (S2–S4, at the lateral ligaments — parasympathetic); and the pelvic plexus (anterolaterally, near the seminal vesicles and DENONVILLIERS’ fascia).
- Total mesorectal excision with sharp dissection in the avascular “holy plane” is designed to preserve these nerves while removing the mesorectum en bloc.
- Other classic operative nerve injuries: thyroidectomy — recurrent and external laryngeal nerves; parotidectomy — facial nerve and FREY syndrome (auriculotemporal); axillary dissection — long thoracic (winged scapula), thoracodorsal and intercostobrachial; inguinal hernia repair — ilioinguinal/iliohypogastric and genitofemoral (chronic groin pain); posterior triangle node biopsy — spinal accessory nerve.
- Other pelvic structures at risk: the ureter (at the pelvic brim and near the uterine artery/cardinal ligament).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Two variables must be matched — which autonomic system and which function. Sympathetic = ejaculation and bladder, not erection.

**REMEMBER:** *Parasympathetic points, sympathetic shoots — cut the sympathetics and semen goes backwards.*

### 48. Hernias — Types and Eponyms

**PEARL:** Richter’s hernia — only part of the bowel circumference is trapped, so it can strangulate without complete obstruction (no absolute constipation). LITTRE’S contains a Meckel’s diverticulum; AMYAND’S contains the appendix; a sliding hernia has a retroperitoneal viscus (sigmoid colon, caecum or bladder) forming part of the wall of the sac; a PANTALOON hernia has both direct and indirect sacs straddling the inferior epigastric vessels. “taxis” IS the manual reduction of an incarcerated hernia.

**UNDERSTAND:** Richter’s hernia is dangerous precisely because it is quiet: the lumen stays patent so the patient passes flatus and faeces and does not look obstructed, while the trapped knuckle of antimesenteric bowel wall becomes ischaemic and perforates. It is classic in femoral hernias and at laparoscopic port sites — narrow-necked defects. The lesson generalises: a narrow neck strangulates early, and the absence of obstruction never excludes strangulation.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Ventral hernias are anterior abdominal wall hernias — epigastric, umbilical/paraumbilical, incisional, parastomal, Spigelian and (by convention) lumbar. A femoral hernia IS not ventral, since it passes beneath the inguinal ligament.
- Contents of the inguinal canal: the spermatic cord in men (vas deferens, testicular vessels, pampiniform plexus, genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve) or the round ligament in women — not the broad ligament — plus the ilioinguinal nerve in both.
- Mayo’s “double-BREASTING” repair is the classic operation for paraumbilical hernia (mesh for larger defects).
- Taxis IS contraindicated IF strangulation IS suspected (tenderness, toxicity, >6 h) — risk of “reduction EN masse” (reducing the sac with still-obstructed bowel inside) and of returning gangrenous bowel to the abdomen. An obstructed or strangulated hernia needs emergency surgery — never force taxis.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Strangulates without obstruction → Richter’s hernia; bowel forming part of the sac wall → sliding hernia; reduction of an incarcerated hernia → taxis; “not a ventral hernia” → femoral; umbilical hernia after pregnancy → Mayo’s operation.

**REMEMBER:** *A narrow neck kills quietly — Richter’s strangulates while the bowels still open.*

### 49. Femoral Hernia

**PEARL:** A tense, tender, irreducible swelling below and lateral to the pubic tubercle in an elderly woman with bowel obstruction is a strangulated femoral hernia. An inguinal hernia lies above and medial to the pubic tubercle.

**UNDERSTAND:** The femoral canal is a rigid tunnel bounded by the inguinal ligament in front, the pectineal (Cooper’s) ligament behind, the sharp unyielding lacunar (Gimbernat’s) ligament medially and the femoral vein laterally. Because three of those four walls are bone or unyielding ligament, bowel entering is squeezed at a narrow neck and strangulates early — the hernia most likely of all to obstruct and become ischaemic. This is why femoral hernias are always repaired, and repaired promptly, even when asymptomatic.

**MUST KNOW:**

- The femoral ring boundaries: anterior/superior — inguinal ligament; posterior/inferior — pectineal (Cooper’s) ligament and pecten pubis; medial — lacunar (Gimbernat’s) ligament; lateral — the femoral vein (not the conjoint tendon).
- Commoner in women (wider pelvis), especially thin, elderly, multiparous women — but an inguinal hernia is still the commonest hernia in women overall.
- Up to 40–50% present as an emergency with strangulation — every unexplained small bowel obstruction demands examination of both groins, including in the obese, where a small femoral hernia is easily missed.
- Repair approaches: low (LOCKWOOD) — below the inguinal ligament, best elective; high (McEVEDY) — best in emergencies because it allows bowel inspection and resection; inguinal (LOTHEISSEN). Laparoscopic TEP/TAPP is also used.
- If the neck must be divided to reduce a strangulated femoral hernia, cut the lacunar ligament medially — beware an abnormal obturator artery (“artery of death”) crossing it.
- Signs of strangulation: severe pain, tenderness, irreducibility, absent cough impulse, overlying erythema, and systemic features (tachycardia, fever, leucocytosis, raised lactate). Obstruction can occur without strangulation, and vice versa.
- Femoral canal contents normally: fat and the lymph node of Cloquet — the canal exists to let the femoral vein expand.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The whole question is the relationship to the pubic tubercle. Below and lateral = femoral; above and medial = inguinal. “Wrong femoral ring boundary” → lateral–conjoint tendon.

**REMEMBER:** *Below and lateral, rigid neck, strangles early — operate now.*

### 50. Inguinal Hernia Repair and the Named Repairs

**PEARL:** “herniorrhaphy” means A tissue (suture) repair; Lichtenstein’s tension-free mesh repair IS A HERNIOPLASTY. HERNIOTOMY (excision of the sac alone) suffices IN children and for congenital hydrocele. A patient with one inguinal hernia has roughly a 50% lifetime risk of a contralateral hernia, and the diagnosis is primarily clinical.

**UNDERSTAND:** Modern repair is built on one principle — tension IS the enemy of healing. Older pure-tissue repairs pull dissimilar structures together under tension, which hurts and recurs; mesh spreads the load and lowers recurrence from ~10% to 1–2%. The Desarda repair is the interesting compromise — a tissue repair that is nonetheless tension-free, using a living aponeurotic strip, favoured where mesh is unaffordable or contamination precludes it.

**MUST KNOW — the named repairs:**

- BASSINI — conjoint tendon to the inguinal ligament; no double-breasting.
- Shouldice — double-BREASTING of the transversalis fascia creating a 4-layered posterior wall (the best pure tissue repair).
- LICHTENSTEIN — tension-free synthetic mesh over the defect; the current gold standard open operation.
- Desarda — an UNDETACHED strip of external oblique aponeurosis sutured to the conjoint tendon and inguinal ligament; NO mesh.
- McVAY (Cooper’s ligament) — conjoint tendon to Cooper’s ligament; repairs both inguinal and femoral hernias (the tissue repair of choice for femoral).
- Mayo — “double-breasting” overlap of fascia for a small umbilical/paraumbilical hernia.
- Shouldice and Mayo both use “double-BREASTING” — Shouldice for the inguinal canal, Mayo for the umbilicus (the classic confusion).

**MUST KNOW — laparoscopic anatomy:**

- The “triangle of doom” (between the vas deferens medially and gonadal vessels laterally) contains the external iliac vessels — and the genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve. The “triangle of pain” (lateral to the gonadal vessels, below the iliopubic tract) contains the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve, the femoral branch of the genitofemoral nerve and the femoral nerve.
- Never place tacks OR STAPLES below the iliopubic tract laterally — the classic cause of postoperative lateral thigh pain from lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury.
- Laparoscopic (TEP/TAPP) repair IS particularly good for bilateral and recurrent (previously open) hernias, and can also treat femoral, sliding and obturator hernias — but a “hernia of a hydrocele” is not a hernia at all.
- Watchful waiting is acceptable for a minimally symptomatic direct inguinal hernia in an elderly man — but never for a femoral hernia.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Wrong statement” → that Lichtenstein’s mesh repair is a herniorrhaphy; strip of external oblique aponeurosis → Desarda; “not in the triangle of pain” → genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve; tacker below the iliopubic tract → lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh.

**REMEMBER:** *Mesh removes tension; keeping tacks above the iliopubic tract removes the pain. Shouldice = 4 layers of transversalis; Mayo = umbilical overlap; McVay = Cooper’s; McEvedy = high femoral.*

### 51. Chronic Pain After Inguinal Hernia Repair

**PEARL:** The commonest complication of inguinal hernia repair is chronic groin pain (INGUINODYNIA) — 10–15% of patients — far more common than infection, haematoma, seroma or recurrence.

**UNDERSTAND:** Tension-free mesh repair solved the mechanical problem (recurrence fell below 2%) but created a new one: the mesh incites a fibrotic reaction around the three nerves crossing the field — ilioinguinal, iliohypogastric and the genital branch of the genitofemoral — and sutures or tacks can entrap them directly. The complication profile shifted from failure of repair to nerve-related morbidity, which is why “chronic pain” is now the answer to a question that used to be “recurrence”.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Definition: pain persisting >3 months. Types: neuropathic (nerve injury/entrapment — burning, shooting, dermatomal, allodynia) and nociceptive/somatic (mesh fibrosis, periostitis from a suture in the pubic tubercle).
- MERALGIA PARAESTHETICA — burning pain and numbness over the anterolateral thigh from entrapment of the lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh (L2–L3) — is the recognised complication of laparoscopic (TEP/TAPP) repair when tacks or mesh are placed in the triangle of pain. It is purely sensory — no motor weakness (contrast femoral nerve injury, which weakens knee extension and abolishes the knee jerk).
- Other post-repair neuralgias: ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric (groin, scrotum/labia, medial thigh — after open repair) and the genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve.
- Management ladder: reassurance and time → NSAIDs and neuropathic agents (gabapentin, pregabalin, amitriptyline) → nerve blocks (diagnostic and therapeutic) → physiotherapy → triple neurectomy ± mesh removal.
- Prevention: identify and preserve (or deliberately excise) the nerves, avoid tacks in the triangle of pain, use lightweight large-pore mesh, and prefer laparoscopic repair for bilateral or recurrent hernias.
- Other complications: seroma/haematoma, ischaemic orchitis and testicular atrophy (damage to the pampiniform plexus), urinary retention, recurrence (<2%), mesh infection.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Most common complication” — the intuitive answers (infection, recurrence) are wrong; the mesh-era answer is chronic pain. For groin pain after laparoscopic repair, the anterolateral thigh distribution gives the nerve.

**REMEMBER:** *Mesh fixed the recurrence and left the nerves complaining.*

### 52. Gallstones and Their Complications

**PEARL:** Asymptomatic gallstones are not routinely operated ON — but prophylactic cholecystectomy IS indicated for stones >3 cm, polyps >1 cm, a porcelain gallbladder, chronic haemolytic disease (sickle cell, spherocytosis), an anomalous pancreaticobiliary junction, transplant and bariatric patients, and in high-incidence populations (northern India, Chile, Native Americans). Pigment stones predominate IN Asia (cholesterol stones in the West). Mirizzi syndrome = a stone impacted in the cystic duct/Hartmann’s pouch compressing the common hepatic duct and causing jaundice.

**UNDERSTAND:** The size threshold exists because a large stone chronically irritates the mucosa and is strongly associated with gallbladder carcinoma — a cancer usually incurable when it presents. The same logic runs through the whole list: every indication is a marker of either cancer risk (large stone, polyp, porcelain gallbladder, anomalous junction) or of predictably severe complications (haemolysis, immunosuppression). Where neither applies, the risk of surgery exceeds the risk of the stones.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Complications: biliary colic, acute and chronic cholecystitis, mucocele (obstructed cystic duct with sterile mucus) and empyema, choledocholithiasis with obstructive jaundice and cholangitis (Charcot’s triad — pain, fever, jaundice; Reynolds’ pentad adds shock and confusion), gallstone pancreatitis, gallstone ileus, Mirizzi syndrome, carcinoma.
- Brown pigment stones — biliary infection/stasis and parasites (*Clonorchis*, *Ascaris*), typically in the bile ducts. Black pigment stones — chronic haemolysis and cirrhosis, staying in the gallbladder. Only ~10–15% of gallstones are radio-opaque (“gallstones are usually radiolucent, renal stones usually radio-opaque”).
- Acute cholangitis needs resuscitation, antibiotics and urgent biliary drainage (ERCP) — it is the commonest cause of a pyogenic liver abscess. Contrast the amoebic abscess: a young man, right lobe, “anchovy SAUCE” pus, positive serology, treated with metronidazole.
- After CBD exploration a T-tube drains the duct (one limb in the CBD — it is not a transhepatic drain); a T-tube CHOLANGIOGRAM is done once the patient is well and the bile clear, and residual stones can be EXTRACTED through the mature tract (BURHENNE’S technique) after 4–6 weeks.
- IN gallstone pancreatitis, cholecystectomy should be performed during the same admission, as soon as the patient is fit — delay carries a high risk of recurrence.
- Medical dissolution (ursodeoxycholic acid) works only for radiolucent cholesterol stones <10 mm in a functioning gallbladder with a patent cystic duct, in uncomplicated biliary pain — radio-opaque stones will not dissolve (radio-opacity means calcium, which UDCA cannot touch), and pigment stones do not respond at all. Dose 10–15 mg/kg/day for 6–24 months; recurrence ~50% at 5 years, which is why it is rarely used. UDCA also has established roles in primary biliary cholangitis and intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Asymptomatic 4 cm gallstone → laparoscopic cholecystectomy; “incorrect about gallstones” → that cholesterol stones are commonest in Asia; “incorrect about the T-tube” → that it is used for transhepatic drainage; commonest cause of pyogenic liver abscess → ascending cholangitis; timing in biliary pancreatitis → before discharge; UDCA “considered in all except” → radio-opaque stones.

**REMEMBER:** *Operate on the asymptomatic gallbladder only when the stone, the wall or the patient carries a cancer risk. Asia = pigment; brown = infection in the duct, black = haemolysis in the gallbladder.*

### 53. Bile Duct Stones — Naming by Timing

**PEARL:** Stones in the CBD within 2 years of cholecystectomy are retained; after 2 years they are recurrent, and are almost always primary (brown pigment) stones formed de novo in the duct.

**UNDERSTAND:** The two-year cut-off is a surrogate for origin. A cholesterol stone that escaped the gallbladder before its removal is unlikely to stay silent beyond two years. A stone appearing later must have formed in the duct itself, which requires bile stasis and bacterial deconjugation of bilirubin (*E. Coli* β-glucuronidase) — hence its brown pigment composition, quite different from the cholesterol stones of the gallbladder.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Primary (brown pigment) stones: soft, earthy, formed in the duct, associated with stasis, strictures and biliary infection; common in Asia with *Clonorchis*/*Ascaris* infestation. Secondary stones: cholesterol stones from the gallbladder that migrated down.
- Investigation: ultrasound first — a CBD >8 mm with abnormal liver enzymes suggests choledocholithiasis. Suspected CBD stones (raised bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase with a dilated duct) are investigated with MRCP (or endoscopic ultrasound) first; ERCP IS reserved for therapy — going straight to ERCP exposes the patient to pancreatitis for a purely diagnostic purpose.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The interval since cholecystectomy is the entire question. Gallstones with raised bilirubin and ALP → MRCP.

**REMEMBER:** *Within two years it was left behind; after two years it was made there.*

### 54. Imaging the Obstructed Biliary Tree

**PEARL:** In obstructive jaundice with a distended gallbladder and dilated ducts on ultrasound, the next test to localise the cause is MRCP — non-invasive, complete ductal mapping. ERCP is reserved for therapy (or tissue sampling), and ERCP is least useful in acute pancreatitis without choledocholithiasis.

**UNDERSTAND:** The two tests answer different questions. MRCP is purely diagnostic and carries no procedural risk, so it comes first when you only need to know *where and why* the duct is blocked. ERCP is invasive with a 3–5% risk of post-ERCP pancreatitis, so its risk is justified only when an intervention is intended. Performing ERCP in acute pancreatitis without stones offers all the hazard and none of the benefit.

**MUST KNOW:**

- “MRCP before ERCP” is the general rule; go straight to ERCP when there is cholangitis, a known stone with obstruction, or a planned stent.
- ERCP indications: obstructive jaundice needing drainage, choledocholithiasis, cholangitis, bile leak, periampullary/pancreatic head tumours (stenting and brushings), recurrent pancreatitis (to diagnose pancreas DIVISUM), sphincter of Oddi manometry, pancreatic duct strictures.
- Courvoisier’s law: in a jaundiced patient, a palpable, NON-tender, distended gallbladder is unlikely to be due to stones — chronic stone disease produces a fibrotic, contracted gallbladder — so it suggests a malignant distal obstruction (pancreatic head or periampullary carcinoma). Exceptions: double impaction, a stone in the cystic duct with obstruction below, and Mirizzi syndrome.
- Endoscopic ultrasound is the most sensitive test for small distal CBD stones and small pancreatic/ampullary tumours, and permits FNA. PTC is used when ERCP fails or the obstruction is hilar/proximal.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Two recurring stems — “most useful to localise” → MRCP, and “ERCP least useful” → acute pancreatitis without stones.

**REMEMBER:** *Map it with MRCP; only reach for ERCP when you intend to do something.*

### 55. Bile Leak and the Classifications of Bile Duct Injury

**PEARL:** A collection in the gallbladder fossa with fever on day 2–3 after laparoscopic cholecystectomy is a bile leak — the first step is ultrasound-guided percutaneous (pigtail) drainage, not ERCP, not re-exploration. The Hannover classification is the one that includes CONCOMITANT vascular injury as well as the anatomical location.

**UNDERSTAND:** Management of any post-operative bile collection follows the universal surgical sequence drain the collection first, define the anatomy second, repair last. An undrained bile collection is an infected space causing sepsis; controlling the sepsis buys time. Only once drained do you ask *where* the leak is and whether it needs stenting or reconstruction. As for the classifications, each answers a different question — Strasberg and Bismuth describe *where* the duct is damaged, McMahon *how big* the hole is, and Hannover was created because outcome depends heavily on whether the right hepatic artery was also injured — an ischaemic duct strictures no matter how good the anastomosis.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Commonest source of a post-cholecystectomy leak: the cystic duct stump (clip slippage), then the SUBVESICAL duct of Luschka.
- ERCP + stent works for a low-pressure leak from the stump/Luschka because it abolishes the sphincter pressure gradient — but only after drainage. MRCP is non-invasive road-mapping; it never treats, so it cannot be the next step in a septic patient.
- Hepaticojejunostomy (Roux-en-Y) is for a major duct transection (Strasberg E / Bismuth) and is deliberately delayed until sepsis is controlled — early repair in an inflamed field strictures.
- Hannover A–E: A = peripheral leak (cystic stump, duct of Luschka); B = stricture without leak; C = lateral CHD/CBD injury; D = complete transection of the CHD; E = late main-duct stricture without leak. Vascular injury IS coded within types C and D.
- Strasberg A–E — the most widely used, purely anatomical: A = cystic duct/Luschka leak; B = occluded aberrant right duct; C = transected aberrant right duct; D = lateral CBD injury; E1–E5 = Bismuth types 1–5. McMAHON — by width of the injury (minor <25% of circumference vs major). Bismuth–Corlette — originally stricture level, now best known for hilar cholangiocarcinoma extension.
- Prevention: the critical view of safety (2 structures entering the gallbladder, cleared hepatocystic triangle); convert if the anatomy is unclear. CALOT’S triangle = cystic duct, common hepatic duct, inferior surface of the liver.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The bile-leak stem’s distractors are all real treatments in the *wrong order* — drain → define → repair. The classification question’s instinctive answer is Strasberg (the most famous), which is exactly the trap: vascular → Hannover.

**REMEMBER:** *Sepsis first, anatomy second, surgery last. Strasberg maps the duct, McMahon measures the hole, Bismuth grades the hilum, Hannover remembers the artery.*

### 56. Gallbladder Carcinoma

**pearl:** Risk factors: gallstones >3 cm (the most important), adenomatous polyps >10 mm, porcelain gallbladder, anomalous pancreatobiliary junction, choledochal cyst, primary sclerosing cholangitis, chronic *Salmonella typhi* carriage and obesity. A single polyp smaller than 1 cm IS not A risk factor, and adenomyomatosis IS not A recognised risk factor. Incidental T1a cancer (confined to the lamina propria) after simple cholecystectomy with clear margins needs NO further treatment; T1b and above require extended (radical) cholecystectomy.

**UNDERSTAND:** Every risk factor works through prolonged mucosal irritation and chronic inflammation → dysplasia → carcinoma. A big stone abrades the mucosa for decades; a porcelain gallbladder is the end-stage of that inflammation; an anomalous pancreatobiliary junction lets pancreatic enzymes reflux into the biliary tree, producing continuous chemical injury. A small solitary polyp causes no irritation and is usually a cholesterol polyp — not even a true neoplasm. The T1a/T1b line turns on the gallbladder’s unique anatomy — it has NO submucosa and only a thin muscular layer, and its bed is in direct contact with the liver, so once the tumour reaches muscle, lymphatic and direct hepatic spread become likely.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Polyp management: <5 mm — no follow-up; 6–9 mm — ultrasound surveillance; ≥10 mm, rapid growth, sessile, solitary, age >50, or coexisting PSC/gallstones — cholecystectomy.
- Adenocarcinoma in >90%; spreads early to liver segments IVb and V and to the cystic and pericholedochal nodes. Extended cholecystectomy = resection of segments IVb/V plus portal lymphadenectomy, with excision of port sites if found after laparoscopic surgery.
- Presentation is late and non-specific (RUQ pain, weight loss, anorexia); jaundice is late and ominous. Most are found incidentally at cholecystectomy — the only ones with a good prognosis — so every gallbladder specimen must go for histopathology. CA 19-9 for monitoring.
- India — especially the Gangetic belt — has among the highest incidence rates in the world; female preponderance. Avoid percutaneous biopsy of a resectable mass (tract seeding).
- Adenomyomatosis is a benign hyperplastic change (Rokitansky–Aschoff sinuses, “comet-tail” artefact) needing treatment only if symptomatic.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Which is not a risk factor?” → the small solitary polyp, or adenomyomatosis. Management of T1a → no further treatment. The two numbers examiners use: 3 cm stone, 1 cm polyp.

**REMEMBER:** *Three centimetres of stone and one centimetre of polyp are the lines — and with no submucosa, once the tumour reaches muscle the liver bed must come out too.*

### 57. Cholangiocarcinoma and Choledochal Cysts

**PEARL:** Bismuth–Corlette type III hilar cholangiocarcinoma involves the confluence with unilateral extension to second-order radicles. A Todani type III cyst (CHOLEDOCHOCELE) IS treated by endoscopic sphincterotomy, unlike types I and IV, which need complete excision with Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy.

**UNDERSTAND:** Choledochal cysts arise from an anomalous pancreaticobiliary junction, which allows pancreatic enzymes to reflux and chronically injure the biliary epithelium — which is why simple drainage is never acceptable: the cyst wall remains and carries a lifelong cholangiocarcinoma risk. The cyst must come out.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Bismuth–Corlette: I — below the confluence; II — reaching the confluence; IIIa — confluence plus right hepatic duct; IIIb — plus left hepatic duct; IV — bilateral second-order radicles or multifocal (generally unresectable). Resection usually requires hepatectomy with caudate lobe resection and biliary reconstruction.
- Todani management: I and IV — complete excision with Roux-EN-Y hepaticojejunostomy; II — simple excision; III — endoscopic sphincterotomy; V (Caroli) — segmental resection or transplantation. Classic triad: pain, jaundice and a mass.
- Cholangiocarcinoma presents with painless obstructive jaundice; CA 19-9 supports the diagnosis; biliary drainage before resection IS selective, since it introduces infection.
- Risk factors: primary sclerosing cholangitis (the strongest), choledochal cysts and Caroli disease, liver flukes (*Opisthorchis viverrini*, *Clonorchis sinensis*), hepatolithiasis, thorotrast, chronic typhoid carriage. Hepatitis B and C are risk factors for hepatocellular carcinoma, not classically for cholangiocarcinoma — the exam treats HBV as the odd one out.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Confluence with unilateral second-order extension → Bismuth–Corlette III; Todani type III → endoscopic sphincterotomy.

**REMEMBER:** *Never just drain a choledochal cyst — the wall itself turns malignant.*

### 58. Liver Tumours

**PEARL:** A cirrhotic liver with A rapidly ARTERIALLY enhancing MASS and A raised AFP IS hepatocellular carcinoma. Staging systems include BARCELONA clinic (BCLC), OKUDA and clip — Astler–Coller IS A colorectal system. Kasabach–Merritt syndrome = thrombocytopenia + consumptive coagulopathy + haemolysis within a large vascular tumour — not hepatic adenoma.

**UNDERSTAND:** The BCLC system IS preferred because IT links stage to treatment rather than merely describing extent — which is what a clinician actually needs.

**MUST KNOW:**

- BCLC: very early/early — resection, ablation or transplantation (MILAN criteria: one lesion ≤5 cm, or up to three ≤3 cm, with no vascular invasion or extrahepatic spread); intermediate — TACE; advanced — systemic therapy (sorafenib/lenvatinib, atezolizumab–bevacizumab); terminal — best supportive care.
- Surveillance of cirrhosis with 6-monthly ultrasound (± AFP) IS what makes curative treatment possible — AFP alone is neither sensitive nor specific.
- Liver resection depends on the future liver remnant and function (Child–Pugh, portal pressure, ICG clearance); portal vein embolisation can hypertrophy the remnant before major hepatectomy.
- Kasabach–Merritt is classically associated with kaposiform haemangioendothelioma/tufted angioma rather than ordinary infantile haemangioma, and is treated with steroids, vincristine, sirolimus or embolisation.
- Imaging diagnosis: a cirrhotic background + AFP >400 ng/mL + arterial hyperenhancement with venous washout on CECT is diagnostic — biopsy IS not required. Contrast: haemangioma shows peripheral nodular enhancement with centripetal fill-IN; hepatic adenoma is linked to OCPs and anabolic steroids.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Cirrhosis with an enhancing mass and raised AFP → hepatoma; “not a liver cancer staging system” → Astler–Coller; “not part of Kasabach–Merritt” → hepatic adenoma.

**REMEMBER:** *BCLC is the one that tells you what to do, not just how bad it is.*

### 59. Portal Hypertension — TIPSS and Balloon Tamponade

**PEARL:** Tipss IS contraindicated IN portal vein thrombosis — without a patent portal vein the shunt cannot be created — and in severe hepatic encephalopathy. Its indications are refractory variceal bleeding, refractory ascites and a bridge to transplantation. Sengstaken–Blakemore = 3 lumens (gastric aspiration, gastric balloon, oesophageal balloon); Minnesota = 4 lumens, adding an oesophageal aspiration port.

**UNDERSTAND:** Tipss works by creating a channel between a hepatic vein and an intrahepatic portal vein branch — so it presupposes a patent portal vein to decompress. Its major complication follows just as directly: diverting portal blood past the hepatocytes causes or worsens hepatic encephalopathy in up to a third of patients. The Minnesota tube’s extra port solves a different specific hazard: with the oesophageal balloon inflated, saliva and blood pool above it and are aspirated — being able to suck out that column is the whole advantage.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Balloon tamponade technique: inflate the gastric balloon first and apply traction at the gastro-oesophageal junction; inflate the oesophageal balloon only if bleeding continues, and deflate it periodically to avoid pressure necrosis. Maximum 24 hours as a bridge. Always intubate first. Complications: aspiration pneumonia, oesophageal rupture, mucosal ulceration and necrosis, airway obstruction if the balloon migrates.
- Definitive management of variceal bleeding: resuscitation → terlipressin/octreotide + prophylactic antibiotics (ceftriaxone reduces mortality — often forgotten) → urgent endoscopy with band ligation (preferred; fewer complications than sclerotherapy) → balloon tamponade or an oesophageal stent if uncontrolled → tipss as salvage.
- Gastric varices are treated with cyanoacrylate glue injection. Prophylaxis: non-selective beta-blockers (propranolol, carvedilol) and/or band ligation programmes.
- Other tipss contraindications: right heart failure/severe pulmonary hypertension, polycystic liver disease, sepsis. Main complications: encephalopathy and stent stenosis.
- Other portal hypertension interventions: splenectomy/devascularisation and shunt surgery in selected cases.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “not an indication for tipss” → portal vein thrombosis. Count the ports on the image: three = Sengstaken–Blakemore, four = Minnesota.

**REMEMBER:** *tipss needs a patent portal vein to decompress — and buys ascites control at the price of encephalopathy. The fourth lumen sucks out what would otherwise be aspirated.*

### 60. Child–Turcotte–Pugh Score

**PEARL:** CTP uses bilirubin, albumin, prothrombin time/INR, ascites and encephalopathy. Creatinine IS not included — that belongs to MELD.

**UNDERSTAND:** CTP was designed to measure hepatic synthetic and functional reserve, so every variable reflects a liver function: bilirubin (excretion), albumin and INR (synthesis), ascites and encephalopathy (clinical decompensation). MELD was designed later to predict short-term mortality for transplant allocation and deliberately added creatinine, because renal failure is the dominant prognostic factor in advanced liver disease.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Each variable scores 1–3. Class A = 5–6 (well compensated, ~10% surgical mortality); class B = 7–9 (~30%); class C = 10–15 (decompensated, ~75–80% mortality) for intra-abdominal surgery.
- Mnemonic — “Pour Another Beer At Eleven”: Prothrombin time, Albumin, Bilirubin, Ascites, Encephalopathy.
- Originally devised to predict outcome after portocaval shunt surgery; now used for any intra-abdominal operation in a cirrhotic and to guide HCC treatment.
- MELD = bilirubin, creatinine and INR (plus sodium in MELD-Na); used for transplant prioritisation.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Which parameter will you not use” → creatinine. Learn CTP and MELD as a contrasting pair.

**REMEMBER:** *Child-Pugh measures the liver; MELD adds the kidney.*

### 61. Acute Pancreatitis

**PEARL:** Gallstones are the commonest cause worldwide (alcohol second). Metabolic causes are hypercalcaemia, hyperparathyroidism and hypertriglyceridaemia — hyperkalaemia IS not A cause. SIRS criteria (two or more): temperature >38 or <36 °C, heart rate >90, respiratory rate >20 or PaCO₂ <32 mmHg, white cell count >12,000 or <4,000 — hypotension IS not A SIRS criterion (it defines shock). Intervention IS indicated for infected necrosis — sterile necrosis should not be drained OR disturbed.

**UNDERSTAND:** The rule about sterile necrosis is one of the most important in modern surgery: intervening on sterile necrosis converts a survivable inflammatory illness into an infected one and increases mortality. Hence the modern approach — “delay, drain, debride”: wait at least 4 weeks for the collection to wall off, then use a step-up approach (percutaneous or endoscopic transgastric drainage first, minimally invasive necrosectomy only if that fails) rather than early open necrosectomy.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Diagnosis needs 2 of 3: characteristic pain (epigastric, radiating to the back, relieved by sitting forward), amylase/lipase >3× normal (lipase is more specific and stays raised longer), and characteristic imaging. The enzyme level does not correlate with severity.
- Severity scoring: Ranson, APACHE II, Glasgow, BISAP, modified CT severity index. The revised ATLANTA classification defines mild (no organ failure or complications), moderately severe (transient organ failure <48 h or local complications) and severe (persistent organ failure >48 h). Persistent organ failure — not the enzyme level, not the CT appearance — drives mortality.
- Early management: moderate, goal-directed fluid resuscitation (Ringer’s lactate — the WATERFALL trial was stopped early because aggressive fluid loading caused more fluid overload with no benefit, so “aggressive hydration” is no longer the answer), analgesia, early enteral feeding (nasogastric or oral as tolerated — not prolonged fasting or routine TPN), NO prophylactic antibiotics, and urgent ERCP only IF there IS coexisting cholangitis OR persistent biliary obstruction.[^7]
- Vascular complications: pseudoaneurysm most often of the splenic artery (treat by angioembolisation), and splenic vein thrombosis causing left-SIDED (sinistral) portal hypertension with isolated gastric varices — cured by splenectomy.
- Other local complications: acute peripancreatic fluid collection, pseudocyst (>4 weeks, drained only if symptomatic), acute necrotic collection, walled-off necrosis.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Commonest cause → gallstones; “not a metabolic cause” → hyperkalaemia; “not a SIRS criterion” → BP <90/60; indication for intervention → infected necrotic collection; commonest pseudoaneurysm → splenic artery.

**REMEMBER:** *Leave sterile necrosis alone; delay, then step up only for infection.*

### 62. Chronic Pancreatitis, Annular Pancreas and Pancreatic Cancer

**PEARL:** A dilated pancreatic duct (>7 mm) with intraductal stones and intractable pain IS treated by A lateral PANCREATICOJEJUNOSTOMY — Puestow’s (Partington–Rochelle) procedure. An inflammatory mass in the head is treated by BEGER’S (duodenum-preserving head resection) or Frey’s procedure; small-duct disease confined to the tail by distal pancreatectomy. Annular pancreas causing neonatal duodenal obstruction IS bypassed by duodenoduodenostomy — the annulus IS never divided.

**UNDERSTAND:** Chronic pancreatitis surgery is chosen by “big duct versus small duct” disease. A dilated, obstructed duct implies raised intraductal pressure as the pain mechanism, so drainage (Puestow) relieves it without sacrificing pancreatic tissue, preserving endocrine and exocrine function. Small-duct disease has no duct to drain, so resection (or denervation) is required. The annular pancreas is never divided because the ring contains pancreatic ducts — dividing it causes a pancreatic fistula — so the obstruction is bypassed instead.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Chronic pancreatitis: alcohol is the commonest cause in adults; tropical (idiopathic juvenile) pancreatitis is important in India, with large intraductal calculi, diabetes and a risk of pancreatic cancer. Pain, steatorrhoea and diabetes; imaging shows calcification and a “chain of lakes” duct. Treat with abstinence, analgesia, pancreatic enzyme replacement and fat-soluble vitamins, then endoscopic or surgical drainage.
- Pancreatic adenocarcinoma: painless obstructive jaundice with a palpable gallbladder (COURVOISIER), weight loss and new-onset diabetes; CA 19-9; a pancreatic-protocol CT determines resectability by arterial and venous involvement; Whipple’s PANCREATODUODENECTOMY for resectable head tumours with adjuvant chemotherapy. Only 15–20% are resectable AT diagnosis.
- Hereditary risks: Peutz–Jeghers (STK11 — the highest relative risk of the familial syndromes), hereditary pancreatitis (PRSS1), BRCA2, CDKN2A/FAMMM, Lynch syndrome, cystic fibrosis.
- Duodenal atresia, annular pancreas and down syndrome travel together — the “double bubble” baby needs an echocardiogram and a karyotype as well as an operation.
- Palliation of unresectable disease: biliary and duodenal stenting, coeliac plexus block for pain, pancreatic enzymes, nutritional support.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Dilated duct with stones → Puestow’s procedure; annular pancreas in a Down syndrome neonate → duodenoduodenostomy; highest risk of pancreatic adenocarcinoma → STK11.

**REMEMBER:** *Big duct, drain it; small duct, resect it; annular pancreas, bypass it.*

### 63. Pancreatic Cystic Neoplasms — Cyst Fluid Analysis

**PEARL:** Mucin positive + high CEA + high amylase = IPMN. Amylase is high only in IPMN because it communicates with the pancreatic duct.

**UNDERSTAND:** Two markers answer two different questions. CEA/mucin answer *is it mucinous, i.e. Premalignant?* Amylase answers *does it connect to the ductal system?* Serous cystadenoma is neither mucinous nor connected (low, low); mucinous cystic neoplasm is mucinous but has no ductal connection (high CEA, low amylase); IPMN is both. A pseudocyst is not neoplastic — very high amylase but low CEA.

**MUST KNOW:**

| | Mucin | CEA | Amylase | Cytology |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Serous cystadenoma | – | Low | Low | Glycogen-rich, **PAS +**, central scar/microcystic honeycomb |
| Mucinous cystic neoplasm | + | **High** | Low | Columnar mucinous, **ovarian-type stroma**, women, body/tail |
| IPMN | + | **High** | **High** | Tall columnar mucinous, **duct communication**, head, “fish-mouth” ampulla |
| Pseudocyst | – | **Low** | **Very high** | Inflammatory debris, no epithelium |

- CEA >192 ng/mL is the standard cut-off for a mucinous lesion.
- MCN and main-duct IPMN are premalignant → resect. Branch-duct IPMN may be surveilled. Serous cystadenoma is benign → observe unless symptomatic.
- Worrisome/high-risk features for IPMN: main duct >10 mm, enhancing mural nodule, obstructive jaundice, size >3 cm.
- EUS-FNA is how the fluid is obtained.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** A three-marker stem where the examiner expects you to pivot on amylase — the only marker separating IPMN from MCN. “Ovarian-type stroma” or “middle-aged woman, tail” → MCN.

**REMEMBER:** *Amylase means the cyst talks to the duct — that’s IPMN.*

### 64. Hydatid Cyst: WHO-IWGE Staging Drives Management

**PEARL:** CE1 (simple unilocular, “double-line” sign) and CE3a → albendazole ± pair; CE2 (multiseptated “rosette/honeycomb”) and CE3b (daughter cysts in a solid matrix) → surgery (pericystectomy); CE4 (heterogeneous “ball of wool”) and CE5 (thick calcified wall) are inactive → observe, no intervention.

**UNDERSTAND:** The classification is an activity scale, not merely a description. A calcified (CE5) cyst is dead — the parasite has been walled off and destroyed by the host, so there is nothing to kill and nothing to spill; intervening only risks anaphylaxis, bleeding and secondary infection. Active cysts (CE1, CE2) contain viable protoscolices, so they must be sterilised or removed. Reading the CT is reading the biological activity of the parasite.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Pair (Puncture, Aspiration, Injection of a scolicidal, Re-aspiration) is done under albendazole cover (started days before, continued for weeks after) to prevent secondary echinococcosis if spillage occurs. Contraindicated in CE2/CE3b (septa prevent complete drainage), inaccessible cysts, and cysts communicating with the biliary tree (a scolicidal agent would cause sclerosing cholangitis).
- Scolicidal agents: hypertonic (20%) saline, 0.5% silver nitrate, cetrimide. Formalin IS NO longer used (sclerosing cholangitis). Pack the field with scolicidal-soaked swabs before opening any cyst.
- Surgical options: total or partial pericystectomy, hepatic segmentectomy, or “unroofing” with omentoplasty. The greatest intraoperative risk is spillage causing anaphylaxis and disseminated secondary cysts — have adrenaline and steroids ready.
- Complications: rupture into the biliary tree (jaundice, cholangitis), into the peritoneum (anaphylaxis), or into the pleura/bronchus; secondary bacterial infection.
- Serology (ELISA/IHA) supports the diagnosis; eosinophilia is present in only ~25%. Casoni’s test is obsolete.
- *E. Granulosus* — dog definitive host, sheep intermediate, human accidental dead-end; liver (70%) > lung. *E. Multilocularis* — fox; alveolar echinococcosis, behaves like a malignancy, treated with radical resection and lifelong albendazole.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The CT appearance gives the CE stage, and the stage gives the management. Calcified = CE5 = observe.

**REMEMBER:** *Calcified means dead — leave it alone; active and unilocular means pair; honeycomb means surgery.*

### 65. Breast Cancer — Staging Traps and Molecular Subtypes

**PEARL:** Contralateral (opposite-side) nodes = M1, not N3. Skin changes (peau d’orange/erythema) over >1/3 of the breast = T4d, inflammatory carcinoma. Ipsilateral supraclavicular node = N3c. Luminal A = ER and/or PR positive, HER2 negative, low Ki-67 — the commonest subtype and the one with the best prognosis.

**UNDERSTAND:** The nodal categories only ever describe ipsilateral drainage — axillary, infraclavicular, internal mammary, supraclavicular. Tumour that has reached the other side of the body has by definition travelled haematogenously or across watershed lymphatics, so the system calls it distant disease. The molecular classification is likewise a treatment map, not just a label: each subtype is defined by which drug target the tumour expresses — an oestrogen receptor makes it answerable to tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor; HER2 amplification to trastuzumab; and with neither, only cytotoxic chemotherapy remains, which is why triple-negative disease has the worst prognosis despite being highly chemosensitive. Ki-67 simply measures how fast the tumour divides, separating luminal A from luminal B.

**MUST KNOW:**

- T4a chest wall; T4b skin ulceration/satellite nodules/oedema; T4c both; T4d inflammatory carcinoma. N3a infraclavicular; N3b axillary + internal mammary; N3c ipsilateral supraclavicular.
- Inflammatory carcinoma = dermal lymphatic invasion; it is a clinical diagnosis, not a histological subtype; treatment is neoadjuvant chemotherapy first — it is a contraindication to primary surgery and to breast conservation.

| Subtype | ER/PR | HER2 | Ki-67 | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Luminal A** | **+** | **−** | **Low** | **Endocrine therapy**; best prognosis |
| **Luminal B** | + | ± | **High** | Endocrine + chemotherapy (± anti-HER2) |
| **HER2-enriched** | − | **+** | High | **Trastuzumab/pertuzumab + chemotherapy** |
| **Triple negative / basal-like** | − | − | High | **Chemotherapy** (± immunotherapy, PARP inhibitors if BRCA); worst prognosis |

- Prognosis by histology, best → worst: tubular → adenoid cystic → cribriform → papillary → medullary → invasive lobular → invasive ductal (nos) → inflammatory (worst).
- Invasive lobular carcinoma: E-cadherin negative, single-file cells, bilateral/multicentric, poorly seen on mammography.
- BRCA1 → triple-negative/basal-like; BRCA2 → more often ER-positive (luminal).
- Endocrine therapy: tamoxifen in PREmenopausal women — a SERM, antagonist in breast, agonist in uterus, bone and liver, so it causes endometrial carcinoma, VTE, hot flushes and fatty liver while preserving bone density. Aromatase inhibitors (anastrozole, letrozole) in POSTmenopausal women (arthralgia, osteoporosis) — useless premenopausally because ovarian oestrogen synthesis is not aromatase-limited in the same way.
- Trastuzumab causes reversible cardiotoxicity — monitor LVEF; do not give concurrently with an anthracycline. Oncotype DX / MammaPrint guide whether ER-positive, node-negative patients need chemotherapy.
- Clinical signs of locally advanced disease: hard fixed lump, nipple retraction, skin discolouration/peau d’orange, palpable nodes — from tethering to Cooper’s ligaments and dermal lymphatic invasion.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The staging question hangs entirely on spotting “contralateral” → M1; candidates who read only the skin changes pick T4d N3c M0. For subtypes, the trap option adds “high proliferation” to luminal A (that is luminal B).

**REMEMBER:** *Cross the midline and you have left the N’s behind — that’s M1. Luminal A has a receptor to block and is in no hurry.*

### 66. Sentinel Node Biopsy and Breast Conservation

**PEARL:** Sentinel lymph node biopsy IS performed IN clinically node-negative early breast cancer — typically alongside breast conservation. It is not done when the axilla is already clinically or cytologically positive; those patients go to axillary dissection (or neoadjuvant chemotherapy). Breast conservation therapy = wide local excision with clear margins plus whole-breast radiotherapy, and gives survival equivalent to mastectomy.

**UNDERSTAND:** The sentinel node is the first node draining the tumour, so if it is free of tumour the rest of the basin almost certainly is too — the procedure is a staging tool that spares the morbidity of full dissection (lymphoedema, numbness, shoulder dysfunction). It is logically pointless when nodal disease is already proven: you cannot use a test to exclude what you have already demonstrated. Equally, almost every contraindication to BCS is really a contraindication to the radiotherapy that must follow it, or to achieving clear margins.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Technique: dual localisation — radioisotope (99mTc-sulphur colloid) plus blue dye (isosulfan/methylene blue), injected peritumorally or subareolarly; identification rate ~97%. Watch for anaphylaxis and transient falsely low pulse-oximeter readings with blue dye.
- Contraindications to SLNB: clinically positive axilla (proven on ultrasound-guided FNAC), inflammatory breast cancer, previous axillary surgery.
- De-escalation continues past Z0011: the SOUND trial showed that in small (≤2 cm) tumours with a negative axillary ultrasound treated by breast conservation, omitting axillary surgery altogether is non-inferior for 5-year distant-disease-free survival — so the trend is towards no axillary staging at all in selected low-risk women.[^5]
- If the sentinel node is positive: ACOSOG Z0011 showed that with T1–T2 tumours, breast conservation plus whole-breast radiotherapy, and 1–2 positive sentinel nodes, completion axillary dissection can be omitted without a survival difference. More than 2 positive nodes, or mastectomy without radiotherapy → axillary dissection.
- Contraindications to BCS: multicentric tumour (different quadrants — multifocal disease in the same quadrant is acceptable), diffuse malignant microcalcifications, a large tumour-to-breast ratio, persistently positive margins after re-excision, previous chest/breast irradiation, SLE or other collagen vascular disease (severe radiation reaction), ankylosing spondylitis, severe orthopnoea (cannot lie flat), pregnancy in the first and second trimesters, and inflammatory carcinoma.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The image or stem shows blue dye/gamma probe → the question is which patient. Anchor on node-negative.

**REMEMBER:** *Sentinel node answers a question — don’t ask it when you already know the answer. And if she can’t have the radiotherapy or clear margins, she can’t have BCS.*

### 67. DCIS After Breast Conservation

**PEARL:** After breast-conserving surgery for DCIS with clear margins, the standard next step is adjuvant radiotherapy to the breast. Chemotherapy has NO role IN DCIS — it is an in-situ disease with no capacity to metastasise.

**UNDERSTAND:** DCIS is malignant epithelium still confined by the basement membrane, so it cannot reach lymphatics or blood vessels — hence no nodal or distant spread and no indication for systemic cytotoxic therapy. The only real risk is local recurrence (about half of which returns as invasive cancer) from residual disease in the same breast. Radiotherapy addresses precisely that risk, roughly halving it.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Whole-breast radiotherapy after BCS reduces local recurrence by ~50% but does not change overall survival. It may be omitted in carefully selected low-risk DCIS (small, low-grade, wide margins, older patient).
- Adequate margin for DCIS IS 2 mm (versus “no ink on tumour” for invasive cancer).
- Endocrine therapy (tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor) for 5 years is offered for ER-positive DCIS, to reduce ipsilateral recurrence and contralateral new primaries.
- Sentinel node biopsy IS not routine IN DCIS — but it IS done when a mastectomy is performed (the option is lost afterwards) or when the lesion is large/high-grade with a high chance of occult invasion.
- DCIS is usually detected as microcalcifications on screening mammography (branching, pleomorphic, linear); comedo type with central necrosis and high nuclear grade carries the highest recurrence risk. Van NUYS prognostic index (size, margin, grade, age) guides management.
- Paget disease of the nipple = DCIS extending into the nipple epidermis — eczematous, unilateral, and does not respond to steroids; biopsy any persistent nipple eczema.
- LCIS IS different — a risk marker, not a precursor: it is not excised for margins but managed by surveillance ± risk-reducing endocrine therapy.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The trap is offering chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy. In situ = local treatment only.

**REMEMBER:** *It cannot spread, so treat only the breast — surgery plus radiotherapy.*

### 68. Radical Mastectomy and Axillary Anatomy

**PEARL:** Halsted radical mastectomy removes the entire breast with skin and nipple-areola complex, both pectoralis major and minor, and axillary nodes at levels I, II and III. Supraclavicular nodes are not removed.

**UNDERSTAND:** Halsted’s operation was designed on the theory that breast cancer spreads by contiguous local extension, so everything anatomically continuous with the tumour was excised. Supraclavicular nodes lie beyond the axillary field, and their involvement signifies disease that surgery cannot control — removing them would not achieve local control and changes the treatment from surgical to systemic.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Modern operations: breast conservation + radiotherapy (equivalent survival to mastectomy); modified radical mastectomy — PATEY removes pectoralis minor only, AUCHINCLOSS/MADDEN preserves both pectorals; simple/total mastectomy; skin-sparing and nipple-sparing mastectomy for reconstruction.
- Axillary levels are defined by pectoralis minor: level I lateral, level II behind, level III medial. Level I + II clearance is standard.
- Structures at risk in axillary dissection: long thoracic nerve (winged scapula), thoracodorsal nerve (latissimus dorsi), intercostobrachial nerve (numb inner upper arm), and the axillary vein.
- The commonest complication of mastectomy is seroma — prevented by closed suction drains; others are wound infection, flap necrosis, lymphoedema and intercostobrachial nerve injury.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Not removed in radical mastectomy” → supraclavicular nodes. Both pectoral muscles are removed in the classical Halsted operation.

**REMEMBER:** *Halsted took everything in the axilla but nothing above the clavicle.*

### 69. Phyllodes Tumour and Fibroadenoma

**PEARL:** Phyllodes arises from hormonally responsive breast stroma; biopsy shows increased stromal cellularity with a “leaf-like” (phyllodes) architecture. Usually benign, in women >40. Treat by wide excision with a ≥1 cm margin; NO axillary dissection and NO role for FNAC. Fibroadenoma, by contrast, is an aberration of normal lobular development (ANDI) — not a true neoplasm.

**UNDERSTAND:** Phyllodes is a fibroepithelial tumour like a fibroadenoma, but the stroma is the neoplastic component and it can turn malignant. Because the malignant element is sarcomatous (mesenchymal), it spreads by blood, not lymphatics — which is why the axilla is left alone.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Malignant phyllodes metastasises haematogenously to the lungs; lymph node metastasis is rare — the standard exam trap.
- FNAC cannot distinguish phyllodes from fibroadenoma — a core biopsy is needed (and even then the final grade comes from the excised specimen).
- Recurrence is local and related to margin width; large or malignant lesions may need mastectomy.
- Clinically: large, rapidly growing, bosselated, mobile mass; skin may ulcerate from pressure necrosis, not invasion — the key discriminator from carcinoma (which tethers and retracts). Also called cystosarcoma phyllodes or serocystic disease of Brodie — misleading names, since most are benign.
- Fibroadenoma: a disorder of the early reproductive period (15–25 years) in the ANDI classification; hormone-responsive (grows in pregnancy, regresses after menopause); firm, smooth, highly mobile “breast mouse”. Small ones in young women may be observed after triple assessment; excise if >3–4 cm, growing or symptomatic. A giant fibroadenoma is >5 cm and is simply ENUCLEATED through a submammary incision.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “False about this condition” → “metastasises to axillary lymph nodes”.

**REMEMBER:** *Leaf-like stroma, spreads by blood not lymph — wide excision, never the axilla.*

### 70. Breast Congenital Anomalies — The Terminology

**PEARL:** Polythelia = accessory nipples (commonest). POLYMASTIA = accessory breast tissue. AMASTIA = congenital absence of breast + nipple. Athelia = absence of nipple only. Amazia = breast tissue absent but nipple–areola present — almost always iatrogenic. SYMMASTIA = midline webbing between the breasts.

**understand:** The Greek roots do the work: -thelia = nipple, -mastia/-mazia = breast gland; poly- = extra, a- = absent. Once you separate “nipple” from “gland”, every term reads itself.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Accessory breast tissue and nipples arise anywhere along the embryonic milk line, from axilla to groin — the axilla is the commonest site, and axillary breast tissue can enlarge and become tender in pregnancy and lactation.
- Amazia is the exam’s favourite because it is acquired: radiation to the chest wall in childhood, excision of A “lump” that was actually the breast bud, or I&D of a suspected abscess in a prepubertal girl. Hence: A prepubertal breast lump should never be biopsied — it is almost always the normal breast bud.
- Poland syndrome = absence of the sternocostal head of pectoralis major ± breast/nipple hypoplasia, with ipsilateral hand anomalies (syndactyly, brachydactyly).
- Polythelia has a weak reported association with renal tract anomalies.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Pure terminology matching, or “all are true about amazia except” → “amazia is absence of the nipple” (that is athelia).

**REMEMBER:** *Thelia = nipple, mastia = gland. Amazia keeps the nipple and is usually the surgeon’s fault.*

### 71. Breast Self-Examination and BI-RADS

**PEARL:** Breast self-examination can be performed standing OR lying down, once a month, ideally on days 3–6 of the cycle (any fixed day if postmenopausal), using the pads of the middle three fingers.

**UNDERSTAND:** Days 3–6 are chosen because the breast is at its least oestrogen-stimulated and therefore least nodular and tender — cyclical engorgement in the luteal phase creates lumpiness that both masks and mimics pathology. Examining at the same point each cycle also makes month-to-month comparison meaningful, which is the entire value of self-examination.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Both visual inspection (contour, dimpling, puckering, nipple retraction or discharge, skin changes) and palpation are required, in standing and supine positions and with arms raised. Palpation patterns: vertical strips, concentric circles or radial spokes — cover the axillary tail and press the nipple gently.
- BI-RADS: 0 incomplete, needs more imaging; 1 negative and 2 benign → routine annual screening; 3 probably benign (≤2% malignancy) → 6-monthly follow-up for 2 years; 4 suspicious (4A >2–10%, 4B >10–50%, 4C >50–95%) → biopsy; 5 highly suggestive (≥95%) → biopsy and treat; 6 biopsy-proven malignancy.
- Triple assessment — clinical examination, imaging (mammography and/or ultrasound) and pathology (core biopsy) — for any palpable lump.
- Mammographic screening: the USPSTF 2024 final statement recommends biennial mammography for ALL women from age 40 to 74 (grade B) — the previous “individualise in the forties” advice is gone. Ultrasound is preferred under 35 (dense breasts); MRI for high-risk/BRCA carriers.[^4]

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Incorrect regarding self-examination” → the claim that it must be done only while standing. The BI-RADS 3 answer is 6-monthly follow-up, not biopsy.

**REMEMBER:** *Examine when the breast is quietest — just after the period.*

## Endocrine Surgery

### 72. Post-Thyroidectomy Complications by Timing

**PEARL:** Carpopedal spasm 2–3 days after total thyroidectomy = hypocalcaemia from parathyroid ischaemia. Thyroid surgery is the commonest cause of hypoparathyroidism.

**UNDERSTAND:** The parathyroids share their blood supply (inferior thyroid artery) with the thyroid, so total thyroidectomy — especially with central compartment neck dissection — devascularises them. The lag of 24–72 hours reflects the half-life of circulating PTH and the time for calcium to fall; that delay is exactly what the stem is testing.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Earliest symptom: circumoral and fingertip paraesthesia, then carpopedal spasm, then tetany/laryngospasm/seizures.
- Chvostek sign — tap over the facial nerve anterior to the ear → facial twitch. Trousseau sign — BP cuff inflated 3 minutes → carpopedal spasm (more specific). ECG: prolonged QT.
- Transient hypoparathyroidism is common; permanent is rare (<2% in expert hands). Treat with oral calcium plus calcitriol (active vitamin D, bypassing the PTH-dependent 1α-hydroxylation step).
- The full timeline: immediate hoarseness → recurrent laryngeal nerve (bilateral palsy → stridor, an emergency); external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve (cricothyroid) → loss of high pitch and a weak voice; hours → haematoma (airway compromise — open AT the bedside); 24–48 hours → thyroid storm; 2–3 days → hypocalcaemia.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Timing is the discriminator — “2–3 days later” is calcium; “immediately on extubation” is nerve; “within 6 hours with neck swelling” is haematoma.

**REMEMBER:** *Day 0 hoarse = nerve. Day 2 twitchy = calcium.*

### 73. Preoperative Preparation in Graves Disease

**PEARL:** Potassium iodide (Lugol’s iodine) is given for 10–14 days before thyroidectomy specifically to reduce the vascularity of the gland and hence intraoperative bleeding. Antithyroid drugs and beta-blockers render the patient euthyroid, but they do not shrink the blood supply.

**UNDERSTAND:** Iodine has two distinct effects. Acutely it inhibits hormone release and organification (the Wolff–Chaikoff effect), which is why it is used in thyroid storm. Separately — and this is the surgical point — it causes involution of the hyperplastic gland: the follicles shrink, colloid re-accumulates and the abnormal vascularity regresses, producing a firmer, less bloody gland. The effect is transient (“escape” after about 10–14 days), which is exactly why it is started shortly before, and never long before, surgery.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Preparation sequence: antithyroid drug (carbimazole/PTU) until euthyroid → beta-blocker (propranolol) for symptom control and to blunt the adrenergic surge → Lugol’s iodine for the last 10–14 days. Operating on a thyrotoxic patient risks thyroid storm.
- Thyroid storm (“the 5 Bs”): Beta-blocker (propranolol); Block synthesis (propylthiouracil — preferred here, as it also blocks peripheral T4→T3 conversion); Block release (iodine, given at least one hour after the antithyroid drug, or it will fuel new hormone synthesis); Block conversion (steroids); and supportive care (cooling, fluids) — plus treat the precipitant.
- Antithyroid drugs: carbimazole/methimazole is first-line (better compliance, less hepatotoxicity) — but propylthiouracil is preferred in the first trimester of pregnancy (carbimazole causes aplasia cutis and choanal atresia) and in thyroid storm. Both cause agranulocytosis — warn every patient to report a sore throat and stop the drug immediately.
- Definitive options in Graves: antithyroid drugs, radioiodine (avoid in pregnancy and active eye disease, or cover with steroids), or surgery (large goitre, compressive symptoms, suspicion of malignancy, severe eye disease, pregnancy intolerant of drugs).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Note the exact wording — “to minimise intraoperative bleeding” selects iodine, not the drug that makes the patient euthyroid.

**REMEMBER:** *Iodine shrinks the gland and dries the field — the last two weeks before the knife.*

### 74. Retrosternal Goitre and Thyroglossal Cyst

**PEARL:** Over 95% of retrosternal goitres can be removed through a cervical (neck) incision — sternotomy is rarely needed. Surgery is indicated for symptoms or suspicion of malignancy, not for all patients.

**UNDERSTAND:** Almost all retrosternal goitres are secondary — a downward extension of a cervical nodular goitre dragged into the mediastinum by swallowing and negative intrathoracic pressure. Because they retain their blood supply from the superior and inferior thyroid arteries IN the neck, they can be delivered upward once those vessels are controlled cervically. Only the rare primary (ectopic mediastinal) goitre, supplied by intrathoracic vessels, requires sternotomy.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Definition: >50% of the gland lies below the suprasternal notch.
- Presentation: often asymptomatic, or dysphagia, tracheal compression, stridor, hoarseness and SVC obstruction. PEMBERTON’S sign — facial plethora and neck vein distension on raising the arms above the head.
- CECT neck and thorax is the investigation of choice (though iodinated contrast may delay radioiodine treatment).
- Indications for sternotomy: primary/ectopic goitre, invasive malignancy, a very large or recurrent goitre, or extension below the aortic arch.
- Thyroglossal cyst — a midline neck swelling that moves both on swallowing and on tongue protrusion (tethered to the hyoid and foramen caecum); commonest site infrahyoid; treated by SISTRUNK’S operation (excision of the cyst with the central portion of the hyoid body and the tract to the foramen caecum) — the only way to prevent recurrence.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “True regarding retrosternal goitre” → most are removable through the neck.

**REMEMBER:** *It came down from the neck, so it can go back up through the neck.*

### 75. MEN Syndromes and the RET Proto-Oncogene

**PEARL:** Medullary thyroid carcinoma (amyloid-rich stroma, raised calcitonin) + phaeochromocytoma (raised metanephrines) + parathyroid hyperplasia (raised calcium and PTH) with a family history = MEN 2A (Sipple syndrome), caused by a gain-of-function mutation in the RET proto-oncogene (10q11.2).

**UNDERSTAND:** RET is a receptor tyrosine kinase; the MEN 2 mutations make it constitutively active, so it behaves as a classic oncogene — a single activating allele suffices, giving autosomal dominant inheritance with high penetrance. Contrast MEN 1 (menin) and other tumour suppressors, which require loss of function of both alleles. Paradoxically, loss-of-function RET mutations cause Hirschsprung disease — the same gene, opposite direction, entirely different disease.

**MUST KNOW:**

- MEN 2A: medullary thyroid carcinoma (~100%), phaeochromocytoma (50%), parathyroid hyperplasia (20–30%) — may also feature cutaneous lichen amyloidosis and Hirschsprung disease.
- MEN 2B (MEN 3): MTC + phaeochromocytoma + mucosal neuromas and intestinal ganglioneuromatosis + marfanoid habitus — and NO parathyroid disease. The most aggressive; MTC develops in infancy.
- MEN 1 (menin, 11q13, tumour suppressor — “3 Ps”): parathyroid hyperplasia (commonest and earliest), pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours and pituitary adenoma (prolactinoma). The commonest site of a gastrinoma in MEN 1 is the duodenum (not the pancreas) — usually small and multiple, within PASSARO’S gastrinoma triangle (cystic duct, the junction of the second and third parts of the duodenum, and the neck/body of the pancreas). Diagnose Zollinger–Ellison with gastrin >1000 pg/mL with gastric pH <2, or a secretin stimulation test showing a rise of >200 pg/mL.
- RET carriers require prophylactic total thyroidectomy — timing by mutation risk (infancy for MEN 2B, by age 5 for high-risk MEN 2A). Always exclude and treat the phaeochromocytoma first (alpha then beta blockade) — operating on an unrecognised phaeochromocytoma can be fatal.
- Medullary thyroid carcinoma arises from parafollicular C cells (neural crest): secretes calcitonin (and CEA), stroma stains with Congo red for amyloid, spreads early to nodes, and is not iodine-avid — so radioiodine and TSH suppression do not work. Treat with total thyroidectomy + central compartment node dissection; vandetanib/cabozantinib and selective RET inhibitors for advanced disease.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The question tests oncogene vs tumour suppressor. MEN 2 = RET, gain of function; MEN 1 = menin, loss of function.

**REMEMBER:** *RET switched ON gives MEN 2; RET switched off gives Hirschsprung.*

### 76. Intraoperative PTH — The Miami Criterion

**PEARL:** Miami criterion: an adequate parathyroidectomy is confirmed when intraoperative PTH falls to more than 50% below the highest pre-excision value, measured 10 minutes after removal.

**UNDERSTAND:** PTH has a half-life of only 3–5 minutes, so removing the sole hyperfunctioning gland should produce a steep, measurable fall within two half-lives. Failure of the level to halve means another hyperfunctioning gland is still secreting — so the operation must convert from a focused approach to bilateral neck exploration there and then, rather than discovering persistent disease weeks later.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Most primary hyperparathyroidism (~85%) is a single adenoma, which is why focused/minimally invasive parathyroidectomy is feasible — after localisation with a sestamibi scan and ultrasound (± 4D-CT).
- Failure of the PTH to drop suggests MULTIGLANDULAR disease or hyperplasia — as in MEN 1 and MEN 2A, where SUBTOTAL (3½ gland) parathyroidectomy or total parathyroidectomy with autotransplantation is required.
- Indications for surgery in asymptomatic primary hyperparathyroidism: age <50, serum calcium >1 mg/dL above normal, creatinine clearance <60, T-score ≤−2.5 or fragility fracture, nephrolithiasis or nephrocalcinosis, 24-h urine calcium >400 mg.
- Postoperative risks: hypocalcaemia (including hungry bone syndrome) and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Both numbers matter — 50% and 10 minutes. The options permute one-third/half and 10/30 minutes.

**REMEMBER:** *Two half-lives, so it should halve in ten minutes.*

## Urology

### 77. Urethral Injury and the Cardinal Rule of Blood at the Meatus

**PEARL:** Blood AT the external urethral meatus = do not pass A urethral catheter. Straddle/perineal blow → bulbar (anterior) urethral injury with a butterfly perineal haematoma. Pelvic fracture → membranous (posterior) urethral injury with a high-riding prostate. Drain by suprapubic catheter once the bladder fills.

**UNDERSTAND:** Blind catheterisation converts a partial urethral tear into a complete transection and introduces infection into the periurethral haematoma. Waiting for the bladder to fill is not passivity — a palpable, full bladder is what makes suprapubic puncture safe and confirms the kidneys are producing urine. Anatomically, the bulbar urethra is crushed against the inferior pubic ramus by a direct perineal blow, and blood tracks within Colles’ fascia — attached to the perineal body, fascia lata and Scarpa’s fascia — confining the haematoma into the classic butterfly shape. The membranous urethra is fixed by the urogenital diaphragm, so it shears when the pelvic ring is disrupted and the prostate rides up.

**MUST KNOW:**

| | Posterior (proximal) | Anterior (distal) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Site | Membranous > prostatic | **Bulbar** > penile |
| Mechanism | **Pelvic fracture** | **Straddle** injury, fall astride, instrumentation |
| Signs | Retention, **high-riding prostate (VERMOOTEN SIGN)**, deep pelvic haematoma | Perineal **BUTTERFLY haematoma**, blood at meatus |
| Extravasation | Above the urogenital diaphragm, into the pelvis | Superficial perineal pouch (Colles’ fascia) — scrotum, penis, anterior abdominal wall |

- Investigation of choice: retrograde URETHROGRAPHY (RGU) before any instrumentation. RGU — contrast instilled at the meatus, shows the anterior urethra (penile, bulbar). MCU/VCU — contrast into the bladder with films during voiding, shows the posterior urethra and bladder, and is the investigation of choice for vesicoureteric reflux and posterior urethral valves.
- If the bladder is not yet distended, the correct next step is to wait for it to fill, then place a suprapubic catheter.
- Anterior injuries → suprapubic diversion and delayed urethroplasty. Posterior → suprapubic catheter with delayed repair (or primary realignment).
- Posterior injuries associate with pelvic fracture and extraperitoneal bladder rupture (managed by catheter drainage); intraperitoneal rupture follows a blow to a full bladder and needs surgical repair.
- Late complications: stricture, incontinence and impotence (worse with posterior injuries).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The trap option is always “immediate Foley catheterisation”. The mechanism in the stem decides the site: manhole/bicycle/straddle → bulbar; RTA with pelvic fracture → membranous.

**REMEMBER:** *Blood at the meatus — hands off the urethra. RGU for the front, MCU for the back.*

### 78. Renal and Ureteric Stones

**PEARL:** ACETOHYDROXAMIC acid is a urease inhibitor used for struvite (triple-phosphate) stones. Struvite crystals are coffin-LID shaped and the stone is classically staghorn. Hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria and HYPERURICOSURIA promote calcium stones; citrate and magnesium inhibit stone formation — so HYPOcitraturia and HYPOmagnesuria (not hyper-) are the risk factors.

**UNDERSTAND:** Struvite stones form only in alkaline, infected urine: urease-producing organisms split urea into ammonia, driving pH above 7.2 and precipitating magnesium ammonium phosphate. Treating the stone therefore means attacking the enzyme — but AHA cannot dissolve existing stone, so complete surgical clearance of every fragment is essential or the infection (and the stone) recurs. More generally, stones form when the concentration product exceeds the formation product — when solute outruns the inhibitory capacity of the urine. Citrate chelates calcium, keeping it in solution; magnesium binds oxalate; and uric acid promotes calcium oxalate stones by heterogeneous nucleation, acting as a template.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Urease producers: Proteus (classic), Klebsiella, Pseudomonas, *Staphylococcus saprophyticus*, Ureaplasma. *E. Coli* does not produce urease.
- Stone types: calcium oxalate (commonest, ~75%); struvite (infection stones, staghorn, alkaline urine); uric acid (radiolucent, acid urine — gout, myeloproliferative disease, chronic diarrhoea); cystine (cystinuria, hexagonal crystals, faintly radio-opaque).
- Crystal shapes: coffin-LID = struvite; envelope/octahedral = calcium oxalate; hexagonal = cystine; rhomboid or rosette = uric acid.
- PCNL is the treatment of choice for staghorn and for any stone >2 cm; ESWL is for stones <2 cm in favourable positions and is a poor choice for staghorn. Cystine stones are the most ESWL-resistant (hard and homogeneous; calcium oxalate MONOhydrate and brushite are also hard), while uric acid and struvite fragment easily. Fragments collecting in the ureter after ESWL = steinstrasse.
- Prevention: high fluid intake (>2.5 L/day), normal dietary calcium (restriction increases oxalate absorption), reduced sodium and animal protein, potassium citrate, and thiazides for hypercalciuria. Allopurinol for hyperuricosuric calcium and uric acid stones; urinary alkalinisation dissolves uric acid stones; cystine → alkalinise plus penicillamine/tiopronin.
- Primary hyperoxaluria, enteric hyperoxaluria (after small bowel resection or in Crohn disease) and vitamin C excess raise oxalate.
- AHA side effects (haemolytic anaemia, DVT, tremor) restrict its use to patients unfit for or refusing surgery.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Coffin-lid crystals + a plain X-ray KUB showing a large stone → X-ray KUB, PCNL. The prefix trap: hypercitraturia and hypermagnesuria are protective.

**REMEMBER:** *Urease splits urea, alkali makes struvite, AHA blocks urease. Citrate and magnesium hold calcium in solution.*

### 79. Testicular Tumours

**PEARL:** A solid testicular mass with retroperitoneal nodal disease is treated by radical inguinal orchidectomy followed by chemotherapy (BEP) — never by a scrotal approach.

**UNDERSTAND:** Two anatomical facts dictate everything. The testis drains lymphatically to the para-aortic nodes at L2 — following its embryological origin — not to the inguinal nodes; that is why retroperitoneal nodes are the first station and why the scrotal skin must not be violated (a scrotal incision seeds the inguinal nodes and changes the drainage basin, upstaging the disease). And germ cell tumours are exquisitely chemosensitive, so systemic disease is curable — testicular cancer is the model of a metastatic solid tumour cured by chemotherapy.

**MUST KNOW:**

- High inguinal orchidectomy with early cord clamping is both diagnostic and therapeutic. Trans-scrotal biopsy IS contraindicated.
- Markers before and after surgery: AFP (never raised in pure seminoma), β-hCG (may be mildly raised in seminoma from syncytiotrophoblasts), LDH (tumour bulk). A raised AFP means the tumour IS treated AS NON-seminomatous, whatever the histology says.
- Seminoma — radiosensitive and chemosensitive, orderly lymphatic spread, excellent prognosis. NON-seminomatous (embryonal, yolk sac, choriocarcinoma, teratoma) — earlier haematogenous spread; choriocarcinoma is the most aggressive, with lung metastases and very high hCG.
- Regimen: BEP — bleomycin, etoposide, cisplatin, 3–4 cycles. A residual retroperitoneal MASS after chemotherapy IN NSGCT → RPLND (it may contain mature teratoma, which is chemoresistant and must be excised — “growing teratoma syndrome”).
- Offer sperm banking before treatment. Risk factors: cryptorchidism (orchidopexy reduces but does not abolish the risk and makes surveillance possible), previous contralateral tumour, infertility, Klinefelter (mediastinal germ cell tumours).
- Ultrasound is first-line imaging for any testicular lump; a solid intratesticular mass is malignant until proven otherwise.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The stem gives the stage through the nodes. Node-positive → systemic disease → chemotherapy after orchidectomy, not RPLND or radiotherapy first.

**REMEMBER:** *Through the groin, never the scrotum — then chemotherapy for the para-aortic nodes.*

### 80. Fournier Gangrene

**PEARL:** A necrotising fasciitis of the perineum, genitalia and perianal region caused by a mixed aerobic and anaerobic (polymicrobial, type I) infection — typically in a diabetic, alcoholic or immunosuppressed man. Treatment is immediate, radical surgical debridement plus broad-spectrum antibiotics.

**UNDERSTAND:** The synergy between organisms is what makes it so fast. Aerobes consume oxygen and create an anaerobic microenvironment; anaerobes then produce heparinase and collagenase, thrombosing the subcutaneous vessels. That thrombosis is why the infection spreads along fascial planes far beyond the visible skin change, why antibiotics alone fail (the drug cannot reach dead, thrombosed tissue), and why debridement must go back to bleeding, healthy tissue and be repeated daily.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Pain out of proportion to the physical signs, crepitus, dishwater discharge, skin anaesthesia and rapidly advancing erythema are the early clues; frank necrosis is late. Gas on X-ray/CT supports it but its absence excludes nothing. LRINEC score aids diagnosis but should never delay surgery.
- The testes are characteristically spared — they have a separate blood supply from the abdomen (testicular arteries) — so orchidectomy IS not routinely performed. Likewise urinary diversion is not compulsory (a urethral catheter usually suffices), and anti-gas-gangrene serum has no role (this is not clostridial myonecrosis).
- Antibiotics: broad-spectrum triple cover — a carbapenem or piperacillin-tazobactam + clindamycin (antitoxin effect) + vancomycin/linezolid for MRSA.
- Adjuncts: aggressive resuscitation, glycaemic control, colostomy for faecal diversion if the anus is involved, hyperbaric oxygen (adjunctive, controversial), and eventual reconstruction/skin grafting. Mortality 20–40%.
- Type I necrotising fasciitis = polymicrobial (as here); type II = MONOMICROBIAL group A streptococcus (young, healthy patients, may follow trivial trauma or varicella, associated with streptococcal toxic shock).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Which statement is accurate?” — the true one is the mixed aerobic and anaerobic microbiology. Orchidectomy and compulsory urinary diversion are the standard false options.

**REMEMBER:** *Mixed flora thrombose the fascia — cut it all out, and leave the testes alone.*

### 81. Varicocele, Scrotal Swellings and TURP

**PEARL:** Percutaneous embolisation of the gonadal (testicular) vein is first-line for a symptomatic varicocele, with surgical ligation reserved for failure or recurrence (~20%). Bipolar TURP uses normal saline irrigation and therefore abolishes the risk of TUR syndrome.

**UNDERSTAND:** In monopolar TURP the current must travel through the patient to a distant pad, so the irrigant must be non-conductive and electrolyte-free (glycine, sorbitol) — and when that hypo-osmolar fluid is absorbed through open prostatic venous sinuses it causes dilutional hyponatraemia, cerebral oedema and heart failure. In bipolar TURP the current passes only between two electrodes at the resectoscope tip, so isotonic saline can be used and absorption causes no osmotic disturbance — which is why bipolar resection is chosen for the elderly cardiac patient.

**MUST KNOW:**

- TUR syndrome: confusion, restlessness, nausea, bradycardia, hypertension then hypotension, visual disturbance and seizures, from absorption of >2 L of irrigant; sodium falls below 125 mmol/L. Risk rises with resection time >60 minutes, large glands and a high irrigation bag height. Treat with fluid restriction, furosemide and cautious hypertonic saline; glycine metabolism to ammonia can also cause transient blindness and encephalopathy.
- Other TURP complications: retrograde ejaculation (the commonest, 65–75% — warn every patient), bleeding, urethral stricture, incontinence (rare), erectile dysfunction.
- Varicocele: “bag of worms”, dragging discomfort worse on standing and relieved on lying down; left-sided in ~90% (the left testicular vein drains at a right angle into the left renal vein, has a longer course and often incompetent valves). A new, right-SIDED or NON-emptying varicocele in an older man demands imaging for a renal cell carcinoma obstructing the renal vein or IVC.
- Indications to treat: pain, testicular atrophy or growth arrest in an adolescent, and infertility with abnormal semen parameters. Grading: I palpable only on Valsalva; II palpable at rest; III visible through the scrotal skin.
- Other scrotal swellings: hydrocele — fluctuant, transilluminant, you cannot get above it; treated by JABOULAY eversion or LORD’S plication. Epididymal cyst/spermatocele — transilluminant, above and behind the testis, excised. Testicular tumour — solid, does not transilluminate → urgent ultrasound and inguinal orchidectomy.
- Prostate cancer: active surveillance for low-risk disease (Gleason 6, PSA <10, small-volume T1c) — particularly in men over 70 — with DRE and PSA every 3–6 months and repeat biopsy every 1–3 years.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Both stems name the comorbidity or complication to be avoided (cardiac failure → bipolar/saline; symptomatic varicocele → the least invasive effective option, embolisation).

**REMEMBER:** *Bipolar lets you irrigate with saline, so there is nothing to dilute.*

### 82. Horseshoe Kidney and Renal Cell Carcinoma

**PEARL:** The isthmus of a horseshoe kidney is arrested by the inferior mesenteric artery at L3–L4. Division of the isthmus does not improve function and risks urinary fistula.

**UNDERSTAND:** The two lower poles fuse in the pelvis at 4–6 weeks, and the fused kidney then ascends until the isthmus catches on the inferior mesenteric artery — which is why it always lies just below the IMA origin and lower than a normal kidney. The obstruction that causes symptoms is not the isthmus itself but the high ureteric insertion and anterior ureteric course over it, so dividing the isthmus achieves nothing.

**MUST KNOW:**

- The commonest renal fusion anomaly; male predominance ~2:1; the adrenals remain IN their normal position. IVU shows the “flower vase” appearance — medially deviated lower poles with ureters draped over the isthmus.
- Complications: hydronephrosis and PUJ obstruction, recurrent infection, stones, and increased risk of renal cell carcinoma, Wilms tumour and transitional cell carcinoma. Associations: Turner syndrome, trisomy 18, VACTERL. It is vulnerable to blunt trauma because it lies across the vertebral column.
- Renal cell carcinoma: partial nephrectomy (nephron-sparing) is the treatment of choice for tumours <4 cm (also for bilateral tumours, a solitary kidney, or pre-existing renal impairment); radical nephrectomy for larger/locally advanced disease. RCC is radio- and chemo-resistant; advanced disease is treated with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (sunitinib, sorafenib), mTOR inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors.
- Putty kidney — homogeneous “ground-glass” calcification of a destroyed kidney — is a sign of genitourinary tuberculosis, which classically presents with sterile pyuria; diagnose with three early-morning urine samples for AFB and culture.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “False regarding horseshoe kidney” → division of the isthmus improves function. The IMA relation is asked separately.

**REMEMBER:** *It stopped rising because the inferior mesenteric artery was in the way.*

### 83. Haemorrhagic Cystitis in Children

**PEARL:** Acute haemorrhagic cystitis in a child is caused by adenovirus (types 11 and 21) — self-limiting, treated with supportive care and hydration.

**MUST KNOW:**

### 84. Varicose Veins, CEAP and the Venous Ulcer

**PEARL:** C2 = varicose veins; the suffix A = asymptomatic, S = symptomatic — so uncomplicated varicose veins in an asymptomatic patient are C2A. A venous ulcer sits in the gaiter area (medial malleolus most often), is caused by ambulatory venous hypertension, and has sloping/SHELVING edges with a granulating base — punched-out edges belong to the ischaemic (arterial) or neuropathic ulcer.

**UNDERSTAND:** CEAP separates what you see (Clinical) from why it happened (Etiology), where (Anatomy) and how (Pathophysiology); the clinical class is a strict ladder of increasing severity, so classification means identifying the most advanced finding present and adding the symptom suffix. Edge morphology is likewise a read-out of how the tissue is dying: a venous ulcer heals and breaks down repeatedly at its margin, so epithelium creeps inward, producing a sloping edge; an arterial ulcer dies where perfusion stops, with viable tissue right up to the boundary — hence a sharp, punched-out edge.

**MUST KNOW:**

- C0 no visible disease; C1 telangiectasia/reticular veins; C2 varicose veins; C3 oedema; C4a pigmentation or eczema; C4b lipodermatosclerosis or atrophie blanche; C4c corona phlebectatica; C5 healed ulcer; C6 active ulcer. E: primary, secondary (post-thrombotic), congenital. A: superficial, deep, perforator. P: reflux, obstruction, or both.
- Pathophysiology: ambulatory venous hypertension from valvular incompetence or deep venous obstruction → capillary fibrin cuffs and leukocyte trapping → lipodermatosclerosis (“inverted CHAMPAGNE bottle” leg), haemosiderin pigmentation, venous eczema, atrophie blanche, and finally ulceration.
- Duplex ultrasound is the investigation of choice (reflux >0.5 s in superficial veins).
- Treatment ladder: compression stockings and elevation → endovenous thermal ablation (laser or radiofrequency — now first line) → ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy or cyanoacrylate glue → open surgery (high ligation and stripping, phlebectomy).
- For the ulcer: MULTILAYER graduated compression bandaging is the mainstay — but measure the ABPI first; compression is dangerous if ABPI <0.8. Add elevation, exercise, wound care and pentoxifylline; treat superficial reflux to reduce recurrence.
- Ulcer edges: sloping = venous/healing; punched-out = arterial, neuropathic (also syphilitic gumma); undermined = tuberculous; everted = squamous cell carcinoma (Marjolin — biopsy any non-healing ulcer); rolled/beaded = basal cell carcinoma. Neuropathic/trophic ulcers are painless, punched-out with regular margins, over pressure points (plantar surface), surrounded by callus.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Identify the highest clinical class in the image, then add A or S from the history. “All are true except” with the edge as the false option. And the ABPI trap — never bandage without it.

**REMEMBER:** *Class by the worst sign, suffix by the symptoms. Venous ulcers slope; arterial ulcers are punched out.*

### 85. Tests for Varicose Veins

**PEARL:** Brodie–Trendelenburg and MORRISSEY’S cough impulse tests assess saphenofemoral incompetence. FEGAN’S test locates perforator incompetence. Modified Perthes’ test assesses deep venous patency.

**UNDERSTAND:** Each test isolates one component by controlling where blood can enter or leave. Occluding the saphenofemoral junction with a tourniquet and watching what fills tells you whether reflux comes from above (SFJ) or from below (perforators). Perthes’ works the opposite way — by occluding the superficial system and asking the patient to exercise, it forces flow into the deep system and reveals whether it is patent.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Brodie–Trendelenburg part I — release the tourniquet after standing; rapid filling from above = saphenofemoral incompetence. Part II — keep the tourniquet on; gradual filling from below upward = perforator incompetence.
- MORRISSEY’S cough impulse — an expansile impulse at the saphenous opening on coughing = SFJ incompetence.
- FEGAN’S test — palpate for fascial defects at perforator sites with the patient standing, mark them, then confirm with the leg elevated.
- Modified Perthes’ test — tourniquet below the SFJ, ask the patient to walk; increasing pain and distension indicate deep venous obstruction (a contraindication to superficial vein stripping).
- Schwartz test — tapping a distal varix transmits a thrill to the SFJ.
- Duplex ultrasound has replaced all of these clinically, but the eponyms remain examinable.
- MONDOR’S disease — thrombophlebitis of superficial breast/chest wall veins, presenting as a tender subcutaneous cord with a groove on stretching the skin; risk factors include smoking, breast surgery or biopsy, trauma, and underlying breast cancer.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The question groups all four eponyms — sort them by which part of the venous system each interrogates.

**REMEMBER:** *Tourniquet above tests the junction; tourniquet plus walking tests the deep veins.*

### 86. Lymphoedema

**PEARL:** Congenital lymphoedema presents before the age of 2 years; when familial it is Milroy disease — inherited as an autosomal dominant trait. Lymphoedema praecox (2–35 years) is the commonest type and is called Meige disease.

**UNDERSTAND:** Primary lymphoedema is classified by age of onset because that reflects the severity of the underlying lymphatic hypoplasia. Complete aplasia manifests at birth; hypoplasia that is marginally adequate decompensates only in adolescence (praecox, often at puberty in girls); minimal hypoplasia may not fail until adulthood (tarda).

**MUST KNOW:**

- Congenital (<2 years) — Milroy if familial; often bilateral, may involve multiple limbs, genitalia and face; more common in males; caused by VEGFR-3 (FLT4) mutations.
- Praecox (2–35 years) — Meige disease; commonest overall; usually unilateral and limited to below the knee; more common in females.
- Tarda (>35 years) — rare; always exclude an underlying malignancy causing obstruction.
- Secondary lymphoedema is far more common worldwide: filariasis (*Wuchereria bancrofti* — the commonest cause globally), and in developed countries axillary/inguinal node dissection, radiotherapy and malignancy.
- Clinical: NON-pitting oedema in established disease (pitting only early), a positive STEMMER sign (cannot pinch the skin at the base of the second toe), peau d’orange and hyperkeratosis.
- Management: complex decongestive therapy — skin care, manual lymphatic drainage, compression bandaging and exercise; surgery (vascularised lymph node transfer, lymphaticovenous anastomosis, Charles/Homans debulking) for selected cases. Diuretics are ineffective.
- Complication to know: LYMPHANGIOSARCOMA (Stewart–Treves syndrome) in long-standing lymphoedema.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The age of onset defines the class; the eponym then follows. Note the inheritance is dominant, not recessive.

**REMEMBER:** *Before two is congenital and familial Milroy; after two is praecox and Meige.*

## Paediatric Surgery

### 87. Neonatal Surgical Obstruction

**PEARL:** Suction rectal biopsy IS the diagnostic test of choice for Hirschsprung disease, showing absent ganglion cells with hypertrophied nerve trunks and increased acetylcholinesterase (contrast enema shows the transition zone but is not diagnostic). Meconium ileus obstructs AT the terminal ileum — not at the duodenojejunal flexure — is almost always associated with cystic fibrosis, shows a “soap-bubble” pattern with an unused MICROCOLON, and may be relieved by a Gastrograffin enema. Bilious vomiting IN any neonate IS A surgical emergency until malrotation IS excluded.

**understand:** The pathology explains the level: in cystic fibrosis, pancreatic exocrine insufficiency leaves meconium abnormally thick and sticky, and it impacts where the lumen is narrowest and the contents most desiccated — the terminal ileum. The colon distal to the block has never been used, hence the MICROCOLON. A hyperosmolar Gastrograffin enema works therapeutically by drawing fluid into the lumen to soften the impacted meconium — a rare example of a contrast study being the treatment.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Hirschsprung disease: failure of craniocaudal neural crest migration leaving a distal aganglionic segment (rectosigmoid in 75%); failure to pass meconium IN 48 hours, distension and bilious vomiting, with an explosive gush on rectal examination; associated with down syndrome; treated by pull-through surgery. Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis IS the life-threatening complication.
- Complicated meconium ileus (volvulus, atresia, perforation with meconium peritonitis and intra-abdominal calcification) requires surgery — resection with primary anastomosis or a temporary stoma (the historic Bishop–Koop operation is now rarely used). Confirm CF by sweat chloride and CFTR genotyping in every case.
- Neonatal obstruction by level: duodenal atresia — “double bubble”, Down syndrome; jejunoileal atresia — “triple bubble”/multiple levels, from an intrauterine vascular accident; malrotation with volvulus — bilious vomiting demanding an urgent upper GI contrast study; anorectal malformation — always examine the perineum.
- Malrotation with midgut volvulus → Ladd’s procedure: anticlockwise detorsion + division of Ladd’s bands + Broadening of the mesentery + incidental appendicectomy + small bowel placed on the right and colon on the left. The upper GI contrast study is the gold standard (abnormal DJ flexure position); ultrasound shows the WHIRLPOOL sign and SMA/SMV inversion.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Investigation of choice for Hirschsprung → suction rectal biopsy; “incorrect about meconium ileus” → that the occlusion is at the DJ flexure; the Bishop–Koop procedure → meconium ileus.

**REMEMBER:** *No ganglion cells on suction biopsy; thick meconium stuck at the terminal ileum with an unused colon beyond.*

### 88. Anorectal Malformation and VACTERL

**PEARL:** An anorectal malformation in a newborn should prompt a search for the VACTERL association: Vertebral defects (hemivertebrae), Anorectal malformation, Cardiac defects, tracheo-oesophageal fistula with oesophageal atresia, Renal anomalies and limb defects (radial ray). Wilms tumour IS not part of VACTERL.

**UNDERSTAND:** VACTERL is a non-random association of defects arising during BLASTOGENESIS (the first 4 weeks), when the caudal mesoderm, hindgut, foregut septation, cardiac looping, renal induction and limb buds are all forming together. It is therefore a checklist, not a syndrome: finding one component obliges you to look for the others, because a missed cardiac or renal anomaly can be more dangerous than the malformation that brought the baby to attention.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Work-up on finding an anorectal malformation: echocardiogram, renal and spinal ultrasound, vertebral X-rays, a nasogastric tube to exclude oesophageal atresia, and limb examination.
- Anorectal malformations are classified by whether the rectum ends above or below the levator ani and by the fistula (perineal, rectourethral, rectovesical, rectovaginal). Low lesions have a good prognosis with a primary perineal operation; high lesions need a diverting colostomy followed by posterior sagittal ANORECTOPLASTY (PSARP) and later closure.
- Continence outcome depends ON the level of the lesion and ON sacral development (the sacral ratio) — high lesions and sacral dysgenesis predict poor continence, which must be discussed with parents.
- Oesophageal atresia with tracheo-oesophageal fistula: suspect with polyhydramnios, drooling, choking on the first feed and a coiled nasogastric tube on X-ray; the commonest type is proximal atresia with A distal fistula (type C).
- Distinguish: charge syndrome, OEIS complex, and the caudal regression syndrome of a diabetic mother.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Least likely association with anorectal malformation → Wilms tumour.

**REMEMBER:** *Find one VACTERL defect and you are obliged to hunt for five more.*

### 89. Biliary Atresia and the Kasai Procedure

**PEARL:** The Kasai HEPATOPORTOENTEROSTOMY — a Roux-en-Y jejunal loop anastomosed to the transected porta hepatis — is the operation for extrahepatic biliary atresia, and must be performed before 60 days of age. Associated anomalies (the biliary atresia splenic malformation syndrome, ~20%) include POLYSPLENIA, situs inversus, malrotation, a preduodenal portal vein, absent inferior vena cava and cardiac defects — but not renal agenesis.

**UNDERSTAND:** Two separate logics. The association is with a laterality (situs) defect, not a renal one — the embryological insult occurs when left–right asymmetry is being established, so the anomalies cluster around organs whose position and number depend on laterality (spleen, gut rotation, great veins, heart); the kidneys are established independently, which is why absent kidney is the outlier. And the operation works because, although in type III there is no patent duct to anastomose to, microscopic biliary ductules remain open at the transected porta hepatis — laying bowel directly against that raw surface lets bile drain from them. Success therefore depends entirely on operating before those ductules obliterate.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Presentation: persistent (>14 days) conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia, pale/acholic stools and dark urine in an otherwise thriving newborn, with hepatomegaly. Any jaundice persisting beyond 14 days must have A split (fractionated) bilirubin measured — conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia is never physiological.
- Investigations: ultrasound (absent or contracted gallbladder, the “triangular cord sign”), HIDA scan with phenobarbitone priming (uptake but NO excretion into the gut), and liver biopsy (bile ductular proliferation, bile plugs, portal fibrosis); intraoperative CHOLANGIOGRAM is definitive.
- Types: I — atresia of the common bile duct (a Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy suffices); II — common hepatic duct; III — right and left hepatic ducts (~90%, requires the Kasai).
- Even after a successful Kasai, most children eventually need liver transplantation — biliary atresia is the commonest indication for paediatric liver transplantation. Postoperative complication: ascending cholangitis.
- Differential for neonatal conjugated jaundice: choledochal cyst, neonatal hepatitis, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, Alagille syndrome (paucity of bile ducts, butterfly vertebrae, peripheral pulmonary stenosis, posterior embryotoxon), galactosaemia, TPN-associated cholestasis, cystic fibrosis.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Not usually associated” → the anomaly outside the laterality spectrum (absent kidney). The image of a Roux loop to the liver hilum identifies the operation.

**REMEMBER:** *A laterality defect — spleens, situs, rotation and veins go wrong together. Anastomose the bowel to the raw porta while the tiny ductules are still open, before 60 days.*

### 90. The Vanishing Testis and the Undescended Testis

**PEARL:** At diagnostic laparoscopy for an impalpable undescended testis, blind-ending testicular vessels and vas above the internal ring prove a “vanishing testis” (testicular regression) — nothing further needs to be done.

**UNDERSTAND:** Blind-ending vessels are the key finding, not blind-ending vas. The gonad develops before and independently of the vas, and the vessels follow the testis wherever it goes — so vessels that simply stop mean the testis descended and then infarcted in utero (usually antenatal torsion). A blind-ending vas alone does not exclude a testis, since vas and gonad have separate origins. So the surgical decision rests entirely on tracing the vessels.

**MUST KNOW:**

- If the vessels enter the internal ring, the testis is inguinal or in the scrotal remnant → inguinal exploration. If a viable intra-abdominal testis is found → orchidopexy (single-stage, or a staged Fowler–STEPHENS procedure dividing the testicular vessels when the vas’s collateral supply must be relied on).
- Timing: orchidopexy between 6 and 18 months of age (ideally by 12 months). Spontaneous descent essentially ceases after 6 months; delay costs germ cells.
- Undescended testis risks: infertility (bilateral much worse), malignancy (3–8×, seminoma — the risk is reduced but not abolished by orchidopexy, which mainly makes the testis examinable), torsion, trauma, and an associated indirect inguinal hernia (>90%). The contralateral descended testis also carries a slightly increased cancer risk — teach self-examination.
- A RETRACTILE testis (brisk cremasteric reflex; it can be brought to the scrotum and stays) needs only observation; a true undescended testis cannot be manipulated into the scrotum.
- Laparoscopy is the gold standard for the impalpable testis — ultrasound is unreliable and does not change management.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The finding is blind-ending vessels. The trap is offering further exploration in each anatomical compartment.

**REMEMBER:** *Follow the vessels — if they end blindly, so does the search.*

### 91. Pulmonary Sequestration

**PEARL:** Pulmonary sequestration receives its arterial supply from a systemic artery — usually the descending thoracic aorta — not from the pulmonary artery.

**UNDERSTAND:** The sequestered lung bud arises as an accessory outgrowth of the foregut caudal to the normal lung, so it recruits its supply from the primitive splanchnic (systemic aortic) plexus and never connects to the pulmonary artery or the tracheobronchial tree. That aberrant systemic vessel is the diagnostic feature — and the surgical hazard, since it retracts into the abdomen if divided carelessly.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Intralobar (~75%): within the normal lung, shares its pleura, usually the left lower lobe (posterobasal); systemic arterial supply with pulmonary venous drainage; presents in older children/adults with recurrent infection.
- Extralobar (~25%): outside the lung with its own pleura, usually left-sided and infradiaphragmatic or basal; systemic arterial supply with systemic venous drainage (azygos/hemiazygos); presents in neonates and is associated with congenital diaphragmatic hernia.
- Diagnosis: CT angiography to demonstrate the feeding vessel; treatment is surgical resection (or embolisation of the feeding artery).
- Differential of a congenital lung lesion: congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM), congenital lobar emphysema, bronchogenic cyst.
- Bochdalek hernia: respiratory distress within 6 hours of birth IS A poor prognostic sign (severe pulmonary hypoplasia); contents typically retroperitoneal fat, left kidney, spleen or bowel; diagnosed prenatally at 16–24 weeks. A pericardial cyst is the differential of a cardiophrenic mass.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The blood supply is the answer, and the intralobar/extralobar distinction turns on pleural covering and venous drainage.

**REMEMBER:** *Lung tissue that never joined the airway keeps its embryonic aortic supply.*

## Neurosurgery and Thoracic Surgery

### 92. Diffuse Axonal Injury

**PEARL:** Unconscious from the moment of impact, a normal CT, and MRI showing microhaemorrhages in the corpus callosum and dorsolateral midbrain with no mass effect = diffuse axonal injury.

**UNDERSTAND:** DAI is a shearing injury, not a mass lesion. Rotational acceleration makes brain regions of differing density slide against one another, tearing axons and their microvasculature at grey–white junctions and along tethered midline structures (corpus callosum, dorsolateral brainstem). Because there is no space-occupying clot, CT is normal and there is no midline shift — the injury is microscopic and diffuse, which is exactly why the patient is deeply unconscious out of all proportion to the scan.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Classic triad for the stem: high-velocity RTA + coma from impact + normal CT.
- MRI is the investigation of choice; SWI (susceptibility-weighted imaging)/GRE is the most sensitive sequence — it blooms on haemosiderin and detects far more lesions than CT or standard T2.
- ADAMS grading: Grade 1 grey–white junction only → Grade 2 adds the corpus callosum → Grade 3 adds the dorsolateral brainstem (worst prognosis). Other sites: fornix, internal/external capsule, cerebellar peduncles.
- Contrast: extradural (lucid interval, biconvex, middle meningeal artery), subdural (crescentic, bridging veins, elderly/alcoholic), SAH (thunderclap, blood in the cisterns) — all visible on CT.
- Treatment is supportive; prognosis is the worst of the traumatic intracranial injuries.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Any stem that pointedly says “CT was normal” after a severe head injury is signalling DAI. A second version asks the best imaging modality → MRI, SWI sequence.

**REMEMBER:** *Deep coma + clean CT = shearing, not bleeding.*

### 93. Intracranial Aneurysm: Coiling versus Clipping

**PEARL:** Endovascular coiling is the interventional radiologist’s procedure and, since the ISAT trial, is the preferred treatment for most ruptured aneurysms when the anatomy is suitable. Clipping is the neurosurgical alternative. Nimodipine is not a treatment for the aneurysm at all — it prevents vasospasm.

**UNDERSTAND:** Coiling packs the aneurysm sac from inside the lumen with platinum coils that induce thrombosis, so the parent vessel is never exposed and the brain is never retracted — hence lower morbidity and better one-year outcomes than craniotomy in ISAT. The trade-off is incomplete occlusion and recanalisation, so coiled aneurysms need surveillance angiography. Clipping obliterates the neck from outside and is more durable, so it is preferred for wide-necked, middle cerebral artery or distal aneurysms, and when a large haematoma needs evacuating anyway.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Subarachnoid haemorrhage: “the worst headache of my life”, thunderclap onset, neck stiffness, photophobia, sometimes a sentinel bleed. NCCT within 6 hours IS >95% sensitive; if negative and suspicion persists, do a lumbar puncture AT 12 hours looking for xanthochromia (which distinguishes true SAH from a traumatic tap). Then CT angiography.
- Commonest sites: anterior communicating artery; posterior communicating artery (→ a painful, pupil-involving third nerve palsy); and the middle cerebral artery bifurcation. Associations: polycystic kidney disease, Ehlers–Danlos type IV, coarctation, fibromuscular dysplasia, smoking, hypertension.
- Nimodipine 60 mg 4-hourly for 21 days reduces delayed cerebral ischaemia from vasospasm (which peaks at days 4–14) — it improves outcome without necessarily reversing angiographic spasm. Maintain euvolaemia; “triple-H therapy” is now largely replaced by induced hypertension alone.
- Other complications: rebleeding (the greatest early risk — hence securing the aneurysm within 24–72 hours), hydrocephalus (may need external ventricular drainage), seizures, and hyponatraemia from cerebral salt wasting or SIADH — treat salt wasting with salt and fluid, not restriction.
- Grading: hunt and Hess or WFNS clinically; Fisher grade on CT predicts vasospasm.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The question names the operator (“interventional radiologist”) — that alone selects coiling. Nimodipine is the recurring distractor.

**REMEMBER:** *Coils fill the sac from inside; clips close the neck from outside; nimodipine only protects the brain afterwards.*

### 95. Neck Lumps and Neck Dissection

**PEARL:** A branchial cyst arises at the anterior border of sternomastoid at the junction of the upper and middle third, from a persistent cervical sinus; a congenital branchial fistula opens at the junction of the middle and lower third. The MacFEE incision — two parallel transverse cervical incisions along skin creases with an intervening skin bridge — gives the best cosmesis and the safest flap vascularity for modified radical neck dissection.

**UNDERSTAND:** Neck incisions that create trifurcations over the carotid risk flap-tip necrosis and carotid blowout — especially in irradiated tissue — so MacFee’s parallel design keeps the bridge broadly perfused. Its cost is less exposure, making it unsuitable for bulky fixed nodes.

**MUST KNOW:**

- The branchial cyst derives from 2nd arch mesoderm overgrowing arches 3, 4 and 6; it is lined by squamous epithelium and contains turbid fluid full of cholesterol crystals. Beware: a cystic neck mass in a patient over 40 is a metastatic cystic node from an HPV-related tonsillar or base-of-tongue SCC until proven otherwise.
- Neck dissection incisions: MacFEE (two transverse), SCHOBINGER (trifurcate flap), hayes Martin (double-Y), APRON, HOCKEY-stick.
- Radical neck dissection removes levels I–V plus the sternocleidomastoid, internal jugular vein and spinal accessory nerve. The modified radical dissection preserves one or more of these three (type I preserves the accessory nerve) — preserving the accessory nerve prevents shoulder syndrome.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Two transverse creases → MacFee. Site of the cyst versus the fistula is a separate recall item.

**REMEMBER:** *Cyst at the upper third, fistula at the lower third; two transverse creases is MacFee.*

### 99. Bladder-Drained Pancreas Transplant

**PEARL:** Draining exocrine pancreatic secretions into the bladder loses bicarbonate in the urine → metabolic acidosis (plus chemical cystitis, reflux pancreatitis, urethritis and prostatic abscess, and dehydration from the obligate fluid loss). The exam TRAP IS “metabolic alkalosis” — IT IS acidosis.

**UNDERSTAND:** The pancreas secretes 1–2 L/day of alkaline, enzyme-rich fluid. Divert that into the bladder and you are effectively running a continuous bicarbonate drain, while exposing urothelium to activated proteolytic enzymes it was never designed to meet.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Losing bicarbonate from below (bladder, diarrhoea, fistula) causes a normal anion GAP (hyperchloraemic) acidosis; losing acid from above (vomiting) causes alkalosis. The same logic explains the acidosis of high-output ileostomy, pancreatic fistula, ureterosigmoidostomy and proximal RTA.
- Enteric drainage IS now standard — more physiological, and it avoids all these urological complications. Bladder drainage survives mainly because urinary amylase can be measured as an early marker of rejection.
- Simultaneous pancreas–kidney (SPK) transplant is the operation of choice in type 1 diabetes with ESRD; it gives insulin independence and stabilises or improves secondary complications.
- Bicarbonate losses may need oral sodium bicarbonate; up to 25% eventually require enteric conversion.
- Liver transplantation for secondary (metastatic) tumours is exceptional — essentially only for neuroendocrine metastases and, in some centres, selected metastatic germ cell tumours with a controlled primary and liver-only disease. Melanoma, breast and active renal cell carcinoma are contraindications.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Not a complication of bladder drainage of a transplanted pancreas” → metabolic alkalosis.

**REMEMBER:** *Alkaline juice down the drain = acid left behind.*

### 100. The Dentate Line and Inguinal Node Drainage

**PEARL:** The anal canal below the dentate line drains to the superficial inguinal nodes — so anal carcinoma presents with inguinal lymphadenopathy. Above the dentate line, drainage is to the internal iliac and inferior mesenteric nodes. Testicular tumours drain to para-aortic nodes, not inguinal.

**UNDERSTAND:** The dentate line is an embryological watershed — endoderm (hindgut) above, ectoderm (proctodeum) below — and lymphatic drainage follows embryology, not geography. Everything below drains like skin; everything above drains like gut. By the same rule, the testis drains where it came from (the posterior abdominal wall), taking its lymphatics with it as it descends — so inguinal nodes are involved only if the scrotal skin is breached, which is exactly why orchidectomy for tumour uses the inguinal, not scrotal, approach.

**MUST KNOW:**

- The whole dentate line rule — above: columnar epithelium, visceral (painless) sensation, superior rectal vein (portal), internal iliac nodes, adenocarcinoma. Below: squamous, somatic (exquisitely painful), inferior rectal vein (systemic), inguinal nodes, squamous cell carcinoma.
- Practical corollary: internal haemorrhoids are painless; an anal fissure or thrombosed external pile is agonising.
- Everything else below the dentate line/perineal skin drains inguinally: anal margin, vulva, penis and scrotal skin, lower vagina, and the skin of the buttock, lower abdominal wall and lower limb.
- Anal SCC is strongly HPV-related and treated primarily with chemoradiation (nigro regimen), not surgery.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Enlarged inguinal node — most likely primary?” → anal cancer; the intended trap is testicular cancer.

**REMEMBER:** *Below the dentate line drains like skin; the testis drains where it came from.*

### 101. FDG-PET and Neuroendocrine Tumours

**PEARL:** Typical (well-differentiated, low-grade) carcinoid IS not FDG-avid — it is imaged with somatostatin receptor scintigraphy (⁶⁸Ga-DOTATATE PET or octreotide scan). Atypical carcinoid, large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma and small cell carcinoma are FDG-avid.

**UNDERSTAND:** FDG-PET measures glucose consumption, which tracks proliferation and dedifferentiation. A well-differentiated tumour with a low Ki-67 divides slowly and consumes little glucose, so it is invisible on FDG-PET — but it retains its differentiated feature, abundant somatostatin receptors, which is what DOTATATE binds. As grade rises the tumour loses somatostatin receptors and gains glycolytic activity — so the two scans are inversely informative, and a tumour that is DOTATATE-negative but FDG-positive is high-grade with a poor prognosis (the “flip-flop” phenomenon).

**MUST KNOW:**

- Other FDG-PET false negatives: well-differentiated hepatocellular carcinoma, renal cell carcinoma (and the urinary tract generally, because FDG is excreted in urine), prostate cancer (use PSMA-PET), mucinous adenocarcinoma, bronchioloalveolar/lepidic adenocarcinoma, and lesions <8–10 mm. False positives: infection, granulomatous disease (tuberculosis, sarcoidosis), inflammation, post-surgical change, brown fat, and physiological brain/myocardial/bowel uptake.
- Patient preparation: fast 4–6 hours, control blood glucose (hyperglycaemia competes with FDG and reduces tumour uptake), rest and keep warm to minimise muscle and brown-fat uptake.
- Carcinoid tumours: commonest sites are the small bowel (ileum) and appendix; they secrete serotonin. Carcinoid syndrome (flushing, diarrhoea, wheeze, right-SIDED valvular fibrosis — tricuspid regurgitation and pulmonary stenosis) requires either liver metastases or an EXTRAPORTAL primary (bronchial, ovarian), because the liver otherwise inactivates serotonin. Diagnose with urinary 5-HIAA and chromogranin A; treat with octreotide — and give octreotide before surgery to prevent carcinoid crisis.
- THERANOSTICS: ⁶⁸Ga-DOTATATE for imaging, ¹⁷⁷Lu-DOTATATE (PRRT) for treatment — the same receptor used to find and to treat.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** “Which will not be FDG-avid?” — the answer is the best-differentiated, slowest-growing tumour.

**REMEMBER:** *Slow, well-differentiated tumours ignore FDG — find them with somatostatin receptors instead.*

### 102. Surgical Numbers Worth Memorising

**PEARL:** Jenkins’ rule — suture length should be four times the wound length for closing a midline laparotomy (1 cm bites, 1 cm apart, mass closure, continuous, slowly absorbable monofilament). French (Fr) gauge = outer circumference in mm ≈ 3 × outer diameter — so Fr measures the outer diameter (1 Fr = 0.33 mm), and a larger Fr number means a wider catheter.

**UNDERSTAND:** Jenkins’ 4:1 ratio exists because abdominal wound dehiscence is a problem of suture cutting through tissue, not of suture breakage. Too little suture length means bites too far apart or tied too tightly, so the tissue strangulates and the suture cheese-wires through; the 4:1 ratio guarantees enough length for closely spaced, appropriately loose bites that distribute tension. Sutures should be approximated, not strangulated.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Burst abdomen (dehiscence) typically occurs around day 5–8, heralded by a serosanguinous (“salmon-pink”) discharge; risk factors — poor technique, raised intra-abdominal pressure (cough, ileus), malnutrition, sepsis, steroids, diabetes, obesity, jaundice, malignancy. Management: cover with saline-soaked packs, resuscitate, and RESUTURE IN theatre.
- Typical adult urinary catheter 14–16 Fr; a three-way irrigating catheter (20–22 Fr) for haematuria and clot retention.
- NG (Ryle’s) tube: insert with the conscious patient sitting and the neck flexed (flexion closes the airway and opens the oesophagus; extension does the opposite and risks tracheal intubation). Confirm position by aspirating gastric contents with pH testing and, definitively, by X-ray — auscultation of an air bolus is unreliable. Approximate marks: 40 cm cardia, 50 cm body, 60 cm pylorus, 70 cm duodenum.
- ATLS sequence: cervical spine stabilisation and airway first, then breathing, then circulation (IV access, fluids/blood), then disability and exposure — CT only after the primary survey and only in a stabilised patient.
- Frozen section is used intraoperatively for margin assessment, sentinel node status and confirming metastasis — it is not a substitute for definitive paraffin-section diagnosis (fat, calcified and small lesions are unreliable).
- Insulinoma is the commonest functioning pancreatic neuroendocrine tumour (Whipple’s triad; 90% benign, solitary and intrapancreatic); gastrinoma is the commonest functioning PNET IN MEN-1, and in MEN-1 gastrinomas are most often duodenal and multiple.

**exam connection:** Pure recall one-liners: 4:1 suture rule, Fr = outer diameter, sitting with neck flexed, insulinoma commonest PNET.

**REMEMBER:** *Four times the wound, outer diameter in French, chin down for the Ryle’s tube.*

### 103. Duke Criteria for Infective Endocarditis (2023 Duke-ISCVID)

**PEARL:** Two separate blood cultures growing a typical organism (viridans streptococci, *S. Aureus*, *S. Gallolyticus*, HACEK, or community-acquired enterococcus with no primary focus) is a major criterion.

**UNDERSTAND:** The criteria are built to avoid a false conclusion from a single observation. A single positive culture with a skin commensal (coagulase-negative staphylococci) almost always means contamination, whereas persistent bacteraemia with an organism that has a genuine affinity for the endocardium is meaningful — which is why the criteria specify both the organism and the number of cultures.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Major criteria: (1) typical organism in two separate cultures, or persistently positive cultures, or a single positive culture for coxiella burnetii / phase I IgG >1:800; (2) evidence of endocardial involvement — vegetation, abscess, new valve dehiscence, or new valvular regurgitation.
- Minor: predisposition, fever >38 °C, vascular phenomena (Janeway lesions, emboli, mycotic aneurysm), immunological phenomena (Osler nodes, Roth spots, glomerulonephritis, rheumatoid factor), and microbiological evidence not meeting a major criterion.
- Definite = 2 major, or 1 major + 3 minor, or 5 minor.
- Viridans streptococci (oral flora) cause subacute endocarditis on an abnormal valve (mitral valve prolapse, rheumatic disease); *S. Aureus* causes acute endocarditis on normal valves and in IV drug users (tricuspid); coagulase-negative staphylococci → prosthetic valves; *S. Gallolyticus* (bovis) → look for colonic carcinoma; HACEK → culture-negative endocarditis.
- Take three sets of blood cultures from separate sites before antibiotics; toe is more sensitive than TTE.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Check both the organism and the number of cultures. A single culture only counts for Coxiella.

**2023 Duke-ISCVID update:** The requirement that blood cultures be drawn from separate VENIPUNCTURES AT spaced times has been removed. New major microbiological evidence: PCR, amplicon/metagenomic sequencing, in-situ hybridisation, and *Bartonella* enzyme immunoassay. New major imaging evidence: ¹⁸F-FDG PET/CT and cardiac CT. Intraoperative inspection of the valve is a new major clinical criterion. Predisposing conditions now explicitly include TRANSCATHETER valve implants, cardiac implantable electronic devices and previous endocarditis, and the list of “typical” organisms is expanded, some counting as typical only with an intracardiac prosthesis.[^6]

**REMEMBER:** *Two cultures, typical organism — that’s a major criterion; since 2023, PET/CT, PCR and what the surgeon sees at operation count as major too.*

### 104. Addisonian Crisis After Steroid Withdrawal

**PEARL:** Hypotension, hypoglycaemia, hyperkalaemia and hyponatraemia after abrupt withdrawal of long-term steroids = Addisonian crisis — treat with IV hydrocortisone 100 mg plus fluid resuscitation with normal saline, started before biochemical confirmation.

**MUST KNOW:** Long-term exogenous steroids suppress the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, so the adrenal cannot mount a stress response when the drug stops or when surgery, sepsis or trauma intervenes. Never stop long-term steroids abruptly, and give perioperative stress-dose cover.

**REMEMBER:** *Low sodium, high potassium, low sugar, low pressure — give hydrocortisone first and ask questions later.*
