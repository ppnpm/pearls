# A. Head, Neck & Special Senses

## 1. Optic Canal

**PEARL:** The optic canal lies **between the two roots of the lesser wing of the sphenoid** and transmits the **optic nerve and the ophthalmic artery** — the structures damaged when it fractures.

**MUST KNOW:** The optic nerve here is surrounded by **all three meninges** (hence papilloedema and the risk in raised ICP). The **superior orbital fissure** is the neighbouring opening — transmitting **III, IV, V1, VI and the superior ophthalmic vein**; damage there gives **complete ophthalmoplegia with a fixed dilated pupil (SOF syndrome)**, and if the optic nerve is also involved it becomes **orbital apex syndrome**.

**REMEMBER:** *Optic canal = optic nerve + ophthalmic artery, between the two roots of the lesser wing.*

---

## 2. Corneal Sensation

**PEARL:** The cornea is supplied by the **long ciliary nerves**, branches of the **nasociliary nerve (from V1, ophthalmic division)** — the **afferent limb of the corneal reflex**.

**MUST KNOW:** The **efferent** limb is the **facial nerve** (orbicularis oculi). So an absent blink with **intact sensation on the other side** localizes to VII; **absent sensation** localizes to V1. The cornea is the **most densely innervated tissue in the body**.

**REMEMBER:** *Corneal reflex: in through V1, out through VII.*

---

## 3. Retinal Thickness

**PEARL:** The retina is **thinnest at the ora serrata (0.08 mm)** and **thickest at the foveal rim (~0.23–0.32 mm)**.

**MUST KNOW:** The **fovea centralis itself is thin** (the inner layers are swept aside) — it is the **rim/parafoveal region** that is thickest. The thin ora serrata is where **retinal breaks and detachment** commonly begin.

**REMEMBER:** *Thinnest at the ora (where it tears), thickest at the foveal rim.*

---

## 4. Eye — Basic Dimensions and Development

**PEARL:**
- **AP diameter of the adult eye = 24 mm (2.4 cm).**
- The **canal of Schlemm appears by the 4th month** of gestation.
- **Full visual acuity (6/6) is attained by ~6 years** of age.
- The **choroid is derived from neural crest cells**.

**MUST KNOW:** Failure of proper development of the angle/Schlemm's canal → **congenital glaucoma (buphthalmos)**. Amblyopia must be treated before visual maturity is complete — hence the urgency of treating squint/cataract in early childhood.

**REMEMBER:** *24 mm eye, Schlemm at 4 months, adult vision by 6 years, choroid from neural crest.*

---

## 5. Tarsal Plates

**PEARL:** The tarsal plates are **NOT cartilage** — they are firm, dense **fibrous connective tissue**.

**MUST KNOW:** They contain the **Meibomian (tarsal) glands** — modified sebaceous glands whose blockage causes a **chalazion**. The **superior tarsal muscle (Müller's)** inserts on the upper tarsal plate.

**REMEMBER:** *Tarsal plate = fibrous, not cartilage; it houses the Meibomian glands.*

---

## 6. Horner Syndrome — Ptosis

**PEARL:** Ptosis in Horner syndrome is due to paralysis of the **superior tarsal muscle (Müller's muscle)**, which is **sympathetically innervated** (fibres originating around **T1**).

**UNDERSTAND:** Because Müller's muscle contributes only ~2 mm of lid elevation, Horner's ptosis is **partial** — unlike the complete ptosis of a third nerve palsy (levator palpebrae superioris, supplied by CN III).

**MUST KNOW:** Full triad: **partial ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis** (± apparent enophthalmos and "upside-down ptosis" of the lower lid). Causes: **Pancoast tumour (T1 root)**, carotid dissection, brainstem stroke (lateral medullary), neck surgery/trauma.

**REMEMBER:** *Partial ptosis + small pupil = sympathetic (Müller's); complete ptosis + big pupil = CN III.*

---

## 7. Whitnall's Tubercle

**PEARL:** The **check ligament of the lateral rectus** attaches to **Whitnall's (lateral orbital) tubercle**.

**MUST KNOW:** The tubercle lies on the **zygomatic bone**, ~1 cm below the frontozygomatic suture, and is also the attachment for the **lateral palpebral ligament, the lateral horn of the levator aponeurosis, and Lockwood's suspensory ligament**.

**REMEMBER:** *Whitnall's tubercle is the lateral anchor of the orbit.*

---

## 8. Blowout Fracture

**PEARL:** **Severe ocular damage is RARE** in a blowout fracture.

**UNDERSTAND:** The thin **orbital floor (maxillary) and medial wall (lamina papyracea)** give way first, acting as a pressure-release valve that **protects the globe** — the fracture is protective, not just destructive.

**MUST KNOW:** Features: **enophthalmos, diplopia on upgaze from inferior rectus/orbital fat entrapment, infraorbital nerve anaesthesia**, and a **teardrop sign** on coronal CT. A child with a "white-eyed" **trapdoor** fracture and oculocardiac reflex (nausea, bradycardia) needs **urgent** surgery.

**REMEMBER:** *The floor breaks so the eye doesn't — look for upgaze diplopia and cheek numbness.*

---

## 9. Incudomalleolar Joint

**PEARL:** The **incudomalleolar joint is a saddle joint**.

**MUST KNOW:** The **incudostapedial joint is a ball-and-socket** joint. Both are **synovial** joints — among the very few in the head. Ossicular chain gives a **lever advantage**, part of the middle ear's ~22:1 impedance matching.

**REMEMBER:** *Malleus–incus = saddle; incus–stapes = ball and socket.*

---

## 10. Labyrinthine Artery

**PEARL:** The labyrinthine (internal auditory) artery is most commonly a branch of the **AICA (anterior inferior cerebellar artery)**, occasionally directly from the **basilar**.

**MUST KNOW:** It is an **end artery** — hence **AICA occlusion causes sudden ipsilateral deafness and vertigo**, along with facial palsy and ipsilateral facial sensory loss (lateral pontine syndrome). This distinguishes AICA from **PICA** (lateral medullary/Wallenberg), which spares hearing.

**REMEMBER:** *AICA feeds the inner ear — AICA stroke makes you deaf, PICA doesn't.*

---

## 11. Hypoglossal Canal vs Jugular Foramen

**PEARL:** The **hypoglossal nerve (XII)** has its **own canal**; **IX, X and XI** pass through the **jugular foramen**.

**MUST KNOW:** Jugular foramen also transmits the **internal jugular vein** and the inferior petrosal sinus; its compression = **Vernet syndrome** (IX, X, XI palsies). Add XII and it becomes **Collet–Sicard syndrome**.

**REMEMBER:** *9, 10, 11 through the jugular foramen; 12 goes alone.*

---

## 12. Genioglossus

**PEARL:** Genioglossus arises from the **upper genial tubercle of the mandible**; **unilateral paralysis makes the tongue deviate TOWARDS the paralysed side** on protrusion.

**UNDERSTAND:** Genioglossus protrudes the tongue by pulling it forward on its own side. With one side paralysed, the normal side pushes across unopposed — so the tip swings towards the weak side.

**MUST KNOW:** Genioglossus is also the **safety muscle of the tongue** — its paralysis (bilateral) causes the tongue to fall back and obstruct the airway. **Upper motor neuron** lesions deviate the tongue **away** from the lesion (towards the hemiplegic side); **lower motor neuron/XII nerve** lesions deviate **towards** the lesion, with wasting and fasciculation.

**REMEMBER:** *The tongue points to the lesion (LMN) — "lick your wounds".*

---

## 13. Risorius

**PEARL:** The **risorius** (the "grinning" muscle) is supplied by the **facial nerve (CN VII)** — as are all muscles of facial expression.

**MUST KNOW:** VII supplies all **second pharyngeal arch** muscles: facial expression, **stapedius, stylohyoid, posterior belly of digastric**. Muscles of mastication (first arch) are supplied by **V3**.

**REMEMBER:** *Every facial expression muscle = VII (second arch).*

---

## 14. Great Auricular Nerve

**PEARL:** Formed by **C2 and C3** (cervical plexus) — sensory to the skin over the **ear and parotid region**.

**MUST KNOW:** It is the nerve commonly **sacrificed in parotidectomy** (numb earlobe), and is a favourite **nerve graft donor**. Its involvement is a classic sign of **thickened nerves in leprosy**. **Frey's syndrome** (gustatory sweating after parotid surgery) involves aberrant auriculotemporal fibres, not this nerve.

**REMEMBER:** *C2–C3, over the parotid — numb ear after parotidectomy.*

---

## 15. Inferior Thyroid Artery

**PEARL:** A branch of the **thyrocervical trunk** (from the **first part of the subclavian artery**). It ascends anterior to the medial border of **scalenus anterior**, turns medially just below the **C6 transverse process**, and descends on **longus colli** to the lower pole of the thyroid.

**MUST KNOW:** It has an intimate relation to the **recurrent laryngeal nerve** — ligate it **away from the gland (laterally)** to spare the nerve; the superior thyroid artery is ligated **close to the gland** to spare the **external laryngeal nerve** (the "nerve of Amelita Galli-Curci", supplying cricothyroid). The **C6 landmark** is also where the carotid tubercle (Chassaignac) lies.

**REMEMBER:** *Inferior thyroid artery + recurrent laryngeal nerve — tie it far from the gland.*

---

## 16. Levator Scapulae

**PEARL:** Innervated by the **dorsal scapular nerve (C5)** — plus direct C3, C4 branches; strain causes posture-related neck pain and stiffness.

**MUST KNOW:** The dorsal scapular nerve arises from the **root of C5** (before the trunks) and also supplies the **rhomboids**; its injury causes **lateral winging of the scapula** (versus the medial winging of long thoracic nerve/serratus anterior palsy).

**REMEMBER:** *Dorsal scapular = C5 root = rhomboids + levator scapulae = lateral winging.*

---

## 17. Sibson's Fascia

**PEARL:** The **suprapleural membrane** — attached to the **inner border of the first rib**, covering the **apex of the lung**; the **subclavian vessels pass above/over it**.

**MUST KNOW:** It arises from the transverse process of **C7** and protects the apex against pressure changes during respiration. Its presence explains why the lung apex extends into the **root of the neck**, above the clavicle — hence lung apex injury in neck stab wounds and **Pancoast tumours** invading the brachial plexus and sympathetic chain.

**REMEMBER:** *Sibson's fascia is the roof over the lung apex, from C7 to the first rib.*

# B. Thorax, Abdomen & Pelvis

## 18. Aspiration — Which Segments

**PEARL:** In a **recumbent** patient, aspiration goes to the **posterior segment of the right upper lobe** and the **superior (apical) segment of the lower lobes**.

**UNDERSTAND:** The **right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical**, so material preferentially enters it; gravity in the supine position then directs it into the segments that are **most dependent when lying down**.

**MUST KNOW:** In the **upright/sitting** position it goes to the **basal segments of the lower lobes** (right more than left). Aspiration pneumonia/abscess is typically **polymicrobial with anaerobes**.

**REMEMBER:** *Lying down → posterior upper and superior lower segments, right side.*

---

## 19. Third Part of the Duodenum

**PEARL:** The third (horizontal) part of the duodenum crosses at the **L3 level**.

**MUST KNOW:** It is crossed anteriorly by the **superior mesenteric vessels** — compression between the SMA and the aorta causes **superior mesenteric artery (Wilkie's) syndrome**, classically in rapid weight loss (loss of the fat pad narrowing the aortomesenteric angle). Duodenal levels: **1st = L1, 2nd = L1–L3 (receives the ampulla), 3rd = L3, 4th = L2** (ends at the duodenojejunal flexure, held by the **ligament of Treitz**).

**REMEMBER:** *Third part at L3, squeezed by the SMA.*

---

## 20. Internal Iliac Artery — Branches

**PEARL:** **Anterior division:** inferior gluteal, superior vesical, inferior vesical, middle rectal, vaginal (and obturator, internal pudendal, uterine, umbilical). **The SUPERIOR gluteal artery comes from the POSTERIOR division.**

**MUST KNOW:** The posterior division has only three branches — **iliolumbar, lateral sacral, superior gluteal**. **Both gluteal arteries are the trap**: superior = posterior division, **inferior = anterior division**.

**REMEMBER:** *Posterior division = "I Love Sex" (Iliolumbar, Lateral sacral, Superior gluteal) — everything else is anterior.*

---

## 21. Prostate Volume

**PEARL:** Normal adult prostate volume ≈ **20 cc (20 g)**; enlargement beyond this suggests **BPH**.

**MUST KNOW:** Volume is measured by **transrectal ultrasound** (ellipsoid formula). Volume >30–40 cc predicts better response to **5α-reductase inhibitors**; the zone of BPH is the **transitional zone**, whereas **carcinoma arises in the peripheral zone** (hence palpable per rectum).

**REMEMBER:** *20 cc normal; BPH in the transitional zone, cancer in the peripheral zone.*

---

## 22. Vulva — What It Includes

**PEARL:** **The cervix is NOT part of the vulva.**

**MUST KNOW:** The vulva (external genitalia) = **mons pubis, labia majora, labia minora, clitoris, vestibule, vestibular bulbs, Bartholin's and Skene's glands, and the hymen**. The vagina and cervix are **internal** genitalia.

**REMEMBER:** *Vulva stops at the hymen.*

# C. Limbs

## 23. Arches of the Foot

**PEARL:**
- **Medial longitudinal arch:** **calcaneus (medial half), talus, navicular, three cuneiforms, and the first three metatarsals**.
- **Lateral longitudinal arch:** **calcaneus (lateral half), cuboid, and the 4th and 5th metatarsals**.
- **The phalanges take no part in the arches** — toes can be amputated without losing arch function.

**MUST KNOW:** The **medial arch is higher and more mobile** (shock absorption); the lateral is low and rigid (weight transmission). Chief supports: **plantar aponeurosis, spring (plantar calcaneonavicular) ligament, and tibialis posterior** — tibialis posterior dysfunction is the commonest cause of acquired **flat foot**.

**REMEMBER:** *Medial = talus + navicular + cuneiforms + 3 metatarsals; lateral = cuboid + last 2. Toes don't count.*

---

## 24. Talus and Navicular

**PEARL:** The **talus** is the uppermost foot bone — articulating with **tibia and fibula above, calcaneus below**, and its **head with the navicular** in front. The **navicular** is boat-shaped and is a landmark for the **dorsalis pedis artery**.

**MUST KNOW:** **The talus has no muscular attachments** and a **retrograde blood supply** → high risk of **avascular necrosis** after fracture (Hawkins classification). The navicular articulates with the **three cuneiforms**.

**REMEMBER:** *Talus: no muscles attached, blood comes backwards, dies after fracture.*

---

## 25. Deltoid and the Axillary Nerve

**PEARL:** The **deltoid** gives the shoulder its rounded contour; **axillary nerve** injury causes wasting → a **flat, flabby, asymmetrical shoulder** with loss of sensation over the **regimental badge area (upper lateral arm)**.

**MUST KNOW:**
- Axillary nerve (C5, C6) is injured in **surgical neck fracture of the humerus** and **shoulder dislocation** — always test the badge area before and after reduction.
- **In deltoid pyomyositis, abduction, flexion and extension are all impaired** (anterior, middle and posterior fibres) **but sensation is preserved** — the muscle is diseased, the nerve is not. Sensory loss over the **LOWER lateral arm** is from the **radial nerve**, not the axillary.
- Abduction: **supraspinatus 0–15°, deltoid 15–90°**, then scapular rotation (trapezius + serratus anterior).

**REMEMBER:** *Axillary nerve = deltoid + regimental badge (upper lateral arm only).*

---

## 26. Bipartite Patella

**PEARL:** The accessory ossification centre producing a bipartite patella is most commonly at the **superolateral angle**.

**MUST KNOW:** Present in ~2–8%, usually **bilateral (a clue that it is not a fracture)**, with **smooth rounded sclerotic margins** — unlike the sharp irregular edges of an acute fracture. Usually asymptomatic and an incidental finding.

**REMEMBER:** *Superolateral, smooth and bilateral = bipartite, not broken.*

---

## 27. Kanavel's Signs

**PEARL:** **Kanavel's signs indicate (flexor) tenosynovitis** of the hand.

**MUST KNOW:** The four cardinal signs: **(1) finger held in slight flexion, (2) fusiform (sausage) swelling of the whole digit, (3) tenderness along the flexor tendon sheath, (4) severe pain on passive extension** — the earliest and most reliable sign. It is a **surgical emergency** — needs drainage and antibiotics to prevent tendon necrosis.

**REMEMBER:** *Sausage finger, held flexed, agony on passive extension = Kanavel.*

# D. Neuroanatomy & Histology

## 28. Spinal Cord Arterial Syndromes

**PEARL:**
- **Anterior spinal artery** occlusion → loss of **pain, temperature, crude touch** (spinothalamic) and motor function, with **fine touch/proprioception/vibration preserved** (dorsal columns spared).
- **Posterior spinal artery** occlusion → loss of **proprioception, fine touch and vibration** (dorsal columns).

**MUST KNOW:** The **single** anterior spinal artery supplies the **anterior two-thirds** of the cord — hence anterior spinal artery syndrome is far commoner and more devastating; the **two** posterior spinal arteries supply the posterior third and have richer collaterals. The watershed zone is around **T4–T8**, and the **artery of Adamkiewicz** (usually T9–T12, left) is at risk in aortic surgery.

**REMEMBER:** *Anterior = motor + pain/temperature lost, vibration kept. Posterior = the opposite.*

---

## 29. Dejerine Syndrome (Medial Medullary Syndrome)

**PEARL:** Occlusion of the **anterior spinal artery** (or vertebral) supplying the **medial medulla** → **contralateral hemiplegia, contralateral loss of proprioception/fine touch, and IPSILATERAL tongue deviation** (CN XII).

**UNDERSTAND:** Three medial structures, three deficits: **pyramid** (contralateral hemiplegia), **medial lemniscus** (contralateral dorsal column loss), **hypoglossal nucleus/nerve** (ipsilateral tongue weakness — deviating towards the lesion).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Contrast **lateral medullary (Wallenberg, PICA)**: ipsilateral facial pain/temperature loss with contralateral body loss, **Horner syndrome, dysphagia/hoarseness (IX, X), vertigo, ataxia — and NO hemiplegia**.

**REMEMBER:** *Medial = motor + medial lemniscus + XII (all the "M"s). Lateral = everything else, no weakness.*

---

## 30. Osmium Tetroxide

**PEARL:** **Osmium tetroxide is reduced to a black substance by the unsaturated fatty acids in the myelin sheath** — so myelin appears **black**.

**MUST KNOW:** Used to demonstrate myelinated nerve fibres and as a **fixative/stain for lipids in electron microscopy** (it is also an electron-dense heavy metal, adding contrast). Other myelin stains: **Weigert–Pal, Luxol fast blue**; **osmium is the one that turns fat black**.

**REMEMBER:** *Osmium blackens fat — hence black myelin.*
