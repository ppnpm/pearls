## General Surgical Principles — Wound Healing, Sutures, Incisions and Asepsis

### 1. Pneumoperitoneum: Causes and Non-Causes

**PEARL:** Free gas under the diaphragm means a **perforated viscus or an iatrogenic cause** — **acute intestinal obstruction does NOT cause pneumoperitoneum** (until it perforates).

**UNDERSTAND:** Gas enters the peritoneal cavity only when the bowel wall is breached or gas is deliberately introduced. Obstruction distends bowel *from within* — the gas stays intraluminal, which is why the film shows dilated loops and air–fluid levels rather than a crescent of free air.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Causes: **perforated duodenal/gastric ulcer** (commonest surgical cause), any bowel perforation, **post-laparoscopy** (residual CO₂, benign for ~3–7 days), **peritoneal dialysis**, post-laparotomy.
- Best film: **erect chest X-ray** — detects as little as **1–2 mL** of free gas; if the patient can't sit up, use a **left lateral decubitus** film (gas over the liver).
- **Absence of free air does not exclude perforation** (~30% of perforations have none) → **CECT** is the most sensitive test.
- **Small bowel obstruction:** **valvulae conniventes** cross the full lumen diameter, central loops, **step-ladder/concertina** pattern. **Large bowel obstruction:** **haustra** are irregular and do NOT cross the full width, peripheral loops.
- Beware **Chilaiditi sign** — colon interposed between liver and diaphragm mimicking free air (haustra visible within the "gas").

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Classic "all of the following cause this X-ray appearance EXCEPT" with obstruction as the answer; or an image of gas under both domes asking the **diagnosis** or the **next step (erect CXR / laparotomy)**.

**REMEMBER:** *Obstruction fills the bowel; perforation empties it into the peritoneum.*

### 2. Surgical Wound Classification

**PEARL:** **Class I CLEAN** — no viscus opened, no inflammation (hernia repair, breast biopsy). **Class II CLEAN-CONTAMINATED** — a viscus opened under controlled conditions without significant spillage (elective cholecystectomy, elective colon resection with prep). **Class III CONTAMINATED** — fresh accidental wounds, a major break in sterile technique, gross GI spillage, or incision through inflamed but non-purulent tissue — **OPEN CARDIAC MASSAGE and penetrating abdominal trauma belong here.** **Class IV DIRTY** — pus, perforated viscus, or old traumatic wounds with devitalised tissue.

**UNDERSTAND:** The classification predicts the **expected bacterial load at the time of closure**, which is what determines infection risk and antibiotic strategy. **Open cardiac massage is class III because emergency thoracotomy is performed in uncontrolled conditions with an inevitable breach of sterility**, not because the chest is a contaminated cavity — the classification grades **circumstances**, not anatomy.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **SSI rates: clean 1–2% (unchanged by prophylaxis); clean-contaminated 6–9% → 3% with prophylaxis; contaminated 13–20% → 6%; dirty 40% → 7%.**
- **Antibiotic prophylaxis: a single dose given WITHIN 60 MINUTES before the incision (120 minutes for vancomycin/fluoroquinolones), redosed for long operations or major blood loss; it is PROPHYLAXIS, not treatment — continuing it beyond 24 hours does not reduce SSI and breeds resistance.** Dirty wounds need **therapeutic** antibiotics.
- Other measures that genuinely reduce SSI: **normothermia, glycaemic control, supplemental oxygen, appropriate hair removal with CLIPPERS not razors (and only if necessary), chlorhexidine–alcohol skin preparation, and NOT closing a dirty wound primarily (delayed primary closure at 4–5 days).**
- **SSI classification: superficial incisional (skin and subcutaneous), deep incisional (fascia and muscle), and organ/space** — all within **30 days** of surgery, or **90 days if an implant** is placed.
- Patient risk factors: **diabetes, smoking, obesity, malnutrition/hypoalbuminaemia, immunosuppression, remote infection, and prolonged preoperative stay** — the basis of the **NNIS risk index** (ASA >2, contaminated/dirty wound, operation exceeding the 75th percentile duration).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Match the scenario to the class. Emergency procedures in uncontrolled settings (**open cardiac massage, penetrating trauma**) are **contaminated (III)**, not dirty — dirty requires **pus or established perforation**.

**REMEMBER:** *Controlled entry is clean-contaminated; uncontrolled entry is contaminated; pus is dirty.*

### 3. Surgical Knots and Adenoviral Haemorrhagic Cystitis

**PEARL:** The **SURGEON'S KNOT is a reef (square) knot whose FIRST THROW has a DOUBLE WRAP**, which stops the first throw slipping while the second is tied. A **GRANNY knot has both throws in the SAME direction (it slips); a REEF knot has them in OPPOSITE directions (the standard secure knot); a HALF-HITCH is a single throw.** Separately: **acute HAEMORRHAGIC CYSTITIS in a child is caused by ADENOVIRUS (types 11 and 21).**

**UNDERSTAND:** Knot security depends on **friction versus tension**. In a reef knot the two throws lie in opposite directions, so they lock against each other; in a granny knot they lie the same way and simply slide — which is why a granny knot is used deliberately when you *want* to tighten tissue, and is then **locked with a reef throw**. The surgeon's knot adds **a second wrap in the first throw to multiply the friction**, holding the tissue apposed during the vulnerable moment before the second throw is laid — essential in deep cavities and under tension.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Every knot must be laid SQUARE and tightened without sawing or "cheese-wiring"; monofilament sutures (nylon, polypropylene) have MEMORY and low friction, so they need MORE throws (5–6) than braided sutures (3–4).**
- **Adenovirus: a NON-ENVELOPED, double-stranded DNA virus (icosahedral, with fibre projections). Disease by serotype: 1–7 pharyngoconjunctival fever and respiratory illness; 3 and 7 PHARYNGOCONJUNCTIVAL FEVER (swimming pools); 8, 19, 37 EPIDEMIC KERATOCONJUNCTIVITIS ("shipyard eye"); 11 and 21 ACUTE HAEMORRHAGIC CYSTITIS; 40 and 41 infantile GASTROENTERITIS.** It is also a common cause of **fatal disseminated infection in transplant recipients**, and is the vector backbone used in several vaccines.
- **Haemorrhagic cystitis in children: adenovirus (self-limiting, supportive care with hydration); other causes — CYCLOPHOSPHAMIDE/ifosfamide (acrolein metabolite; prevent with hydration and MESNA), BK virus after bone-marrow transplant, radiation, and schistosomiasis.**
- **Commonest cause of UTI in children overall remains *E. coli***; a child with a febrile UTI needs **ultrasound**, and further imaging (**MCU for reflux, DMSA for scarring**) depending on age and recurrence.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Two independent recall items — the **double first throw** identifies the surgeon's knot, and **haemorrhagic cystitis in a child** points to adenovirus.

**REMEMBER:** *Double the first throw so it cannot slip; adenovirus 11 and 21 bleed the bladder.*

### 4. Intra-abdominal Abscess — Commonest Site

**PEARL:** The **RIGHT SUBHEPATIC SPACE (Rutherford Morison's pouch)** is the commonest site of an intra-abdominal abscess.

**UNDERSTAND:** It is the **most dependent part of the peritoneal cavity in the supine patient**, so infected fluid from appendicitis, a perforated duodenal ulcer or cholecystitis gravitates there. Its position **downstream of the right paracolic gutter**, which acts as a channel from the pelvis and right iliac fossa, makes it the natural collecting point.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Boundaries: **liver and diaphragm above and to the right, transverse colon and hepatic flexure below, foramen of Winslow medially, duodenum inferiorly, right kidney posteriorly**.
- Common sources: **appendicitis, perforated duodenal ulcer, cholecystitis, and postoperative upper abdominal surgery**.
- Other sites: **subphrenic (right and left), left subhepatic (lesser sac), paracolic gutters, and the PELVIS (pouch of Douglas — the most dependent site overall in the erect/semi-recumbent patient)**.
- Presentation: **swinging fever, tachycardia, shoulder tip pain, and "pus somewhere, pus nowhere, pus under the diaphragm"**; investigate with **ultrasound or CT**.
- Treatment: **image-guided percutaneous (pigtail) drainage plus antibiotics**; surgery only if drainage fails or the collection is multiloculated.
- **Bile duct injury after laparoscopic cholecystectomy** presents in the first postoperative days with **fever, pain, jaundice or bile leak**; the correct next step is **percutaneous (pigtail) drainage of the collection plus IV antibiotics**, then define the anatomy with **MRCP/PTC/ERCP** and reconstruct later — never immediate re-exploration with a T-tube.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Labelled anatomical diagram, and the paired clinical question about post-cholecystectomy collections answering **pigtail drainage**.

**REMEMBER:** *Fluid runs down the right gutter and pools under the liver.*

### 5. Wound Healing Timeline

**PEARL:** In the **first week the wound is held by sutures, the haemostatic plug and WEAK TYPE III collagen** — not type I. **Type I collagen replaces type III during weeks 2–3**, and final tensile strength reaches only **60–80% of normal skin**.

**UNDERSTAND:** Early wounds have almost no intrinsic strength because **type III collagen is thin and poorly cross-linked** — laid down fast for scaffolding, not strength. Remodelling then substitutes **thick, cross-linked type I collagen** and **realigns the fibres along lines of stress**. Strength comes from the **weave and cross-linking, not the quantity** of collagen, which is why the scar never regains normal strength.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Phases: **haemostasis (immediate) → inflammation (days 1–5, neutrophils then macrophages) → proliferation (days 5–21: fibroblasts, angiogenesis, granulation tissue, epithelialisation) → REMODELLING (3 weeks to 1–2 years)**.
- Strength milestones: **< 10% at 1 week; ~30% at 3 weeks; ~60–80% at 3 months**, then a plateau. Skin sutures are removed at **5 days (face) to 10–14 days (back and limbs)**.
- **Vitamin C, zinc and oxygen are essential for collagen cross-linking**; **steroids, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, diabetes, malnutrition, jaundice, uraemia, infection and smoking impair healing**.
- **Vitamin A reverses the inhibitory effect of steroids** on healing.
- **Negative pressure wound therapy (VAC)**: intermittent **−125 mmHg** hastens debridement and granulation. Contraindicated with **exposed vessels/organs/grafts, untreated infection or inadequately debrided necrotic tissue, and MALIGNANCY (Marjolin ulcer)**.
- **Southampton wound grading of surgical site infection: 0 normal; I erythema only; II erythema plus other inflammatory signs; III serous or serosanguinous discharge; IV PUS; V deep or severe infection.**

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Which is false" → the statement crediting **type I collagen in the first week**. The other three statements are all true.

**REMEMBER:** *Type three first and weak; type one later and strong.*

### 6. Surgical Risk Scores and Incisions

**PEARL:** **Thoracoscore predicts operative MORTALITY in thoracic surgery** from **9 preoperative variables** — **complications of surgery are not among them** (they are outcomes, not predictors).

**UNDERSTAND:** A risk score must be computable **before the operation**, so every variable has to be a **preoperative patient or procedure characteristic**. Including a postoperative complication would make the score circular and useless for decision-making — which is exactly why the distractor is wrong on principle, without needing the list memorised.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Thoracoscore variables: age, sex, ASA grade, performance status, dyspnoea score, priority of surgery (elective vs urgent), extent of surgery, malignant diagnosis, and composite comorbidity score.**
- Other scores: **ASA** — preoperative physical status; **POSSUM/P-POSSUM** — morbidity and mortality in general surgery; **APACHE II** — severity in critical illness and pancreatitis; **Ranson and BISAP** — pancreatitis; **Child–Pugh and MELD** — liver reserve; **Glasgow-Blatchford and Rockall** — upper GI bleeding; **Alvarado** — appendicitis; **SIRS/qSOFA** — sepsis.
- **Named incisions: Kocher — subcostal (open cholecystectomy); Chevron/rooftop — bilateral subcostal; Lanz — appendicectomy (transverse, cosmetic); Gridiron/McBurney — oblique appendicectomy; Pfannenstiel — suprapubic transverse; Battle — lower right paramedian; Rutherford Morison — muscle-cutting iliac fossa; GIBSON — oblique/curvilinear iliac fossa incision for RENAL TRANSPLANT and distal ureteric access.**
- Gibson gives **extraperitoneal access to the iliac vessels, lower ureter and bladder** with low morbidity — the reason it is the standard transplant incision.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Not a component" → **complication of surgery**. Incision identification is a separate image-based item.

**REMEMBER:** *A preoperative score can only contain preoperative facts.*

### 7. Gossypiboma

**PEARL:** A **retained surgical sponge/swab** causing a foreign body reaction.

**MUST KNOW:** Two responses: an **exudative** reaction (early abscess/fistula) or a **fibrinous** reaction (late mass mimicking a tumour). On imaging: a **whorled mass with a radio-opaque marker**, "spongiform" gas pattern. Prevention = swab and instrument counts; it is a classic **medicolegal (negligence res ipsa loquitur)** case.

**REMEMBER:** *Gossypiboma = the sponge you left behind — count them.*

## Fluids, Nutrition, Haemorrhage and Transfusion

### 8. Classification of Surgical Haemorrhage

**PEARL:** **Primary** haemorrhage = during the operation. **REACTIONARY** = within **24 hours** (typically 4–6 hours) as the blood pressure rises and clots/ligatures are dislodged — this is what causes **post-thyroidectomy airway obstruction**. **Secondary** = **7–14 days**, from **infection or erosion** (drain tube, malignancy) of a vessel wall.

**UNDERSTAND:** Each type has a distinct mechanism, and the timing is a direct consequence of it. Intraoperative hypotension and vasospasm hide small bleeders; when the patient is warmed, resuscitated and normotensive again — within hours — those vessels reopen. Later bleeding cannot be mechanical, because clots are organised by then; it takes **days of infection to digest a vessel wall**, hence the 7–14 day window.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Reactionary haemorrhage occurs WITHIN 24 hours** — the stem's favourite false statement is "24–48 hours".
- **Post-thyroidectomy haematoma** is the classic reactionary bleed: tense neck swelling and **stridor within hours**. The airway obstruction is from **venous and lymphatic congestion of the larynx**, not direct compression — so **open the wound at the bedside immediately** (remove clips/sutures, evacuate the clot) before attempting intubation. Every thyroidectomy bed keeps a **clip/suture-remover set**.
- Secondary haemorrhage: manage the **cause** — antibiotics, control sepsis, proximal control of the vessel; local suturing in infected tissue fails.
- Other early post-thyroidectomy problems by timing: **immediate hoarseness → recurrent laryngeal nerve**; **hours → haematoma**; **24–48 hours → thyroid storm**; **2–3 days → hypocalcaemia**.
- **Classes of haemorrhagic shock (ATLS):** I <15% (normal vitals), II 15–30% (tachycardia, **narrowed pulse pressure**, anxiety), III 30–40% (**hypotension**, confusion), IV >40% (lethargy, minimal urine output). The **earliest sign is a narrowed pulse pressure and tachycardia** — hypotension is late, especially in the young.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Identify the INCORRECT statement" — the false option is the timing of reactionary haemorrhage. Learn the three windows: **during, <24 h, 7–14 days.**

**REMEMBER:** *Primary now, reactionary when the pressure comes back, secondary when the infection eats through.*

### 9. Massive Transfusion: The Metabolic Consequences

**PEARL:** Massive transfusion causes **HYPOcalcaemia (not hyper), hypothermia, hypokalaemia or hyperkalaemia, metabolic alkalosis (late, from citrate metabolism) and coagulopathy.** **Hypercalcaemia is NOT a complication.**

**UNDERSTAND:** Stored blood is anticoagulated with **CITRATE, which chelates ionised calcium** — so calcium falls the moment large volumes are infused. The same citrate is then **metabolised by the liver to bicarbonate**, which is why the picture evolves from early acidosis (from shock and the stored blood's own acid load) to a **late metabolic alkalosis**. Potassium leaks from stored red cells (hyperkalaemia on rapid transfusion of old blood), but the alkalosis and catecholamines subsequently drive potassium **into** cells, giving late hypokalaemia. Every complication traces back to the storage lesion or the citrate.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Definition:** ≥10 units of red cells in 24 hours, or ≥4 units in 1 hour with ongoing bleeding, or replacement of one blood volume.
- **Ratio: 1:1:1 red cells : fresh frozen plasma : platelets** — balanced resuscitation reduces mortality (PROPPR trial). Add **tranexamic acid within 3 hours** of trauma (CRASH-2), and **fibrinogen/cryoprecipitate** to keep fibrinogen >1.5 g/L.
- **The lethal triad: HYPOTHERMIA, ACIDOSIS, COAGULOPATHY** — each worsens the others. **Use warmed fluids and blood, and warm the patient.**
- **Give calcium** (calcium chloride/gluconate) empirically during massive transfusion — hypocalcaemia causes hypotension, arrhythmia and worsens coagulopathy ("**diamond of death**" when added to the triad).
- **Damage control resuscitation:** permissive hypotension (SBP ~90, unless head injury), minimal crystalloid, early blood products, rapid haemorrhage control.
- Other transfusion complications: **TRALI** (donor antibodies, non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema within 6 hours), **TACO** (volume overload), **acute haemolytic reaction (ABO error — the commonest fatal cause is clerical)**, febrile non-haemolytic reaction, citrate toxicity, and **2,3-DPG depletion shifting the oxygen dissociation curve LEFT** (impaired oxygen unloading).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Which is NOT an adverse effect" — the answer inverts a real derangement. Calcium goes **down**, temperature goes **down**.

**REMEMBER:** *Citrate steals the calcium and later leaves behind bicarbonate.*

### 10. Nutrition — Parenteral vs Enteral Complications

**PEARL:** **ASPIRATION PNEUMONIA is a complication of ENTERAL feeding, not of parenteral nutrition.** Central-line TPN causes **pneumothorax, line sepsis, refeeding syndrome, metabolic derangement, hepatic steatosis and osteoporosis**.

**UNDERSTAND:** The complication follows the **route**. TPN bypasses the gut entirely, so it cannot cause aspiration — but it requires a **central line (mechanical and infective complications)** and delivers nutrients unphysiologically. Enteral feeding uses the gut, so its problems are **tube-related and gastrointestinal**, chief among them **regurgitation and aspiration**.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **"If the gut works, use it"** — enteral nutrition preserves mucosal integrity, reduces bacterial translocation and is cheaper and safer.
- Enteral complications: **tube-related (commonest — malposition, blockage, displacement, nasal erosion), gastrointestinal (diarrhoea, bloating, ASPIRATION), metabolic and infective**. **Prevent aspiration by semi-recumbent (30–45°) positioning.**
- **REFEEDING SYNDROME** affects **both routes**: on reintroducing carbohydrate to a severely malnourished patient, insulin drives **phosphate, potassium and magnesium intracellularly** → **HYPOPHOSPHATAEMIA** (the hallmark), arrhythmias, heart failure, seizures and rhabdomyolysis. Prevent by **starting at 10 kcal/kg/day, giving thiamine before feeding, and monitoring electrolytes daily**.
- TPN-specific: **central line sepsis (commonest serious complication), pneumothorax, thrombosis, hyperglycaemia, cholestasis and hepatic steatosis, metabolic bone disease, and trace element deficiencies**.
- **Ringer's lactate (Hartmann's)**: **Na 131, Cl 111, K 5, Ca 2, lactate 29 mEq/L; no glucose**. **Do not run it in the same line as blood** — its calcium chelates the citrate anticoagulant and causes clotting. Avoid in **liver failure** (lactate not metabolised) and in **metabolic alkalosis**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Not a complication of this method" — identify from the image whether the route is central (TPN) or enteral, then pick the complication belonging to the other route.

**REMEMBER:** *Only food that passes the throat can be aspirated.*

### 11. Intraosseous Access

**PEARL:** When intravenous access fails in an emergency, place an **INTRAOSSEOUS needle — first choice the ANTERIOR PROXIMAL TIBIA, 1–2 cm below and medial to the tibial tuberosity** — if IV access is not obtained within **1 minute**.

**UNDERSTAND:** The medullary cavity of a long bone is a **non-collapsible venous plexus** draining via emissary and nutrient veins directly into the central circulation. Unlike peripheral veins, **it does not collapse in shock or cardiac arrest** — which is precisely why it remains cannulable when every peripheral vein has shut down.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Any drug or fluid that can be given intravenously can be given intraosseously**, at the same dose, including blood, inotropes and anaesthetic agents. Flow rates improve with a pressure bag.
- Other sites: **distal tibia (above the medial malleolus), distal femur, proximal humerus, sternum, iliac crest**.
- Contraindications: **fracture of the target bone, previous IO attempt in the same bone, infection or burn over the site, osteogenesis imperfecta or osteopetrosis, prosthesis**.
- Complications: **extravasation and compartment syndrome, osteomyelitis (rare), fracture, growth plate injury** — avoid the physis in children.
- The needle should be **removed within 24 hours** once other access is secured.
- **Perforated peptic ulcer**: erect chest X-ray shows **free gas under the diaphragm**; management is **IV fluids and resuscitation PLUS exploratory laparotomy** (with a nasogastric tube, antibiotics and analgesia). CT and endoscopy are not required and endoscopy is **contraindicated** (insufflation worsens the leak).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Image recognition of a needle in the proximal tibia. The purpose — **vascular access for fluids/drugs**, not marrow sampling — is the discriminator.

**REMEMBER:** *Marrow is a vein that cannot collapse.*

## Trauma

### 12. Penetrating Abdominal Trauma — Who Goes Straight to Laparotomy

**PEARL:** **Evisceration mandates emergency laparotomy** even in a haemodynamically stable patient. A stable stab wound **without** these features is investigated (local wound exploration / CT / serial examination), not opened.

**UNDERSTAND:** The decision is never "stab wound = laparotomy". It is: *is there already proof of significant intraperitoneal injury?* Haemodynamic instability, peritonitis, evisceration and gunshot wounds are each independent proof — further imaging only delays a certainty. Everything else is uncertain, and up to half of anterior abdominal stab wounds do not even breach the peritoneum, so operating on all of them is a large non-therapeutic laparotomy rate.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Mandatory laparotomy:** haemodynamic **instability**, **peritonitis**, **evisceration**, **gunshot wound** (high kinetic energy, ~90% visceral injury), impalement, blood on NG/PR.
- **Stable anterior stab wound:** local wound exploration → if anterior fascia not breached, discharge; if breached → **CT with triple contrast** and serial abdominal examination.
- **FAST** in penetrating trauma is useful when **positive** (guides to theatre) but a negative FAST **does not exclude** hollow-viscus injury — its real strength is blunt trauma and pericardial blood.
- **Diagnostic peritoneal lavage** is largely historic but still appears as an answer for the unstable blunt-trauma patient where FAST is unavailable/equivocal.
- Gunshot wound to the abdomen → laparotomy regardless of stability.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Two near-identical stems differing in one detail — one shows **omentum protruding** (→ laparotomy), the other a clean stable stab (→ further investigation). Read the image description, not just the vitals.

**REMEMBER:** *Unstable, peritonitic, eviscerated, or shot — open. Otherwise, look first.*

### 13. Liver Trauma: Penetrating vs Blunt

**PEARL:** **Penetrating** liver injury with **haemodynamic instability** → **OPERATIVE management (laparotomy)**. Non-operative management with **angioembolisation is for the HAEMODYNAMICALLY STABLE BLUNT** injury — instability is the universal veto.

**UNDERSTAND:** Non-operative management works only when two conditions hold: the bleeding is **self-limiting** (the liver's low-pressure parenchymal bleeding usually tamponades within Glisson's capsule) and there is **no hollow-viscus injury** to leak. A penetrating wound violates both assumptions — the tract crosses whatever lies in its path, so bowel, diaphragm and major vessels may all be injured — and hypotension proves the bleeding is not self-limiting.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Non-operative management criteria (blunt):** haemodynamically **stable/responder**, no peritonitis, CT-defined injury, available ICU monitoring, blood on standby, and **angioembolisation available for a contrast blush**. Success rate >85% in blunt liver trauma — the liver is the organ most often managed non-operatively.
- **Operative indications:** instability, peritonitis, **all gunshot wounds**, evisceration, ongoing transfusion requirement, associated hollow-viscus injury.
- **Damage-control surgery:** **PERIHEPATIC PACKING** is the key manoeuvre for uncontrollable bleeding — pack, close temporarily, correct the **lethal triad (hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy)** in ICU, re-explore in 24–48 hours.
- **Pringle manoeuvre** — clamping the **portal triad in the free edge of the lesser omentum** (portal vein + hepatic artery). If bleeding **continues** despite it, the source is the **hepatic veins or retrohepatic IVC** — the most lethal liver injuries.
- **AAST grading I–VI**; **grade VI = hepatic avulsion**, essentially fatal.
- Late complications: biloma, **haemobilia (Quincke triad — jaundice, biliary colic, upper GI bleed)** treated by angioembolisation, abscess, and post-traumatic pseudoaneurysm.
- **The spleen is the most commonly injured organ in blunt abdominal trauma; the liver is the most commonly injured in penetrating trauma** and the one most often responsible for death.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Read the vital signs before the imaging grade. **BP 80/50 → theatre**, whatever the CT shows.

**REMEMBER:** *Unstable or penetrating means open; stable and blunt means embolise.*

### 14. Vascular Trauma: Choosing the Conduit

**PEARL:** A **3.5 cm longitudinal tear of the brachial artery** cannot be repaired primarily — it needs an **INTERPOSITION GRAFT using the REVERSED GREAT SAPHENOUS VEIN harvested from the UNINJURED leg**. Prosthetic grafts are a last resort.

**UNDERSTAND:** Primary end-to-end anastomosis is only possible when the defect is **short enough (<2 cm) to close without tension** — tension causes anastomotic disruption or stenosis. The great saphenous vein is the preferred conduit because it **matches the artery in calibre, has comparable elastic properties allowing pulsatile flow, is non-thrombogenic, and resists infection in a contaminated traumatic field** — where a synthetic graft would almost certainly become infected. It is harvested from the **uninjured limb** so that venous drainage of the damaged limb is preserved.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **The vein graft is REVERSED** so that its valves do not obstruct arterial flow.
- Alternatives — **cephalic vein and lesser saphenous vein** (harder to harvest, and the cephalic vein is needed for future venous access); **PTFE/Dacron** only when no vein is available and the field is clean.
- **Hard signs of vascular injury (→ immediate exploration): pulsatile bleeding, an expanding or pulsatile haematoma, a palpable thrill or audible bruit, absent distal pulses, and signs of distal ischaemia (the 6 Ps).** **Soft signs** (history of bleeding, a small stable haematoma, a proximate nerve deficit) → **measure the injured extremity index (<0.9 is abnormal) or CT angiography**.
- **Sequence in a mangled limb: control haemorrhage → restore perfusion (shunt if necessary) → skeletal fixation → definitive vascular repair → soft-tissue cover.** A **temporary intravascular shunt** buys time when bone must be fixed first.
- **Fasciotomy** for prolonged ischaemia (>4–6 hours) or after revascularisation — **reperfusion causes compartment syndrome**, and delay costs the limb. Watch for **rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalaemia and acute kidney injury**.
- **Systemic heparin** if there is no other bleeding source; give **completion angiography**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The length of the defect is the discriminator — a long tear rules out primary anastomosis and a patch. **Interposition reversed saphenous vein graft** is the answer.

**REMEMBER:** *Short gap, sew it; long gap, bridge it with the patient's own vein.*

### 15. Traumatic Diaphragmatic Hernia: Do Not Insert a Chest Drain

**PEARL:** A post-traumatic chest X-ray showing an **elevated hemidiaphragm with bowel or stomach gas in the thorax (± the NG tube coiled in the chest)** is a **traumatic DIAPHRAGMATIC HERNIA** — **CHEST TUBE INSERTION IS CONTRAINDICATED**, because the drain will perforate herniated viscus.

**UNDERSTAND:** A chest drain is placed blindly through the pleural space on the assumption that only lung lies beneath. When bowel or stomach has migrated into the hemithorax, that assumption is false — the trocar or finger enters **hollow viscus**, converting a repairable hernia into faecal or gastric contamination of the pleural cavity. This is why **finger sweep before insertion is mandatory** in every chest drain, and why the film must be read before the procedure.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Left side is affected in ~75%** (the liver shields the right); a right-sided rupture implies far greater force and worse associated injuries.
- **Diagnosis: chest X-ray (an NG tube seen curling above the diaphragm is pathognomonic), CT with sagittal/coronal reformats; often MISSED initially and presenting late with obstruction or strangulation.**
- **Repair is via LAPAROTOMY in the acute phase** (to deal with associated intra-abdominal injuries); **thoracotomy/thoracoscopy is preferred for chronic presentations**, where adhesions to the lung must be divided.
- **Chest drain: insert in the "TRIANGLE OF SAFETY" — 5th intercostal space, anterior to the mid-axillary line, bounded by the lateral border of pectoralis major, the lateral border of latissimus dorsi and a line at the level of the nipple** — passing **over the upper border of the rib** to avoid the neurovascular bundle.
- **Indications: pneumothorax (tension pneumothorax is treated FIRST by immediate needle decompression, then a drain), haemothorax, empyema, chylothorax, large effusion.** **Contraindications/cautions: diaphragmatic hernia, dense pleural adhesions, coagulopathy.**
- **Immediate drainage of >1500 mL of blood, or >200 mL/hour for 2–4 hours, indicates THORACOTOMY.**

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Which should be AVOIDED?" — read the X-ray description first. Bowel in the chest rules out a chest drain; NG tube, log roll and analgesia are all safe.

**REMEMBER:** *Never put a drain into a chest full of bowel.*

## Burns and Plastic Surgery

### 16. Flaps and Suture Materials

**PEARL:** The **free radial artery forearm flap is a FASCIOCUTANEOUS flap** (Chinese flap). For **vascular anastomosis, use POLYPROPYLENE (Prolene)** — non-absorbable, monofilament, minimally thrombogenic.

**UNDERSTAND:** Flaps are classified by **what tissue travels with the blood supply**; suture choice by **how long the tissue needs support**. An artery must hold for a lifetime and must not harbour bacteria or provoke thrombus, so the suture must be **permanent, smooth and monofilament** — absorbable (polyglactin, PDS) or braided (silk, linen) materials fail on one or both counts.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Flap types:** **fasciocutaneous** — radial forearm (thin, pliable, ideal for **intraoral and tongue reconstruction**, based on the radial artery; needs a **preoperative Allen test**), anterolateral thigh; **myocutaneous** — pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi, TRAM; **osseocutaneous/osteomyocutaneous** — **fibula flap for mandible reconstruction**, DCIA; **random pattern** — no named vessel, length:breadth ≤1:1 (up to 3:1 on the face).
- **Suture properties:** **absorbable** — catgut (obsolete), **polyglactin/Vicryl** (braided, 60–90 days), **polydioxanone/PDS** (monofilament, ~6 months), poliglecaprone/Monocryl; **non-absorbable** — **polypropylene (vascular, hernia mesh, skin)**, nylon, silk (braided, high tissue reaction, still used for drains/ties), steel (sternum), linen.
- **Braided sutures harbour bacteria** in their interstices → avoid in contaminated fields and in vessels; **monofilament** has memory and needs more throws.
- Vascular repair principles: proximal and distal control, heparinised saline, **spatulated end-to-end anastomosis or interposition reversed vein graft**, systemic heparin if isolated injury, and **completion angiography**; watch for **compartment syndrome** after revascularisation → prophylactic fasciotomy for prolonged ischaemia.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Two independent one-liners — flap classification and suture choice. **Prolene for vessels** is asked repeatedly.

**REMEMBER:** *Radial forearm carries skin on fascia; arteries are sewn with Prolene.*

### 17. Skin Grafts: Primary vs Secondary Contracture

**PEARL:** **PRIMARY contracture (immediate, elastic recoil at harvest) is GREATER in FULL-THICKNESS grafts.** **SECONDARY contracture (late, myofibroblast-driven shrinkage after grafting) is GREATER in SPLIT-THICKNESS grafts.**

**UNDERSTAND:** The two contractures have entirely different mechanisms, which is why they run in opposite directions. **Primary contracture is passive** — it is the **ELASTIN in the dermis recoiling the moment the graft is cut free**, so the more dermis harvested, the more it shrinks: full-thickness grafts contract most immediately. **Secondary contracture is active** — it is the **wound bed's myofibroblasts pulling the graft inward over weeks**, and the **dermis is what suppresses that process**. A split graft carries little dermis, so it cannot restrain the bed and contracts markedly over time.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Consequence: FULL-THICKNESS grafts are used where contracture would be disastrous — the FACE, EYELIDS, HANDS and across FLEXION CREASES** (better colour match, less late contracture, but need a well-vascularised bed and leave a donor site that must be closed primarily). **SPLIT grafts cover large areas, can be MESHED to expand, and the donor site re-epithelialises spontaneously from adnexal remnants — but they contract badly and match colour poorly.**
- **Graft take occurs in three phases: PLASMATIC IMBIBITION (0–48 h) → INOSCULATION (2–3 days) → REVASCULARISATION/capillary ingrowth (4–7 days).**
- **Commonest cause of graft failure is HAEMATOMA (then seroma, infection — especially *Streptococcus pyogenes* — shear/movement, and a poorly vascularised bed).** Grafts will **NOT take on bare bone, bare cartilage, bare tendon or irradiated tissue** — those need a **FLAP**, which brings its own blood supply.
- **Donor sites for split grafts: thigh, buttock; for full-thickness: postauricular, supraclavicular, upper eyelid, groin.**
- **Meshing** increases coverage and allows exudate to escape, at the cost of a permanent lattice scar.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Two-statement selection with the contractures deliberately swapped. Anchor: **more dermis → more primary, less secondary.**

**REMEMBER:** *Thick grafts shrink at once and then behave; thin grafts behave at once and then shrink.*

### 18. Burn Dressings and Nerve Entrapment After Hernia Repair

**PEARL:** A **pink, oozing, exquisitely PAINFUL burn (sensitive to moving air and touch) is a SUPERFICIAL PARTIAL-THICKNESS burn** — dress it with a **BIOLOGICAL dressing (COLLAGEN sheet or amniotic membrane)**, which is the preferred dressing for partial-thickness burns. **Excision and grafting is for FULL-thickness burns**, and **silver sulphadiazine is reserved for deep dermal, full-thickness, infected or neglected burns.**

**UNDERSTAND:** Pain is the depth test. **Superficial burns hurt intensely because the sensory nerve endings survive**; a **full-thickness burn is PAINLESS and insensate** because the nerves are destroyed, and it appears dry, leathery and white or charred with no capillary refill. Since a superficial partial-thickness burn **has intact dermal appendages and will re-epithelialise in 2–3 weeks**, the goal is simply to provide a **moist, sterile, non- adherent cover** — which is what collagen does; grafting it would be unnecessary surgery. Silver sulphadiazine, by contrast, forms a pseudo-eschar, delays epithelialisation and selects resistance, so it is not first choice for a clean superficial burn.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Burn depth: EPIDERMAL (erythema only, no blister — sunburn); SUPERFICIAL PARTIAL (blisters, moist, pink, blanches, VERY painful — heals in 2 weeks without scarring); DEEP PARTIAL (mottled red/white, sluggish or absent blanching, reduced sensation — heals slowly with scarring, often grafted); FULL THICKNESS (white/waxy or charred, dry, leathery, INSENSATE, no blanching — needs excision and grafting).**
- **Fluid resuscitation (PARKLAND formula): 4 mL × body weight (kg) × %TBSA of Ringer's lactate in the first 24 hours, HALF in the first 8 hours from the TIME OF INJURY**, titrated to a **urine output of 0.5 mL/kg/hr in adults, 1 mL/kg/hr in children**. **Rule of nines** for area; the **patient's palm ≈ 1%**. **Superficial epidermal burns are NOT counted** in TBSA.
- **Escharotomy** for circumferential full-thickness burns of a limb (vascular compromise) or the chest (ventilatory restriction). Suspect **inhalational injury** with facial burns, singed nasal hair, soot in sputum, hoarseness or a closed-space fire — **intubate EARLY**, before oedema closes the airway.
- **MERALGIA PARAESTHETICA** — burning pain and numbness over the **anterolateral thigh** from entrapment of the **LATERAL CUTANEOUS NERVE OF THE THIGH (L2–L3)**, a recognised complication of **laparoscopic (TEP/TAPP) hernia repair** when tacks or mesh are placed in the **"triangle of pain"** lateral to the cord and below the iliopubic tract. It is **purely sensory — no motor weakness** (contrast femoral nerve injury, which weakens knee extension and abolishes the knee jerk).
- Other post-hernia-repair neuralgias: **ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric** (groin, scrotum/labia and medial thigh — after **open** repair) and the **genital branch of the genitofemoral** nerve.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** For burns, the **pain and colour** give the depth, and the depth gives the dressing. For groin pain after laparoscopic repair, the **anterolateral thigh** distribution gives the nerve.

**REMEMBER:** *If it hurts, it will heal — cover it. If it is numb and leathery, cut it out and graft it.*

### 19. Marjolin's Ulcer

**PEARL:** **Marjolin's ulcer = a well-differentiated (low-grade) squamous cell carcinoma arising in a chronic scar or ulcer.** It is **painless, slow-growing, RADIORESISTANT and does not metastasise via lymphatics** — because scar tissue is **AVASCULAR and has no nerves or lymphatics**.

**UNDERSTAND:** Every feature follows from the **avascular, denervated, alymphatic nature of scar tissue**. No nerves means **no pain**; no lymphatics means **no nodal spread** (until the tumour breaches the scar into normal tissue); poor blood supply means **slow growth** and **radioresistance** (radiotherapy requires oxygen to generate free radicals). So the answer to "highly vascular" is emphatically no.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Arises in **post-burn scars (classically after 10–30 years), chronic venous ulcers, chronic osteomyelitis sinuses, post-radiation ulcers and pressure sores**.
- Clinical signs of malignant change in a chronic ulcer: **everted edges, exuberant cauliflower-like granulation tissue, foul discharge, bleeding on touch, increase in size, and change from painless to painful**.
- Treatment: **wide local excision with a 1 cm margin ± skin grafting**; **amputation** for extensive limb disease. Node dissection only if nodes are clinically involved (once the tumour has escaped the scar).
- Ulcer edges to distinguish: **sloping — healing/venous**; **punched-out — trophic, syphilitic, ischaemic**; **undermined — tuberculous**; **rolled/beaded — basal cell carcinoma**; **everted — squamous cell carcinoma**.
- **Pilonidal sinus** — natal cleft sinus in a hirsute young man, classically a **jeep/lorry driver**; the hair in the sinus is **DEAD hair drawn in by shearing forces — hair FOLLICLES are essentially never found in the sinus wall**. Recurrence is common; treatment ranges from excision with primary or Karydakis/Bascom flap closure to laying open.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "All are true EXCEPT" → **highly vascular**. All the other stated features are consequences of avascularity.

**REMEMBER:** *Scar tissue has no vessels, nerves or lymphatics — so neither does the tumour in it.*

## Oesophagus and Stomach

### 20. Oesophageal Perforation and Rectal Prolapse

**PEARL:** ***BOERHAAVE SYNDROME (spontaneous, full-thickness rupture after forceful vomiting) REQUIRES OPERATIVE MANAGEMENT***, whereas ***CERVICAL and small IATROGENIC (endoscopic) perforations without sepsis or mediastinal contamination may be managed CONSERVATIVELY*** with nil by mouth, antibiotics, nutritional support and drainage. ***IN FULL-THICKNESS RECTAL PROLAPSE, FEMALES ARE AFFECTED ABOUT 6 TIMES MORE OFTEN THAN MALES (not the reverse)***, risk factors include chronic straining, multiparity, ANOREXIA NERVOSA and childhood MALNUTRITION, and ***ABDOMINAL RECTOPEXY HAS LOWER RECURRENCE THAN PERINEAL PROCEDURES (Altemeier having lower recurrence than Delorme).***

**UNDERSTAND:** Oesophageal perforation is deadly because the ***OESOPHAGUS HAS NO SEROSA***, so contamination spreads unchecked into the mediastinum, producing fulminant mediastinitis with mortality rising sharply after 24 hours. That single anatomical deficiency explains why ***the time from perforation to treatment is the strongest predictor of survival***, and why an extensive thoracic rupture with soiling cannot be managed by antibiotics alone.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Boerhaave syndrome: ***MACKLER'S TRIAD (vomiting, lower chest pain, subcutaneous emphysema), usually a LEFT POSTEROLATERAL distal tear; chest X-ray shows pneumomediastinum, pleural effusion and the "V SIGN OF NACLERIO"; confirm with a WATER-SOLUBLE CONTRAST SWALLOW or CT.*** ***PLEURAL FLUID WITH A LOW pH AND HIGH AMYLASE, OR CONTAINING FOOD PARTICLES, IS DIAGNOSTIC.*** Treatment: resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics and antifungals, and primary repair with drainage (or oesophageal stenting/diversion in late or unfit cases).
- ***THE COMMONEST CAUSE OF OESOPHAGEAL PERFORATION OVERALL IS IATROGENIC (endoscopy/dilatation)***, not Boerhaave.
- **Rectal prolapse surgery: ***ABDOMINAL — ventral mesh or suture RECTOPEXY (laparoscopic; lowest recurrence, preferred in the fit); PERINEAL — ALTEMEIER (perineal rectosigmoidectomy) or DELORME (mucosal sleeve resection), preferred in frail or high-risk patients.*** ***ALWAYS EXCLUDE A LEAD POINT (polyp, carcinoma) AND, IN CHILDREN, CYSTIC FIBROSIS AND MALNUTRITION*** — paediatric prolapse is managed conservatively and usually resolves.
- ***FAECAL INCONTINENCE ACCOMPANIES PROLAPSE IN MOST PATIENTS AND MAY NOT FULLY RECOVER AFTER REPAIR*** — an important part of consent.
- **Distinguish full-thickness prolapse (***concentric rings of mucosa***) from prolapsing haemorrhoids (***radial furrows***).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Oesophageal perforation needing operative management → **Boerhaave syndrome**; incorrect about full-thickness rectal prolapse → **that men are affected 6 times more than women**.

**REMEMBER:** *No serosa means no barrier — a ruptured oesophagus contaminates the mediastinum within hours.*

### 21. GERD, Barrett Oesophagus and Hiatus Hernia

**PEARL:** ***WHEN GERD SYMPTOMS FAIL TO RESPOND TO A PROTON PUMP INHIBITOR, THE NEXT STEP IS UPPER GI ENDOSCOPY*** (to look for oesophagitis, Barrett's, stricture or malignancy) — pH studies and manometry come later, and mainly before surgery. ***REGURGITATION RESPONDS POORLY TO PPIs (they suppress acid, not reflux)*** — a key indication for surgery. ***BARRETT'S OESOPHAGUS = salmon-pink columnar mucosa extending above the gastro-oesophageal junction, with INTESTINAL METAPLASIA on biopsy.*** ***THE "MIXED" (type III) HIATUS HERNIA INVOLVES UPWARD MIGRATION OF THE GASTRO-OESOPHAGEAL JUNCTION together with a paraoesophageal component.***

**UNDERSTAND:** The distinction between acid and volume explains the whole treatment algorithm. ***PPIs make the refluxate less acidic but do not stop it refluxing***, so heartburn improves while ***regurgitation, cough, aspiration and laryngitis often persist*** — and those "volume" symptoms are the ones that respond to a mechanical solution, ***FUNDOPLICATION.*** Conversely, ***a SLIDING (type I) hernia predisposes to GERD by disrupting the sphincter mechanism, while a ROLLING/PARAOESOPHAGEAL hernia keeps the sphincter in place and presents instead with OBSTRUCTIVE symptoms and the risk of GASTRIC VOLVULUS.***

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Extra-oesophageal GERD: ***chronic cough, laryngitis, hoarseness, ASTHMA and DENTAL EROSIONS.*** ***ALARM FEATURES DEMANDING PROMPT ENDOSCOPY: DYSPHAGIA, weight loss, anaemia, GI bleeding, persistent vomiting, a mass, and age >45–50 with new symptoms.***
- **Barrett's: ***a risk factor for ADENOCARCINOMA (about 0.5% per year); managed with high-dose PPI and SURVEILLANCE endoscopy; LOW- or HIGH-GRADE DYSPLASIA is treated with ENDOSCOPIC RADIOFREQUENCY ABLATION or endoscopic mucosal resection.***
- ***ROLLING/PARAOESOPHAGEAL HERNIAS ARE REPAIRED EVEN WHEN MILDLY SYMPTOMATIC*** because of the risk of incarceration and gastric volvulus; ***CT WITH ORAL CONTRAST is the best imaging (endoscopy under-detects hernias).***
- **Achalasia treatment: ***the definitive treatment is HELLER'S CARDIOMYOTOMY (usually laparoscopic, with a partial DOR or Toupet fundoplication to prevent reflux) or PER-ORAL ENDOSCOPIC MYOTOMY; pneumatic dilatation gives temporary relief and botulinum toxin is for the unfit.*** ***RAMSTEDT'S PYLOROMYOTOMY IS FOR INFANTILE PYLORIC STENOSIS — a classic distractor.***
- ***PLUMMER–VINSON SYNDROME (post-cricoid WEB + IRON-DEFICIENCY ANAEMIA + glossitis) is PREMALIGNANT for post-cricoid squamous carcinoma; the web is treated by DILATATION (bougies), NOT myotomy, and correcting the iron deficiency alone often relieves the dysphagia.***

**EXAM CONNECTION:** GERD not responding to PPI → **endoscopy**; incorrect about GERD → **that regurgitation is well controlled by PPIs**; salmon-coloured mucosa with reflux → **Barrett oesophagus**; correct about hiatus hernia → **mixed type involves upward migration of the GE junction**; definitive treatment of achalasia → **Heller's myotomy**; incorrect about Plummer–Vinson → **that myotomy is used to dilate the webs**.

**REMEMBER:** *PPIs change the acid, surgery changes the anatomy — regurgitation needs the anatomy fixed.*

### 22. Peptic Ulcer — Bleeding and Perforation

**PEARL:** ***AN ACTIVELY BLEEDING PEPTIC ULCER IS TREATED ENDOSCOPICALLY FIRST — DUAL THERAPY WITH ADRENALINE INJECTION PLUS A SECOND MODALITY (CLIPS OR THERMAL COAGULATION)*** — followed by high-dose PPI; surgery is for failed endotherapy. ***IN SUSPECTED PERFORATION, THE FIRST INVESTIGATION IS AN ERECT CHEST/ABDOMINAL X-RAY looking for free gas (positive in ~70%), with CECT if it is negative but suspicion persists.*** ***GRAHAM'S OMENTAL PATCH REPAIR IS FOR PERFORATION, NOT FOR BLEEDING.***

**UNDERSTAND:** The anatomy predicts the complication: ***a POSTERIOR duodenal ulcer erodes the GASTRODUODENAL ARTERY and BLEEDS; an ANTERIOR duodenal ulcer, with only peritoneum beyond it, PERFORATES.*** This is why the site described in a stem tells you which emergency you are dealing with — and why a posterior ulcer that bleeds massively may need the artery to be under-run surgically (with the risk of injuring the common bile duct).

**MUST KNOW:**

- ***THE FORREST CLASSIFICATION grades endoscopic stigmata and predicts rebleeding: Ia spurting, Ib oozing, IIa visible vessel, IIb adherent clot, IIc flat pigmented spot, III clean base.*** ***CLASSES Ia–IIb REQUIRE ENDOSCOPIC THERAPY; class III needs none.*** The ***GLASGOW–BLATCHFORD score identifies who can be managed as an outpatient, ROCKALL predicts mortality.***
- ***ALWAYS TEST FOR AND ERADICATE *H. PYLORI*, STOP NSAIDs, AND REPEAT ENDOSCOPY FOR GASTRIC ULCERS TO EXCLUDE MALIGNANCY.***
- **Perforation management: ***resuscitation, nasogastric decompression, IV PPI and antibiotics, then LAPAROSCOPIC OR OPEN OMENTAL (GRAHAM) PATCH REPAIR with peritoneal lavage; non-operative management is reserved for the sealed perforation in a stable patient.***
- **Post-gastrectomy syndromes: ***EARLY DUMPING (15–30 min after a meal: colic, diarrhoea, flushing, palpitations — from hyperosmolar chyme and fluid shift) and LATE DUMPING (1–3 hours: REACTIVE HYPOGLYCAEMIA with light-headedness and syncope after carbohydrate).*** ***MANAGEMENT: SMALL, FREQUENT, DRY MEALS (NOT fewer larger meals), avoid high-glycaemic-index carbohydrate, separate fluids from solids, lie down after eating, add acarbose or OCTREOTIDE, and consider revisional surgery (Roux-en-Y) only as a last resort.***
- **Reconstructions: ***BILLROTH I — gastroduodenostomy; BILLROTH II — gastrojejunostomy with a blind duodenal loop (risk of afferent loop syndrome and bile reflux, reduced by a BRAUN anastomosis); ROUX-EN-Y — the best protection against bile reflux.***
- ***A MALLORY–WEISS TEAR (mucosal tear at the gastro-oesophageal junction after retching, with haematemesis) IS USUALLY SELF-LIMITING — contrast with BOERHAAVE'S full-thickness rupture, which presents with chest pain and surgical emphysema rather than haematemesis.***

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Actively bleeding duodenal ulcer → **adrenaline injection with clips**; suspected perforated ulcer → **erect X-ray abdomen**; NOT useful in dumping syndrome → **fewer but larger meals**; haematemesis after binge drinking and retching → **Mallory–Weiss tear**; Billroth II reconstruction identification.

**REMEMBER:** *Anterior ulcers perforate, posterior ulcers bleed — the wall behind decides the emergency.*

### 23. Endoscopy and Oesophageal Cancer Palliation

**PEARL:** ***UPPER GI ENDOSCOPY VISUALISES TO THE SECOND PART OF THE DUODENUM, IS THE GOLD STANDARD FOR MUCOSAL DISEASE AND ALLOWS VARICEAL BANDING — BUT IT IS POOR FOR DIAGNOSING A GIST***, which is a SUBMUCOSAL lesion requiring ***ENDOSCOPIC ULTRASOUND (with biopsy) or CT.*** ***THE COMMONEST COMPLICATION OF OESOPHAGEAL STENTING FOR PALLIATION IS STENT MIGRATION.*** ***NARROW BAND IMAGING uses filtered blue-green light to enhance mucosal vasculature and detect dysplasia*** (chromoendoscopy uses dyes — Lugol's iodine for squamous dysplasia, which leaves abnormal mucosa UNSTAINED).

**UNDERSTAND:** The GIST point illustrates a general endoscopic principle: ***THE SCOPE SEES ONLY THE MUCOSA.*** A submucosal tumour appears as a smooth bulge with normal overlying mucosa, so ordinary forceps biopsies are usually negative ("bite-on-bite" is unreliable) and ***EUS — which images the wall in layers and permits fine-needle aspiration — is the correct tool.*** The same reasoning explains why EUS, not endoscopy, provides the T stage in oesophageal and gastric cancer.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **GIST: arises from the ***INTERSTITIAL CELLS OF CAJAL, is c-KIT (CD117) and DOG1 POSITIVE, is graded by SIZE AND MITOTIC RATE, treated by RESECTION WITHOUT ROUTINE LYMPHADENECTOMY (spread is haematogenous), with IMATINIB for high-risk, unresectable or metastatic disease.*** ***BIOPSY OF A RESECTABLE GIST IS AVOIDED — risk of rupture and seeding.***
- **Oesophageal cancer palliation: ***SELF-EXPANDING METAL STENTS for dysphagia (complications: migration, tumour ingrowth/overgrowth, pain, perforation, reflux with a stent across the GOJ), plus radiotherapy/brachytherapy, laser or argon plasma ablation, and nutritional support.***
- **Endoscopic adjuncts: ***CHROMOENDOSCOPY (methylene blue, acetic acid, indigo carmine, Lugol's iodine), NARROW BAND IMAGING, VIRTUAL endoscopy (CT reconstruction) and INDOCYANINE GREEN fluorescence (used intraoperatively to assess perfusion and map lymphatics).***
- **Endoscopic therapeutic capability: ***haemostasis, variceal banding, polypectomy, EMR/ESD, stenting, dilatation, PEG placement and foreign body retrieval.***
- ***COMPLICATIONS OF DIAGNOSTIC ENDOSCOPY: perforation, bleeding, aspiration and sedation-related cardiorespiratory events — always take informed consent and check anticoagulation.***

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Incorrect about the upper GI endoscope → **that it is useful for diagnosing GIST**; commonest complication of oesophageal stenting → **migration**; identifying narrow band imaging.

**REMEMBER:** *The scope sees mucosa only — a submucosal lump needs ultrasound, not forceps.*

### 24. Gastric Cancer

**PEARL:** ***THE COMMONEST SITE OF GASTRIC ADENOCARCINOMA IS THE ANTRUM WORLDWIDE, BUT THE PROXIMAL STOMACH IN WESTERN COUNTRIES*** (a rising incidence driven by GERD and obesity, while distal cancers fall with *H. pylori* eradication). ***D1 LYMPHADENECTOMY REMOVES PERIGASTRIC NODES (STATIONS 1–6); D2 ADDS THE NODES ALONG THE MAJOR ARTERIAL TRUNKS (7–11), so D2 = STATIONS 1–11.*** ***SIEWERT TYPE II — from 1 cm ABOVE to 2 cm BELOW the gastro-oesophageal junction — IS THE TRUE JUNCTIONAL TUMOUR*** (type I: 1–5 cm above; type III: 2–5 cm below). ***DUKES STAGING WAS NEVER USED FOR STOMACH CANCER — it is a colorectal system.***

**UNDERSTAND:** The east–west difference in nodal surgery makes sense once you see what D2 adds: ***gastric cancer spreads early along the lymphatics accompanying the coeliac axis branches***, so removing only the perigastric nodes understages and under-treats. D2 was pioneered in Japan (where screening detects early cancers) and initially showed higher mortality in Western trials only because it included ***splenectomy and distal pancreatectomy*** — with those omitted, ***SPLEEN- AND PANCREAS-PRESERVING D2 IS NOW THE GLOBAL STANDARD.***

**MUST KNOW:**

- **T staging: ***T1 mucosa/submucosa, T2 MUSCULARIS PROPRIA, T3 subserosa, T4 PERFORATES SEROSA (T4a) or invades adjacent structures (T4b).*** ***RETROPANCREATIC, MESENTERIC AND PARA-AORTIC NODES ARE NON-REGIONAL AND COUNT AS M1.***
- **Gastric polyps: ***METAPLASTIC/HYPERPLASTIC polyps are associated with H. PYLORI and chronic gastritis (and regress after eradication); FUNDIC GLAND polyps with PPI use and FAP; and ADENOMATOUS polyps carry genuine malignant potential.*** ***IT IS THE FUNDIC GLAND POLYP — NOT THE HYPERPLASTIC POLYP — THAT IS LINKED TO FAP.***
- **Gastric lymphoma: ***the commonest extranodal lymphoma site, B-cell derived from MALT, arising most often in the ANTRUM (not the fundus), NON-HODGKIN in type; EARLY LOW-GRADE MALToma REGRESSES WITH H. PYLORI ERADICATION ALONE*** (unless t(11;18)-positive).
- **Risk factors: ***H. pylori, atrophic gastritis and pernicious anaemia, smoked/salted food and nitrosamines, smoking, blood group A, previous gastric surgery, MÉNÉTRIER disease and HEREDITARY DIFFUSE GASTRIC CANCER (CDH1).***
- **Early gastric cancer (confined to mucosa/submucosa regardless of nodes) has ***>90% five-year survival and may be treated by ENDOSCOPIC SUBMUCOSAL DISSECTION*** — the reason Japanese screening programmes matter.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Commonest site in Western countries → **proximal stomach**; D2 lymphadenectomy → **stations 1–11**; true GE junction tumour → **Siewert type II (1 cm above to 2 cm below)**; inaccurate about gastric TNM → **that Dukes staging was used for stomach cancer**; incorrect about gastric polyps → **that metaplastic polyps are strongly associated with FAP**; incorrect about gastric lymphoma → **that the fundus is the commonest site**.

**REMEMBER:** *Perigastric nodes are D1; add the arterial trunks and you have D2.*

### 25. Upper GI Bleeding Scores

**PEARL:** ***THE ROCKALL SCORE USES AGE, SHOCK (systolic BP and pulse), COMORBIDITY, ENDOSCOPIC DIAGNOSIS AND STIGMATA OF RECENT HAEMORRHAGE (Forrest grade) — IT DOES NOT INCLUDE HAEMOGLOBIN.*** ***THE GLASGOW–BLATCHFORD SCORE DOES INCLUDE HAEMOGLOBIN AND UREA but NO endoscopic findings.***

**UNDERSTAND:** The two scores answer different clinical questions at different moments. ***BLATCHFORD IS A PRE-ENDOSCOPY TRIAGE TOOL — computed from bedside observations and blood tests, it identifies the very-low-risk patient (score 0–1) who can be discharged for outpatient endoscopy.*** ***ROCKALL IS A POST-ENDOSCOPY PROGNOSTIC TOOL — it predicts rebleeding and MORTALITY and therefore requires endoscopic information.*** Choosing the wrong one is choosing the wrong question.

**MUST KNOW:**

- ***HAEMOGLOBIN IS A POOR EARLY MARKER OF ACUTE BLOOD LOSS*** — it falls only after haemodilution, which is exactly why the post-endoscopy prognostic score ignores it and why resuscitation is guided by pulse, blood pressure, urine output and lactate rather than by the initial haemoglobin.
- **Management of upper GI bleeding: ***resuscitate first (two wide-bore cannulae, cross-match, restrictive transfusion to a threshold of about 7–8 g/dL), correct coagulopathy, give a PPI, and add TERLIPRESSIN AND PROPHYLACTIC ANTIBIOTICS IF VARICEAL BLEEDING IS SUSPECTED; ENDOSCOPY WITHIN 24 HOURS (within 12 hours if unstable or variceal).***
- **Variceal bleeding: ***BAND LIGATION is first-line (sclerotherapy if not feasible); BALLOON TAMPONADE (Sengstaken–Blakemore) is a temporary bridge; TIPSS is the rescue for uncontrolled or early rebleeding, and secondary prophylaxis is banding plus a non-selective beta-blocker.***
- ***RESTRICTIVE TRANSFUSION IMPROVES SURVIVAL IN UPPER GI BLEEDING, PARTICULARLY IN CIRRHOSIS***, because over-transfusion raises portal pressure and provokes rebleeding.
- ***A NORMAL HAEMOGLOBIN NEVER EXCLUDES A MAJOR BLEED IN THE FIRST HOURS.***

**EXAM CONNECTION:** NOT included in the Rockall score → **haemoglobin**.

**REMEMBER:** *Blatchford decides who can go home; Rockall predicts who might die.*

### 26. Achalasia: Completing the Work-Up

**PEARL:** Dysphagia to **BOTH solids AND liquids from the outset**, with a **bird-beak tapering and dilated oesophagus on barium swallow**, is **achalasia** → confirm with **OESOPHAGEAL MANOMETRY (the gold standard)** and perform **UPPER GI ENDOSCOPY to exclude PSEUDOACHALASIA (a malignancy at the cardia)**.

**UNDERSTAND:** Dysphagia to **liquids as early as to solids** is the signature of a **motility disorder**, because a peristaltic failure affects all consistencies equally; a **mechanical obstruction (stricture or carcinoma) blocks solids first** and progresses to liquids. Manometry is needed because it measures the two defining abnormalities directly — **failure of the lower oesophageal sphincter to relax and absent peristalsis** — and detects them **before** the oesophagus dilates enough to show on barium. Endoscopy is mandatory because a tumour infiltrating the cardia can reproduce every feature.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Manometric criteria: raised integrated relaxation pressure (impaired LOS relaxation), absent peristalsis, ± raised resting LOS pressure. CHICAGO CLASSIFICATION: Type I (classic, no pressurisation), Type II (pan-oesophageal pressurisation — BEST response to treatment), Type III (spastic — worst response, may need a long myotomy).**
- **Pathology: loss of INHIBITORY (NO- and VIP-producing) neurons in the myenteric (Auerbach) plexus** — so the excitatory cholinergic tone is unopposed. **CHAGAS DISEASE (*Trypanosoma cruzi*) produces an identical picture.**
- **Treatment: PNEUMATIC BALLOON DILATATION, laparoscopic HELLER'S CARDIOMYOTOMY WITH A PARTIAL FUNDOPLICATION (Dor/Toupet — to prevent the reflux the myotomy would otherwise cause), or POEM (per-oral endoscopic myotomy — especially good for type III).** **Botulinum toxin and nitrates/calcium channel blockers are reserved for the unfit** (temporary effect).
- **Achalasia is PREMALIGNANT — squamous cell carcinoma** (from chronic stasis and mucosal irritation), with a latency of 15–20 years.
- **Barium features: "bird's beak"/"rat tail" tapering, a dilated tortuous "sigmoid" oesophagus with an air-fluid level, and absent gastric air bubble.**

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The stem gives the barium picture and asks what **else** is needed — the pair is **endoscopy (rule out cancer) + manometry (confirm and subtype)**. pH studies and PET are distractors.

**REMEMBER:** *Liquids and solids together means the muscle, not the lumen — manometry proves it, endoscopy excludes cancer.*

### 27. Rockall vs Glasgow–Blatchford Score

**PEARL:** **Haemoglobin is NOT part of the Rockall score.** Rockall = **age, shock (BP and pulse), comorbidity, endoscopic diagnosis, and stigmata of recent haemorrhage**. **Glasgow–Blatchford includes haemoglobin and blood urea** but uses **no endoscopic findings**.

**UNDERSTAND:** That difference decides their use — **Blatchford is a pre-endoscopy triage tool** (a score of 0 allows outpatient management), while **Rockall predicts rebleeding and mortality after endoscopy**.

**REMEMBER:** *Blatchford before the scope (bloods); Rockall after it (findings).*

### 28. Plummer–Vinson Syndrome

**PEARL:** Oesophageal webs are treated by **dilation with bougies/dilators — NOT myotomy**; correcting the **iron deficiency** alone improves symptoms.

**MUST KNOW:** Triad: **dysphagia + iron deficiency anaemia + oesophageal web** (post-cricoid), classically in middle-aged women; also glossitis, angular stomatitis, koilonychia. ### 29. Acute Appendicitis

**PEARL:** ***LUMINAL OBSTRUCTION (faecolith, lymphoid hyperplasia, stricture, or *ENTEROBIUS VERMICULARIS*) IS THE INITIATING EVENT***, and a diet low in fibre/high in refined carbohydrate is implicated. ***THE ORGANISMS ARE MIXED AEROBIC AND ANAEROBIC (E. COLI and BACTEROIDES FRAGILIS) — NOT purely anaerobic.*** ***MURPHY'S TRIAD = PERIUMBILICAL PAIN MIGRATING TO THE RIGHT ILIAC FOSSA, VOMITING, AND (low-grade) FEVER — GUARDING AND RIGIDITY ARE NOT PART OF IT.*** ***RISK FACTORS FOR PERFORATION: FAECOLITH, EXTREMES OF AGE, IMMUNOSUPPRESSION AND DELAYED PRESENTATION — a RETROCAECAL position is NOT one*** (it causes atypical, milder signs and delayed diagnosis, but is not itself a perforation risk).

**UNDERSTAND:** The classic pain migration is a lesson in innervation: ***early distension of the appendix produces VISCERAL pain referred to the T10 dermatome (periumbilical), and only when the inflammation reaches the PARIETAL PERITONEUM does the pain become SOMATIC, sharp and localised at McBurney's point.*** The whole natural history follows the obstruction: ***distension → venous congestion → mucosal ischaemia → bacterial invasion → gangrene → perforation***, typically within 24–72 hours.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Signs: ***McBURNEY'S point tenderness, ROVSING'S sign, PSOAS sign (retrocaecal appendix), OBTURATOR sign (pelvic appendix) and rebound tenderness.*** ***ALVARADO ("MANTRELS") SCORE aids decision-making; ULTRASOUND is first-line in children and pregnancy, CT in adults (highest accuracy), and MRI in pregnancy where available.***
- **Postoperative complications by timing: ***WOUND INFECTION on days 4–5 (local signs); INTRA-ABDOMINAL/PELVIC ABSCESS around DAY 5–7 with SPIKING FEVER, malaise and DIARRHOEA (pelvic abscess irritates the rectum); PORTAL PYAEMIA (rare — jaundice, rigors, liver abscesses); FAECAL FISTULA; adhesive obstruction later.***
- ***APPENDICEAL MASS (a phlegmon after 3–5 days) IS MANAGED CONSERVATIVELY (OCHSNER–SHERREN regimen) WITH ANTIBIOTICS AND INTERVAL APPENDICECTOMY OR COLONIC EVALUATION; AN ABSCESS IS DRAINED.***
- **Appendiceal neoplasms: ***NEUROENDOCRINE (carcinoid) tumours are the commonest and are usually incidental; SIZE IS THE BEST PREDICTOR OF BEHAVIOUR — <1 cm at the tip: appendicectomy suffices; >2 cm, or base involvement, mesoappendiceal invasion or nodal disease: RIGHT HEMICOLECTOMY; 1–2 cm: individualised, NOT automatically appendicectomy.*** Mucinous neoplasms can cause pseudomyxoma peritonei.
- ***IN PREGNANCY, APPENDICITIS IS THE COMMONEST NON-OBSTETRIC SURGICAL EMERGENCY, and the appendix is displaced upward — a high index of suspicion is needed, and delay is far more dangerous to the fetus than surgery.***

**EXAM CONNECTION:** NOT true about appendicitis aetiology → **that the organisms are anaerobic**; NOT part of Murphy's triad → **guarding and rigidity**; NOT a perforation risk → **retrocaecal position**; spiking fever with diarrhoea on day 6 → **intra-abdominal (pelvic) abscess**; incorrect about appendiceal neoplasms → **that 1–2 cm tumours always get a negative-margin appendicectomy**.

**REMEMBER:** *Obstruction starts it, visceral pain announces it, parietal pain localises it.*

### 30. Meckel's Diverticulum

**PEARL:** ***MECKEL'S DIVERTICULUM IS A TRUE DIVERTICULUM (containing ALL THREE LAYERS of bowel wall) — a persistent remnant of the VITELLO-INTESTINAL DUCT on the ANTIMESENTERIC border of the ILEUM.*** It may contain ***ectopic GASTRIC (commonest) or PANCREATIC mucosa***, and can present as ***diverticulitis, obstruction, intussusception or — classically in children — PAINLESS RECTAL BLEEDING.*** ***A Tc-99m PERTECHNETATE SCAN DETECTS THE ECTOPIC GASTRIC MUCOSA; AN INCIDENTALLY FOUND DIVERTICULUM MAY BE LEFT ALONE IF IT IS BROAD-BASED AND NOT THICKENED.***

**UNDERSTAND:** ***THE ECTOPIC GASTRIC MUCOSA IS THE KEY TO EVERYTHING.*** It secretes acid onto adjacent ileal mucosa that has no defence against it, causing ***peptic ulceration and painless bleeding***; it is also what makes the diverticulum visible on a pertechnetate scan, since the tracer is taken up by gastric mucosa wherever it lies. A diverticulum WITHOUT gastric mucosa neither bleeds nor shows on the scan — which is why a negative scan does not exclude a Meckel's causing obstruction.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **The "rule of 2s": ***2% of the population, 2 FEET (60 cm) from the ileocaecal valve, 2 INCHES long, 2 types of ectopic mucosa, symptomatic usually before age 2, male:female 2:1.***
- ***IN CHILDREN THE COMMONEST PRESENTATION IS PAINLESS PER-RECTAL BLEEDING, NOT AN APPENDICITIS-LIKE PICTURE*** (diverticulitis mimicking appendicitis is the ADULT presentation) — a frequently examined distinction.
- **Complications in order: ***haemorrhage (children), intestinal obstruction (via a persistent fibrous band, volvulus or intussusception — the commonest complication in adults), diverticulitis and perforation, and rarely neoplasia (carcinoid).***
- **Resection is indicated for ***any symptomatic diverticulum***; incidental resection is considered in the young, with a NARROW base, palpable ectopic tissue, or a long diverticulum.
- **Other vitello-intestinal duct anomalies: ***umbilical sinus, fistula (faecal discharge from the umbilicus), a fibrous band and an enterocystoma.***

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Incorrect about Meckel's diverticulum → **that it is a false diverticulum**; incorrect statement → **that in children it presents mimicking appendicitis** (it presents with painless bleeding).

**REMEMBER:** *Gastric mucosa in the wrong place — it bleeds, and it glows on the scan.*

### 31. Intestinal Obstruction

**PEARL:** ***COLICKY PAIN IS THE EARLIEST SYMPTOM OF MECHANICAL INTESTINAL OBSTRUCTION***, followed by vomiting, distension and absolute constipation. ***SMALL BOWEL DILATATION >3 cm IS SIGNIFICANT*** (colon >6 cm, caecum >9 cm). ***A PATIENT WITH PREVIOUS ABDOMINAL SURGERY, COLICKY PAIN, BILIOUS VOMITING AND HYPERPERISTALSIS WITH STEP-LADDER AIR–FLUID LEVELS HAS ADHESIVE OBSTRUCTION — THE NEXT STEP IS CECT ABDOMEN (not immediate laparotomy)***, which confirms the level, cause and any strangulation.

**UNDERSTAND:** The symptom sequence is dictated by ***where the obstruction is.*** ***HIGH (proximal) obstruction: EARLY, PROFUSE VOMITING with rapid dehydration and MINIMAL distension. LOW (distal) obstruction: PAIN AND DISTENSION PREDOMINATE, with LATE, feculent vomiting.*** And the reason CT precedes surgery in adhesive obstruction is that ***most adhesive obstructions settle with conservative management ("drip and suck") — CT identifies the minority with STRANGULATION, closed-loop obstruction or a transition point needing immediate operation.***

**MUST KNOW:**

- ***SIGNS DEMANDING IMMEDIATE SURGERY: peritonism, continuous (rather than colicky) pain, tachycardia, fever, leucocytosis, acidosis, a tender irreducible hernia, or CT features of strangulation (bowel wall thickening, poor enhancement, mesenteric fat stranding, free fluid, pneumatosis, portal venous gas).***
- **Strangulation principles: ***VENOUS OUTFLOW IS OCCLUDED BEFORE ARTERIAL INFLOW (hence congestion, then infarction); a CLOSED-LOOP obstruction strangulates RAPIDLY; RICHTER'S HERNIA (only part of the circumference is caught) STRANGULATES WITHOUT COMPLETE OBSTRUCTION — so ABSOLUTE CONSTIPATION IS NOT A FEATURE.*** ***IN LARGE BOWEL OBSTRUCTION WITH A COMPETENT ILEOCAECAL VALVE IT IS THE CAECUM (thinnest wall, greatest diameter — Laplace's law), NOT the splenic flexure, THAT IS MOST PRONE TO ISCHAEMIA AND PERFORATION.***
- ***THE COMMONEST CAUSE OF SMALL BOWEL OBSTRUCTION IS ADHESIONS (then hernia, then malignancy); OF LARGE BOWEL OBSTRUCTION IT IS CARCINOMA.***
- **Management: ***nil by mouth, nasogastric decompression, intravenous fluids and electrolyte correction, analgesia, catheter and close observation; WATER-SOLUBLE CONTRAST (GASTROGRAFFIN) IS BOTH PROGNOSTIC AND THERAPEUTIC in adhesive obstruction — contrast reaching the colon within 24 hours predicts resolution.***
- ***ACUTE COLONIC PSEUDO-OBSTRUCTION (OGILVIE SYNDROME) — massive colonic dilatation WITHOUT mechanical obstruction, in the elderly, post-operative or critically ill — IS TREATED WITH CORRECTION OF ELECTROLYTES, DECOMPRESSION AND INTRAVENOUS NEOSTIGMINE (with atropine and cardiac monitoring available), then colonoscopic decompression.***

**EXAM CONNECTION:** First symptom in obstruction → **colicky pain**; significant small bowel dilatation → **>3 cm**; adhesive obstruction next step → **CECT abdomen**; incorrect about strangulation → **that the splenic flexure is most prone to ischaemia in large bowel obstruction**; obstipation not a necessary feature → **Richter's hernia**; IV neostigmine → **colonic pseudo-obstruction**.

**REMEMBER:** *Vomit early means high, distend early means low — and the caecum bursts first.*

### 32. Small Bowel Anatomy, Malrotation and Ileocaecal Tuberculosis

**PEARL:** ***THE JEJUNUM HAS A WIDER LUMEN, THICKER WALL, LONGER VASA RECTA AND FEWER ARCADES; THE ILEUM MAKES UP ABOUT 60% OF SMALL BOWEL LENGTH; THE LIGAMENT OF TREITZ MARKS THE DUODENOJEJUNAL FLEXURE.*** ***ILEAL RESECTION IS TOLERATED WORSE THAN JEJUNAL RESECTION*** (the ileum alone absorbs B12 and bile salts and contains the ileocaecal valve). ***IN MALROTATION, THE CAECUM AND APPENDIX LIE ON THE LEFT; LADD'S PROCEDURE DIVIDES LADD'S BANDS, BROADENS THE MESENTERY, PERFORMS AN INCIDENTAL APPENDICECTOMY AND PLACES THE SMALL BOWEL ON THE RIGHT AND THE COLON ON THE LEFT — THE CAECUM IS DELIBERATELY LEFT ON THE LEFT, NOT FIXED TO THE RIGHT.***

**UNDERSTAND:** Ladd's procedure looks paradoxical until you see its aim: ***it does not restore normal anatomy — it creates a NON-ROTATED anatomy with the widest possible mesenteric base***, because volvulus happens when the midgut hangs from a narrow pedicle. Broadening that base is what prevents recurrence; the appendix is removed because it will lie in the left upper quadrant and would confuse any future presentation.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Short bowel syndrome: ***the ileum can adapt to compensate for jejunal loss, but the jejunum CANNOT replace ileal functions — so ileal resection causes B12 deficiency, BILE-SALT DIARRHOEA, steatorrhoea, GALLSTONES and OXALATE RENAL STONES.*** ***LOSS OF THE ILEOCAECAL VALVE WORSENS OUTCOME by allowing bacterial overgrowth and rapid transit.***
- **Ileocaecal tuberculosis: ***ANTI-TUBERCULAR CHEMOTHERAPY CURES MOST CASES; SURGERY IS RESERVED FOR COMPLETE/ACUTE OBSTRUCTION, PERFORATION, massive bleeding or an unresolving mass with diagnostic doubt*** — ulceration, a hyperplastic mass and diarrhoea are all managed medically. ***BEWARE PARADOXICAL WORSENING AND STRICTURE FORMATION DURING TREATMENT.***
- **Meckel's diverticulum causing bleeding is treated by ***DIVERTICULECTOMY/WEDGE RESECTION*** (segmental resection and anastomosis if the base is broad, the ulcer is in the adjacent ileum, or there is perforation).
- **Peutz–Jeghers syndrome: ***AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT, STK11/LKB1 on chromosome 19, MUCOCUTANEOUS PIGMENTATION and HAMARTOMATOUS (NOT neoplastic) polyps — chiefly in the SMALL BOWEL — presenting with intussusception and bleeding, with a greatly raised risk of GI, PANCREATIC, breast, ovarian and testicular cancers.***
- ***ENTEROSCOPY/CAPSULE ENDOSCOPY AND CT ENTEROGRAPHY ARE THE MODERN TOOLS FOR OBSCURE SMALL BOWEL DISEASE.***

**EXAM CONNECTION:** False about small bowel anatomy → **that ileal resections are better tolerated**; incorrect about malrotation → **that the caecum is fixed to the right**; when to operate in ileocaecal TB → **acute obstruction**; treatment of bleeding Meckel's → **wedge resection**; false about Peutz–Jeghers → **that the polyps are neoplastic and predominantly colonic**.

**REMEMBER:** *Ladd's does not straighten the gut — it widens the mesentery so it cannot twist.*

### 33. Enterocutaneous Fistula — What Closes and What Doesn't

**PEARL:** A fistula tract **longer than 2.5 cm** is likely to close spontaneously; **<2.5 cm is not**. Everything else in the FRIEND list *prevents* closure.

**UNDERSTAND:** A long tract behaves like a narrow tunnel — flow resistance is high, the tract collapses and granulates. A short tract is effectively a direct stoma to skin, and epithelium creeps across it, permanently lining the communication so it can never granulate shut. This is why **epithelialisation** is a bar to closure, not an aid.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **FRIEND** = factors preventing spontaneous closure: **F**oreign body, **R**adiation, **I**nflammation/**I**BD, **E**pithelialisation, **N**eoplasm, **D**istal obstruction. (Add: high output, sepsis, short tract, malnutrition.)
- **Output classification:** high **>500 mL/day**, intermediate 200–500, low **<200 mL/day**. High output = less likely to close, more electrolyte loss.
- **Duodenal fistula causes the most malnutrition** — proximal *plus* it loses an extra 1–2 L/day of pancreatic and biliary secretions on top of gastric losses. Distal ileal and colonic fistulae are the easiest to manage.
- **SNAP** management: **S**epsis control + **S**kin protection → **N**utrition (TPN) → **A**natomy (fistulogram/CT) → **P**lanned definitive surgery.
- Wait **6 weeks–3 months** before definitive surgery; **octreotide** reduces output but does not improve closure rates.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Either "which factor **aids** spontaneous closure" (answer: the long tract — everything else listed is a FRIEND) or "which fistula causes maximum malnutrition" (**duodenal**).

**REMEMBER:** *Long tract closes; short, lined, fed-by-a-foreign-body tract never will.*

### 34. Adhesive Small Bowel Obstruction

**PEARL:** Colicky pain, **bilious vomiting** and hyperperistalsis **years after laparotomy** = **adhesive small bowel obstruction** — adhesions are the **commonest cause of SBO in the operated abdomen**. Investigation of choice is **CECT abdomen**, which identifies the transition point and, critically, signs of **strangulation**.

**UNDERSTAND:** The reason CT — not immediate laparotomy — is the next step is that **most adhesive obstructions settle non-operatively**, while a strangulating obstruction must be operated on within hours. Only CT can separate them: it shows the **transition zone**, bowel wall thickening, **reduced/absent mural enhancement, mesenteric fat stranding, closed-loop configuration and free fluid**. Operating on everyone creates the adhesions that cause the next episode.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Initial management ("drip and suck"):** NBM, **nasogastric decompression**, IV fluids, correct electrolytes, catheterise and monitor output, serial examination.
- **Operate immediately for:** peritonitis, strangulation/ischaemia on CT, **closed-loop obstruction**, failure to resolve in **48–72 hours**, or a **virgin abdomen** (no previous surgery → a mechanical cause needing definitive treatment).
- **Gastrografin (water-soluble contrast) challenge** is both diagnostic and therapeutic — contrast reaching the colon within 24 hours predicts non-operative resolution and speeds it.
- Distinguish from **paralytic ileus**: ileus is **painless with ABSENT bowel sounds**, gas throughout including the colon and rectum, usually early post-operative or metabolic (hypokalaemia, opioids, sepsis); obstruction has **colic, hyperactive high-pitched sounds and a gasless distal colon**.
- Classic X-ray: **valvulae conniventes crossing the whole lumen, central dilated loops, multiple air–fluid levels** (SBO) vs **peripheral loops with haustra not crossing the lumen** (LBO).
- Other causes of SBO: **hernia (commonest worldwide and in the un-operated abdomen)**, malignancy, Crohn's stricture, intussusception, gallstone ileus, bezoar, TB.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The stem pairs a diagnosis with a next step — the answer must contain **both correctly**. Adhesive obstruction + **CECT**, not straight to laparotomy in a stable patient without peritonitis.

**REMEMBER:** *Drip and suck while the CT decides whether to cut.*

### 35. Postoperative Ileus vs Mechanical Obstruction

**PEARL:** Distension with **bilious vomiting after starting oral feeds on day 4** and a **non-tender abdomen** is **postoperative ileus (or early adhesive obstruction)** → **stop orals, insert an NG tube, check electrolytes (especially POTASSIUM) and get an abdominal X-ray**. **Nasojejunal feeding is the wrong answer** — you do not feed a gut that is not working.

**UNDERSTAND:** The intolerance of feeding *is* the sign that transit has failed; delivering nutrition beyond the pylorus does nothing about the distended, atonic stomach and small bowel proximal to it, and risks aspiration. The immediate priorities are the **decompression** of the obstructed segment and the **correction of the reversible causes** — of which **hypokalaemia** is the most important and most commonly missed, since potassium is essential for smooth-muscle depolarisation and gut motility.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Normal return of motility:** small bowel within hours, stomach 24–48 hours, **colon 48–72 hours** — ileus is diagnosed when this fails to happen or reverses.
- **Causes to look for and correct:** **hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hyponatraemia**, opioids, anticholinergics, sepsis/intra-abdominal collection or **anastomotic leak**, retroperitoneal haematoma, prolonged bowel handling.
- **Ileus vs mechanical obstruction:** ileus is **painless with absent/quiet bowel sounds** and gas distributed throughout **including the colon and rectum**; mechanical obstruction has **colic, high-pitched hyperactive sounds** and a **gasless distal bowel** with a transition point on CT.
- **Management:** NBM, **NG decompression**, IV fluids, correct electrolytes, stop/minimise opioids (use epidural or NSAID-based analgesia), mobilise, exclude sepsis with **CT if it persists beyond 5–7 days** — an unresolving "ileus" after bowel surgery is an **anastomotic leak until proven otherwise**.
- **Prevention (ERAS):** minimally invasive surgery, avoidance of fluid overload, opioid-sparing analgesia, **chewing gum**, early feeding and early mobilisation.
- **Ogilvie syndrome (acute colonic pseudo-obstruction):** massive colonic dilatation without mechanical cause in the elderly/immobile; **caecal diameter >12 cm risks perforation** → **neostigmine** (with cardiac monitoring) or colonoscopic decompression.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "All of the following EXCEPT" — the exception is always the option that **continues feeding**. Everything decompressive or diagnostic is correct.

**REMEMBER:** *Decompress and check the potassium — never feed a silent gut.*

### 36. Superior Mesenteric Artery (Wilkie) Syndrome

**PEARL:** SMA syndrome = compression of the **THIRD PART OF THE DUODENUM** between the SMA and the aorta when the **aortomesenteric ANGLE NARROWS to LESS than 25° (normal 38–65°)** — so "angle more than 70 degrees" is the false statement. Treatment is **nutritional restoration first; DUODENOJEJUNOSTOMY if that fails.**

**UNDERSTAND:** The duodenum's third part is the only bowel that passes through a **fixed bony-vascular tunnel**, and what holds that tunnel open is the **retroperitoneal FAT PAD at the SMA origin**. Any process that strips that fat — **profound weight loss, cachexia, malabsorption, burns, anorexia nervosa** — collapses the angle onto the duodenum. This creates a vicious cycle: obstruction → vomiting → further weight loss → a narrower angle. Restoring the fat pad by feeding **beyond** the obstruction (nasojejunal or parenteral nutrition) reverses the mechanism, which is why nutrition is the treatment, not merely supportive care.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Presentation: postprandial epigastric pain and BILIOUS vomiting, early satiety, weight loss — classically RELIEVED BY THE PRONE, KNEE–CHEST or LEFT LATERAL position** (which lets the SMA fall away from the aorta).
- **Precipitants: rapid growth spurt in adolescence, severe weight loss (any cause), prolonged bed rest, spinal surgery or a body cast for scoliosis (increasing lumbar lordosis), and after bariatric surgery.**
- **Investigation of choice: CT ANGIOGRAPHY with measurement of the aortomesenteric angle and distance (normal 10–28 mm; SMA syndrome <8 mm)**; barium shows an abrupt vertical cut-off in the third part with **to-and-fro peristalsis**.
- **Surgery: DUODENOJEJUNOSTOMY is the operation of choice** (better results than gastrojejunostomy or Strong's procedure, which divides the ligament of Treitz).
- Related compression syndromes: **NUTCRACKER syndrome (left renal vein between SMA and aorta — haematuria, flank pain, varicocele), MEDIAN ARCUATE LIGAMENT (coeliac artery compression — postprandial pain and an epigastric bruit), and MAY–THURNER syndrome (left common iliac vein compressed by the right common iliac artery → left-sided DVT).**

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Which is INCORRECT?" — the false option inverts the **angle**. Anchor the number: **narrow (<25°) obstructs.**

**REMEMBER:** *Lose the fat pad, close the angle, trap the duodenum.*

### 39. Meckel's Diverticulum

### 40. Familial Adenomatous Polyposis and Lynch Syndrome

**PEARL:** ***THE FIRST-LINE SCREENING TEST FOR AN AT-RISK CHILD IN AN FAP FAMILY IS GENETIC TESTING FOR THE APC MUTATION*** (with annual flexible sigmoidoscopy from age 10–12 if positive or if testing is unavailable). ***EXTRACOLONIC MANIFESTATIONS OF FAP: duodenal/periampullary adenomas and carcinoma, gastric fundic gland polyps, HEPATOBLASTOMA, thyroid carcinoma, EPIDERMOID CYSTS, pilomatrixoma, CHRPE, DESMOID tumours, OSTEOMAS and dental anomalies — RENAL CELL CARCINOMA IS NOT ONE.*** ***IN GARDNER SYNDROME THE COMMONEST FACIAL ABNORMALITY IS MULTIPLE OSTEOMAS (mandible commonest).*** ***THE AMSTERDAM II CRITERIA (for LYNCH syndrome, NOT FAP) REQUIRE AT LEAST 2 SUCCESSIVE GENERATIONS AFFECTED.***

**UNDERSTAND:** Screening an FAP family starts with genetics rather than endoscopy because ***a negative APC test in a family with a KNOWN mutation completely releases that child from a lifetime of colonoscopies***, while a positive test justifies starting surveillance in childhood and planning ***PROPHYLACTIC COLECTOMY in the teens or twenties — before cancer, which is otherwise INEVITABLE by the fourth decade.*** Genetic testing is thus not an academic exercise but the decision that shapes the child's next twenty years.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **The ***AMSTERDAM II ("3-2-1") CRITERIA: ≥3 relatives with a Lynch-associated cancer (colorectal, endometrial, small bowel, ureter or renal pelvis), one a FIRST-DEGREE relative of the other two; ≥2 SUCCESSIVE GENERATIONS; ≥1 diagnosed BEFORE AGE 50; FAP EXCLUDED; and tumours verified by PATHOLOGICAL (not radiological) examination.*** The ***BETHESDA guidelines*** determine who should have tumour MSI/IHC testing.
- **Surgery in FAP: ***PROCTOCOLECTOMY WITH ILEAL POUCH–ANAL ANASTOMOSIS (or colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis if rectal polyps are few, with continued rectal surveillance).*** ***DESMOID TUMOURS AND PERIAMPULLARY CARCINOMA BECOME THE LEADING CAUSES OF DEATH AFTER COLECTOMY***, so upper GI surveillance continues lifelong.
- **The ***ADENOMA–CARCINOMA SEQUENCE: APC loss initiates the adenoma (earliest event); KRAS activation drives its growth; SMAD4/DCC loss and — crucially — p53 MUTATION MARKS THE TRANSITION FROM ADENOMA TO INVASIVE CARCINOMA.***
- **Lynch syndrome (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, EPCAM): ***right-sided, poorly differentiated/mucinous tumours, synchronous and metachronous cancers, endometrial cancer in women, and MSI-HIGH tumours that respond exceptionally to CHECKPOINT INHIBITORS.***
- **MUTYH-associated polyposis is the ***AUTOSOMAL RECESSIVE*** mimic of attenuated FAP.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Screening children in an FAP family → **APC genetic testing**; NOT an extracolonic manifestation of FAP → **renal cell carcinoma**; commonest facial abnormality in Gardner syndrome → **multiple osteomas**; correct about Amsterdam II criteria → **at least 2 successive generations affected**; marker of transition to invasive carcinoma → **p53**.

**REMEMBER:** *Test the gene first — a negative result frees the child, a positive one starts the clock.*

### 41. Colorectal Cancer — Screening, Staging and Symptoms

**PEARL:** ***COLONOSCOPY IS THE GOLD-STANDARD DIAGNOSTIC TEST BUT NOT THE POPULATION SCREENING TEST OF CHOICE — screening is by FAECAL IMMUNOCHEMICAL TESTING (FIT)/faecal occult blood, with colonoscopy for positives.*** ***THE MOST IMPORTANT PROGNOSTIC DETERMINANT IS TUMOUR STAGE, ESPECIALLY LYMPH NODE STATUS***; ***LIVER METASTASES MAY BE RESECTABLE WITH ~40% 5-YEAR SURVIVAL***; and modern protocols ***DO permit mechanical bowel preparation with oral antibiotics*** (which reduces surgical site infection, anastomotic leak and reoperation). ***THE EARLIEST AND COMMONEST SYMPTOM OF RECTAL CANCER IS PAINLESS BLEEDING PER RECTUM.***

**UNDERSTAND:** ***THE SCREENING TEST AND THE DIAGNOSTIC TEST ANSWER DIFFERENT QUESTIONS.*** A screening test must be cheap, safe, acceptable and applicable to millions of asymptomatic people — hence FIT. Colonoscopy is the best *diagnostic* and therapeutic tool (it both finds and removes adenomas) but is invasive, expensive and carries a perforation risk, so it is reserved for ***symptomatic patients, positive screens and high-risk groups*** — where it is unequivocally the investigation of choice.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Presentation by site: ***RIGHT-SIDED — iron-deficiency ANAEMIA, weight loss, a vague mass (the caecum is capacious and stool is liquid, so obstruction is late); LEFT-SIDED — altered bowel habit, obstruction, tenesmus and BLEEDING.*** ***ANY IRON-DEFICIENCY ANAEMIA IN A MAN OR POSTMENOPAUSAL WOMAN NEEDS COLONIC EVALUATION.***
- ***NEOPLASM IS THE COMMONEST CAUSE OF LARGE BOWEL OBSTRUCTION IN ADULTS*** (adhesions are the commonest cause of SMALL bowel obstruction); volvulus, diverticular stricture and hernia follow.
- **Lymphatic drainage decides the nodal field: ***RECTAL CANCER DRAINS UPWARD TO THE MESENTERIC/PARA-AORTIC NODES AND DOES NOT NORMALLY GO TO THE INGUINAL NODES; the ANAL CANAL BELOW THE DENTATE LINE, the PENIS/scrotum and the LOWER LIMB drain to the SUPERFICIAL INGUINAL NODES.***
- **Treatment: ***right/left hemicolectomy or anterior resection with total mesorectal excision; ABDOMINOPERINEAL RESECTION with a permanent colostomy for very low rectal tumours; NEOADJUVANT CHEMORADIOTHERAPY for locally advanced rectal cancer; adjuvant chemotherapy (FOLFOX) for node-positive disease; and metastasectomy for resectable liver or lung disease.*** CEA is for follow-up, not diagnosis.
- **Proctoscopy visualises the ***anal canal, anorectal junction and lower rectum (about 10 cm)***; the rigid sigmoidoscope reaches ~25 cm and the flexible sigmoidoscope 60 cm.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Incorrect about colorectal cancer → **that flexible colonoscopy is the screening test of choice**; commonest cause of colonic obstruction → **neoplasm**; least likely to cause inguinal lymphadenopathy → **rectum**; earliest symptom of rectal cancer → **bleeding per rectum**; length seen at proctoscopy → **10 cm**.

**REMEMBER:** *Screen the population with stool, investigate the patient with a scope.*

### 42. Inflammatory Bowel Disease — Surgical Indications

**PEARL:** ***INDICATIONS FOR SURGERY IN ULCERATIVE COLITIS: failure of medical therapy/STEROID DEPENDENCE, acute severe colitis or TOXIC MEGACOLON, perforation, uncontrollable haemorrhage, DYSPLASIA OR CARCINOMA, GROWTH RETARDATION in children, and intractable EXTRA-INTESTINAL manifestations — but NOT simply having LEFT-SIDED COLITIS***, which is a disease distribution, not an indication. ***THE COMMONEST EXTRA-INTESTINAL MANIFESTATION OF ULCERATIVE COLITIS IS ARTHROPATHY.***

**UNDERSTAND:** ***COLECTOMY CURES ULCERATIVE COLITIS BUT NOT CROHN DISEASE*** — because UC is confined to the colon and rectum, whereas Crohn can recur anywhere in the gut. That single fact governs surgical philosophy: ***in UC, surgery is definitive and even prophylactic (against cancer); in Crohn, surgery is RESERVED FOR COMPLICATIONS and is as CONSERVATIVE AS POSSIBLE (stricturoplasty, limited resection), since repeated resections lead to SHORT BOWEL SYNDROME.***

**MUST KNOW:**

- **UC surgery: ***total PROCTOCOLECTOMY WITH ILEAL POUCH–ANAL ANASTOMOSIS is the standard elective operation; in the acute severe/toxic setting, SUBTOTAL COLECTOMY WITH END ILEOSTOMY is safest, with reconstruction later.*** ***POUCHITIS is the commonest late complication (treated with metronidazole/ciprofloxacin).***
- **Extra-intestinal manifestations that track disease activity: ***peripheral ARTHROPATHY, ERYTHEMA NODOSUM, episcleritis and aphthous ulcers.*** Those that do NOT: ***ANKYLOSING SPONDYLITIS/sacroiliitis, PRIMARY SCLEROSING CHOLANGITIS, uveitis and PYODERMA GANGRENOSUM.*** ***PSC RUNS AN INDEPENDENT COURSE AND PERSISTS AFTER COLECTOMY, AND MULTIPLIES THE COLORECTAL CANCER RISK — so surveillance is annual from diagnosis.***
- **Acute severe colitis: ***TRUELOVE AND WITTS criteria; treat with IV steroids, VTE prophylaxis and daily review; assess response at day 3 (TRAVIS criteria) and RESCUE with infliximab or ciclosporin — with SURGERY IF THERE IS NO RESPONSE BY DAY 5–7.*** ***DELAYING COLECTOMY IN A DETERIORATING PATIENT IS THE CLASSIC AVOIDABLE CAUSE OF DEATH.***
- **Toxic megacolon: ***transverse colon >6 cm with systemic toxicity — AVOID antimotility agents, opioids and colonoscopy.***
- **Crohn surgery: ***for stricture, fistula, abscess, perforation or failure of medical therapy — with STRICTUROPLASTY preferred over resection where feasible.***

**EXAM CONNECTION:** NOT an indication for surgery in UC → **left-sided colitis**; commonest extra-intestinal manifestation of UC → **arthropathy**.

**REMEMBER:** *Colectomy cures colitis but never cures Crohn — so cut generously in one and sparingly in the other.*

### 43. Diverticular Disease and Lower GI Bleeding

**PEARL:** ***THE HINCHEY CLASSIFICATION OF PERFORATED DIVERTICULITIS: I — pericolic/mesenteric abscess; II — pelvic or retroperitoneal abscess; III — GENERALISED PURULENT peritonitis; IV — GENERALISED FAECAL peritonitis.*** ***IN A PATIENT OVER 60 WITH PAINLESS, SOMETIMES MASSIVE, LOWER GI BLEEDING AND A RECENT NORMAL SCREEN, COLONOSCOPY SHOWING DILATED TORTUOUS MUCOSAL AND SUBMUCOSAL VEINS = ANGIODYSPLASIA*** (classically right-sided and associated with AORTIC STENOSIS — HEYDE SYNDROME).

**UNDERSTAND:** Hinchey grading exists because ***it dictates the operation.*** ***Hinchey I–II are usually managed with ANTIBIOTICS ± PERCUTANEOUS DRAINAGE; Hinchey III–IV require surgery — traditionally HARTMANN'S PROCEDURE (resection with end colostomy), with primary anastomosis ± diverting ileostomy or laparoscopic lavage in selected cases.*** The distinction between purulent and faecal peritonitis matters because ***faecal contamination carries much higher mortality and effectively mandates resection.***

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Diverticular disease facts: ***diverticula are FALSE (pulsion) diverticula herniating where the vasa recta penetrate the muscle; commonest in the SIGMOID colon in Western populations (RIGHT-sided in Asians); caused by a low-fibre diet and raised intraluminal pressure.*** ***DIVERTICULOSIS IS THE COMMONEST CAUSE OF MASSIVE LOWER GI BLEEDING (bleeding is typically painless and from the RIGHT colon even when the diverticula are left-sided).***
- ***COLONOSCOPY IS CONTRAINDICATED IN ACUTE DIVERTICULITIS (perforation risk) — CT IS THE INVESTIGATION OF CHOICE; colonoscopy follows 6–8 weeks later to exclude carcinoma.***
- **Approach to lower GI bleeding: ***resuscitate → EXCLUDE AN UPPER GI SOURCE (a nasogastric aspirate or upper endoscopy — brisk upper bleeding can present as fresh red blood) → COLONOSCOPY when stable; for ongoing bleeding, CT ANGIOGRAPHY (detects bleeding ≥0.3 mL/min) then CATHETER ANGIOGRAPHY WITH EMBOLISATION; a nuclear-medicine RBC scan for slow intermittent bleeding; surgery as the last resort.***
- **Causes of lower GI bleeding by age: ***children — MECKEL'S diverticulum, polyps, intussusception; adults — haemorrhoids, fissure, IBD, infective colitis; elderly — DIVERTICULOSIS, ANGIODYSPLASIA, ISCHAEMIC COLITIS and MALIGNANCY.***
- ***HAEMORRHOIDS AND FISSURE ARE THE COMMONEST CAUSES OVERALL — BUT NEVER ATTRIBUTE RECTAL BLEEDING TO THEM IN AN OLDER PATIENT WITHOUT EXCLUDING CANCER.***

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Faecal peritonitis from diverticular perforation → **Hinchey stage 4**; syncope with painless rectal bleeding and a recent normal screen → **angiodysplasia (dilated submucosal veins)**.

**REMEMBER:** *Hinchey tells you whether to drain it or resect it.*

### 44. Volvulus

**PEARL:** ***CAECAL VOLVULUS: commoner in WOMEN in the fourth-to-fifth decades, a CLOCKWISE twist, with a "BIRD-BEAK" tapering on contrast study and a dilated loop pointing to the LEFT UPPER QUADRANT.*** ***CAECOPEXY HAS HIGH RECURRENCE RATES — RIGHT HEMICOLECTOMY (or caecostomy in the unfit) IS PREFERRED.*** ***SIGMOID VOLVULUS gives the "COFFEE-BEAN"/inverted-U loop arising from the pelvis and pointing to the right upper quadrant.***

**UNDERSTAND:** Volvulus needs ***a long mobile loop on a narrow mesenteric base***, which is why the sigmoid (chronic constipation, high-fibre diets, Chagas disease, institutionalised elderly) and a congenitally unfixed caecum are the two sites affected. The therapeutic difference follows anatomy: ***THE SIGMOID CAN BE DECOMPRESSED FROM BELOW BY FLEXIBLE SIGMOIDOSCOPY AND A FLATUS TUBE, WHILE THE CAECUM CANNOT BE REACHED — so caecal volvulus is a primarily surgical problem.***

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Sigmoid volvulus: ***ENDOSCOPIC DETORSION SUCCEEDS IN 70–90% and is first-line if there is no peritonitis or gangrene — but RECURRENCE IS ~50%, so ELECTIVE SIGMOID RESECTION IS OFFERED DURING THE SAME ADMISSION.*** ***GANGRENE, PERITONITIS, PERFORATION OR FAILED DECOMPRESSION MEAN IMMEDIATE LAPAROTOMY (HARTMANN'S PROCEDURE).***
- ***BLOODY EFFLUENT OR A NECROTIC MUCOSA SEEN AT ENDOSCOPIC DETORSION IS AN ABSOLUTE INDICATION TO STOP AND OPERATE.***
- **Other volvuli: ***GASTRIC volvulus (BORCHARDT'S TRIAD — unproductive retching, epigastric pain and inability to pass a nasogastric tube; organoaxial or mesenteroaxial); MIDGUT volvulus in the neonate (malrotation — a surgical emergency); ILEOSIGMOID KNOTTING (a rapidly gangrenous double volvulus, notably reported from India and Africa).***
- **Volvulus is a form of ***CLOSED-LOOP OBSTRUCTION and therefore strangulates rapidly*** — the reason delay is so costly.
- ***SIGMOID VOLVULUS IS A SUBSTANTIALLY COMMONER CAUSE OF LARGE BOWEL OBSTRUCTION IN INDIA AND AFRICA THAN IN THE WEST***, and should be high in the differential in an elderly patient with gross distension and few air–fluid levels.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Incorrect about caecal volvulus → **that recurrence rates are low with caecopexy**.

**REMEMBER:** *A sigmoid volvulus can be untwisted from below; a caecal one has to be resected.*

### 45. Rectal Cancer — Staging and Operations

**PEARL:** ***DUKES STAGING: A — confined to the bowel WALL (>90% 5-year survival); B — extends THROUGH the wall into extra-rectal tissue but NODE-NEGATIVE (~70%); C — REGIONAL NODES involved (C1 pararectal only, C2 up to the vessel origin, ~40%); D (added later) — distant metastases.*** ***HARTMANN'S OPERATION (resection with an END COLOSTOMY and closure of the rectal stump) IS USED WHEN AN ANASTOMOSIS IS UNSAFE OR SPHINCTER FUNCTION IS POOR — NOT when sphincter function is good.*** ***ABDOMINOPERINEAL RESECTION IS FOR TUMOURS TOO CLOSE TO THE SPHINCTER***, and ***ENDOLUMINAL STENTING relieves obstruction as a bridge to surgery or for palliation.***

**UNDERSTAND:** The whole surgical decision in rectal cancer is ***HOW FAR THE TUMOUR IS FROM THE ANAL SPHINCTER.*** If a distal margin can be obtained while preserving the sphincter complex, an ***ANTERIOR RESECTION with a stapled colorectal or coloanal anastomosis (usually with a defunctioning ileostomy)*** is performed. If not, the sphincter must be sacrificed — ***APR with a PERMANENT colostomy.*** Hartmann's sits between them: a resection performed when the patient is too unwell, obstructed or contaminated for an anastomosis to heal.

**MUST KNOW:**

- ***TOTAL MESORECTAL EXCISION (TME) IS THE ONCOLOGICAL STANDARD*** — sharp dissection along the mesorectal fascial plane — and has transformed local recurrence rates. ***MRI PELVIS DEFINES THE CIRCUMFERENTIAL RESECTION MARGIN AND SELECTS PATIENTS FOR NEOADJUVANT CHEMORADIOTHERAPY.***
- **Post-operative issues: ***ANASTOMOTIC LEAK (the most feared complication — fever, tachycardia, ileus and pain around days 5–7), LOW ANTERIOR RESECTION SYNDROME (urgency, frequency and clustering), and stoma complications.***
- ***THE MODERN STAGING SYSTEM IS TNM; DUKES REMAINS EXAMINED BECAUSE IT ENCAPSULATES THE PROGNOSTIC LOGIC — wall, then nodes, then distant spread.***
- **"Watch and wait" is now an option after a ***complete clinical response to chemoradiotherapy*** in selected rectal cancers.
- **Colorectal cancer surveillance after resection: ***colonoscopy at 1 year, CEA and CT surveillance for 5 years.***

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Tumour through the wall without nodes → **Dukes B**; false about rectal cancer surgery → **that Hartmann's is used when sphincter function is good**.

**REMEMBER:** *Distance from the sphincter decides the operation; nodes decide the prognosis.*

### 46. Benign Anorectal Disease

**PEARL:** ***SEVERE PAIN DURING AND AFTER DEFECATION WITH BRIGHT BLOOD STREAKING THE STOOL AND MARKED ANAL SPASM = FISSURE-IN-ANO*** (posterior midline in 90%). ***THE CRYPTOGLANDULAR THEORY EXPLAINS ANORECTAL ABSCESS — infection begins in an INTERSPHINCTERIC anal gland — and the COMMONEST TYPE IS PERIANAL (not ischiorectal)***; abscesses are drained by a ***CRUCIATE INCISION.*** ***A HIGH (SUPRASPHINCTERIC) FISTULA IS MANAGED WITH A SETON***, since laying it open would divide the sphincter and cause incontinence. ***URINARY RETENTION IS THE COMMONEST COMPLICATION AFTER HAEMORRHOIDECTOMY (15–30%).***

**UNDERSTAND:** Anal fissure is a ***self-perpetuating ischaemic ulcer***: pain causes internal sphincter spasm, spasm reduces blood flow in the ***POSTERIOR MIDLINE — the least perfused part of the anal canal*** — and poor perfusion prevents healing, which causes more pain. That is why treatment targets the spasm rather than the ulcer: ***stool softeners and fibre, plus TOPICAL GTN or DILTIAZEM (chemical sphincterotomy), botulinum toxin, and finally LATERAL INTERNAL SPHINCTEROTOMY*** (the definitive operation, with a small risk of incontinence).

**MUST KNOW:**

- ***A FISSURE THAT IS LATERAL, MULTIPLE OR PAINLESS IS NOT A SIMPLE FISSURE — think CROHN DISEASE, TUBERCULOSIS, SYPHILIS, HIV OR MALIGNANCY.***
- ***GOODSALL'S RULE: a fistula with an EXTERNAL opening ANTERIOR to the transverse anal line tracks in a STRAIGHT radial line to the anal canal; a POSTERIOR opening curves to the POSTERIOR MIDLINE.*** ***PARKS' CLASSIFICATION: intersphincteric (commonest), transsphincteric, suprasphincteric and extrasphincteric.***
- **Haemorrhoids: ***internal (above the dentate line, PAINLESS bleeding, graded I–IV) versus external (below, painful when thrombosed); treated by fibre and fluids, RUBBER-BAND LIGATION or sclerotherapy for grades I–II, and haemorrhoidectomy or stapled/Doppler-guided procedures for III–IV.***
- **Pilonidal sinus: ***a disease of YOUNG HAIRY ADULTS (NOT present at birth), caused by hair penetrating the natal cleft skin and setting up a foreign-body reaction (HAIR FOLLICLES ARE NOT PRESENT IN THE SINUS WALL); an INTERDIGITAL form is an occupational disease of BARBERS.*** Treatment: hygiene and hair removal, drainage of abscess, then excision with primary off-midline closure (Karydakis, Limberg flap).
- ***PERIANAL SEPSIS IN A DIABETIC OR IMMUNOCOMPROMISED PATIENT MAY BE FOURNIER'S GANGRENE — a necrotising fasciitis needing immediate radical debridement.***

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Pain on defecation with anal spasm → **fissure-in-ano**; incorrect about anorectal abscess → **that ischiorectal is the commonest type**; suprasphincteric fistula → **seton technique**; commonest complication after haemorrhoidectomy → **urinary retention**; incorrect about pilonidal sinus → **that it presents at birth**.

**REMEMBER:** *Spasm starves the posterior midline — relax the sphincter and the fissure heals.*

### 47. Rectal Prolapse: Abdominal vs Perineal Approaches

**PEARL:** **RIPSTEIN's procedure is an ABDOMINAL operation** (anterior mesh rectopexy). The **PERINEAL** approaches are **DELORME's mucosectomy, ALTEMEIER's perineal rectosigmoidectomy, and THIERSCH's wiring/anal encirclement**.

**UNDERSTAND:** The choice of route is a trade-off between **durability and operative risk**. **Abdominal procedures fix the rectum to the sacrum, correct the underlying anatomy, and have the LOWEST recurrence rates** — but need a general anaesthetic and laparotomy or laparoscopy. **Perineal procedures avoid entering the abdomen**, so they suit the **frail, elderly, high-risk patient**, at the cost of **higher recurrence**. Recognising which category an eponym belongs to is therefore recognising which patient it was designed for.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Abdominal: Ripstein (anterior sling rectopexy), WELLS (posterior mesh rectopexy), suture rectopexy, resection rectopexy (FRYKMAN–GOLDBERG — adds sigmoid resection, useful when constipation coexists), and laparoscopic VENTRAL MESH RECTOPEXY (D'Hoore) — now the preferred modern operation.**
- **Perineal: DELORME — mucosal sleeve resection with plication of the muscularis, best for short full-thickness or mucosal prolapse; ALTEMEIER — full-thickness perineal rectosigmoidectomy, for longer prolapse and the emergency of an incarcerated/strangulated prolapse; THIERSCH — a wire or mesh encircling the anus, purely palliative in the unfit (risks impaction and erosion).**
- **Full-thickness prolapse shows CONCENTRIC rings of mucosa; mucosal prolapse and prolapsed haemorrhoids show RADIAL folds** — the bedside discriminator.
- **Associated findings: a deep pouch of Douglas, a redundant sigmoid, lax pelvic floor and a patulous anal sphincter with INCONTINENCE (present in up to 75%; it often improves after repair).** Assess with **defaecography, anorectal manometry and colonoscopy (to exclude a lead point — always look for a tumour).**
- **In CHILDREN, rectal prolapse is managed CONSERVATIVELY** — treat constipation, diarrhoea and malnutrition, and **exclude CYSTIC FIBROSIS and whipworm (*Trichuris*) infestation**; it almost always resolves without surgery.
- **Solitary rectal ulcer syndrome** is associated with internal intussusception and is treated by biofeedback, not primarily surgery.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Which is NOT a perineal approach?" — the answer is the **abdominal rectopexy (Ripstein or Wells)**. Learn the eponyms sorted into two columns.

**REMEMBER:** *Ripstein and Wells go through the belly; Delorme, Altemeier and Thiersch stay below.*

### 48. Nerve Injury in Rectal and Pelvic Surgery

**PEARL:** Ligating the **inferior mesenteric artery at its origin during abdominoperineal resection** damages the **SYMPATHETIC superior hypogastric plexus** → **RETROGRADE EJACULATION and BLADDER DYSFUNCTION**. **Parasympathetic (nervi erigentes, S2–S4) injury causes IMPOTENCE (failure of erection).**

**UNDERSTAND:** The autonomic division of labour in the male pelvis is captured by **"POINT and SHOOT": PARASYMPATHETIC = Point (erection); SYMPATHETIC = Shoot (emission and closure of the bladder neck during ejaculation)**. The **superior hypogastric plexus lies directly over the aortic bifurcation and the origin of the IMA**, so high ligation predictably injures the sympathetic fibres — abolishing bladder-neck closure, so semen passes backwards into the bladder.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Nerves at risk in rectal surgery: the superior hypogastric plexus (at the sacral promontory/IMA origin — sympathetic), the hypogastric nerves (along the pelvic sidewall), the NERVI ERIGENTES/pelvic splanchnics (S2–S4, at the lateral ligaments — parasympathetic), and the pelvic plexus (anterolaterally, near the seminal vesicles and DENONVILLIERS' fascia).**
- **TOTAL MESORECTAL EXCISION (TME) with sharp dissection in the avascular "holy plane" is designed to preserve these nerves** while removing the mesorectum en bloc; it reduced local recurrence from ~30% to <10%.
- **Abdominoperineal resection** (for very low rectal cancers involving the sphincter) leaves a **permanent end colostomy**; **anterior resection** preserves continuity.
- Other classic operative nerve injuries: **thyroidectomy — recurrent and external laryngeal nerves; parotidectomy — facial nerve and Frey syndrome (auriculotemporal); axillary dissection — long thoracic (winged scapula), thoracodorsal and intercostobrachial; inguinal hernia repair — ilioinguinal/iliohypogastric and genitofemoral (chronic groin pain); posterior triangle node biopsy — SPINAL ACCESSORY nerve.**
- **Retroperitoneal haematoma zones: ZONE 1 (central — aorta, IVC, pancreaticoduodenal) → ALWAYS explore, blunt or penetrating. ZONE 2 (lateral/perinephric) → explore only if EXPANDING, pulsatile or penetrating. ZONE 3 (pelvic) → do NOT explore in blunt trauma (pack and embolise); explore if penetrating.** The **MATTOX manoeuvre mobilises the LEFT colon medially to expose the aorta**; the **CATTELL–BRAASCH manoeuvre mobilises the right colon to expose the IVC**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Match the autonomic limb to the deficit. **Sympathetic → ejaculation and bladder; parasympathetic → erection.**

**REMEMBER:** *Parasympathetic points, sympathetic shoots — cut the sympathetics and semen goes backwards.*

### 49. Fistula-in-Ano: Simple vs Complex

**PEARL:** A **SINGLE, POSTERIOR, INTERSPHINCTERIC (low) fistula with no secondary extensions is a SIMPLE fistula → FISTULOTOMY** (lay it open). **Complex fistulae — high trans-sphincteric, suprasphincteric, extrasphincteric, anterior in a woman, multiple tracts, Crohn-related, or with prior incontinence — require sphincter-preserving procedures (LIFT, advancement flap, seton).**

**UNDERSTAND:** The whole decision is about **how much EXTERNAL SPHINCTER the tract crosses**. Fistulotomy is the most effective operation (>90% cure) precisely because it removes the tract completely — but it necessarily divides whatever muscle lies over it. Dividing a small amount of **distal internal sphincter** is safe; dividing a substantial part of the **external sphincter** causes **incontinence**. So the operation is chosen by asking how much muscle is at risk, which is why MRI or endoanal ultrasound is used to map the tract first.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **PARKS classification: INTERSPHINCTERIC (~70%, commonest), TRANS-sphincteric, SUPRA-sphincteric, EXTRA-sphincteric.**
- **GOODSALL's RULE: a fistula with an EXTERNAL opening POSTERIOR to the transverse anal line curves and opens in the MIDLINE POSTERIORLY (horseshoe); one ANTERIOR to it runs a direct RADIAL course to the nearest crypt.**
- **Origin: cryptoglandular infection of the anal glands in the intersphincteric plane (the same process that causes a perianal abscess — a fistula is the chronic phase of an abscess).**
- **Sphincter-preserving options: LIFT (Ligation of the Intersphincteric Fistula Tract), endorectal ADVANCEMENT FLAP, SETON (cutting or loose/draining), fibrin glue and fistula plug (low success), and VAAFT/laser ablation.** A **DRAINING SETON** is the safe first move in sepsis and in **Crohn disease**.
- **Always consider CROHN DISEASE, tuberculosis, malignancy and HIV in a complex, recurrent or anterior fistula** — treat the underlying disease (anti-TNF agents are highly effective for fistulising Crohn) and **avoid aggressive surgery**, which heals poorly.
- **MRI is the imaging gold standard**; **examination under anaesthesia** remains the definitive assessment.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The stem states the tract type and the number of openings. **Simple and low → fistulotomy**; anything high or multiple → a sphincter-sparing option.

**REMEMBER:** *Lay open what crosses little muscle; preserve the sphincter for everything else.*

### 50. Acute Colonic Pseudo-Obstruction (Ogilvie Syndrome)

**PEARL:** Risk factors are the conditions that **reduce colonic motility: recent SURGERY or trauma, OPIOIDS, and PSYCHIATRIC illness (through anticholinergic drugs)** — plus electrolyte disturbance, immobility, sepsis and severe medical illness. **Adhesions and fasting cause MECHANICAL obstruction and ileus respectively, not pseudo-obstruction.**

**UNDERSTAND:** Ogilvie syndrome is an **autonomic imbalance — excess sympathetic and deficient parasympathetic (sacral) input to the distal colon**, so the colon dilates without any mechanical block. That is why the **transition point is typically at the SPLENIC FLEXURE or descending colon** — the watershed where the vagal (parasympathetic) supply ends and the sacral supply begins — and why the treatment is a **parasympathomimetic (neostigmine)** rather than an operation.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Presentation: progressive abdominal distension with minimal pain and often preserved flatus, in a medically ill or postoperative patient.** X-ray shows **massive colonic dilatation with gas extending to the rectum**; a **WATER-SOLUBLE CONTRAST ENEMA (or CT) is the key investigation to exclude mechanical obstruction.**
- **CAECAL DIAMETER >12 cm (or rapid increase) predicts ISCHAEMIA and PERFORATION** — the caecum dilates most because of **Laplace's law** (largest radius, greatest wall tension).
- **Management ladder: (1) conservative — NBM, nasogastric and flatus tube, correct ELECTROLYTES (especially potassium and magnesium), stop opioids and anticholinergics, mobilise; (2) IV NEOSTIGMINE 2 mg with CARDIAC MONITORING and atropine at hand (risk of bradycardia and asystole; contraindicated in mechanical obstruction, asthma, bradycardia); (3) COLONOSCOPIC DECOMPRESSION; (4) surgery (caecostomy/colectomy) for perforation or ischaemia.**
- **Differentiate from mechanical obstruction (a transition point with collapsed distal bowel on CT) and from TOXIC MEGACOLON (systemic toxicity with colitis).**
- **Chronic intestinal pseudo-obstruction** is a different entity — a myopathy or neuropathy of the gut (scleroderma, amyloid, paraneoplastic, mitochondrial) with recurrent subacute obstruction.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Multi-select of risk factors. Include the **motility-suppressing** ones; exclude the mechanical ones (**adhesions**).

**REMEMBER:** *The colon is paralysed, not blocked — give neostigmine, not a knife.*

### 51. Nerve Injury in Rectal Surgery

**PEARL:** **High ligation of the INFERIOR MESENTERIC ARTERY injures the SUPERIOR HYPOGASTRIC PLEXUS (SYMPATHETIC) → RETROGRADE EJACULATION and bladder dysfunction.**

**UNDERSTAND:** Sympathetic fibres from **L1–L3 form the superior hypogastric plexus, which lies immediately anterior to the aortic bifurcation and the origin of the IMA** — so a high ligation at the aorta sweeps them up. Sympathetic supply closes the **bladder neck during ejaculation**; lose it and semen passes backwards into the bladder. **Erection is parasympathetic (S2–4, nervi erigentes)** and therefore preserved.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Sympathetic (L1–3, superior hypogastric plexus) injury → retrograde ejaculation and bladder dysfunction.** **Parasympathetic (S2–4, nervi erigentes, injured at the lateral ligaments/pelvic side wall) → IMPOTENCE (failure of erection) and bladder atony.** Mnemonic: **"Point and Shoot" — Parasympathetic = erection (Point), Sympathetic = ejaculation (Shoot).**
- **Abdominoperineal resection (APR)** removes the **rectum, anal canal and sphincter complex** with a **permanent end colostomy**; reserved for low rectal cancers with sphincter involvement or poor baseline continence.
- **Total mesorectal excision (TME)** is the oncological standard and reduces local recurrence.
- Other pelvic surgery injuries: **ureter (at the pelvic brim and near the uterine artery/cardinal ligament), hypogastric nerves, and the pelvic autonomic plexus**.
- **Addisonian crisis** — hypotension, hypoglycaemia, **hyperkalaemia and hyponatraemia** after **abrupt withdrawal of long-term steroids** (e.g. in rheumatoid arthritis) — is treated with **IV HYDROCORTISONE 100 mg plus fluid resuscitation with normal saline**, started before biochemical confirmation.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Two variables must be matched: **which autonomic system** and **which function**. Sympathetic = ejaculation and bladder, not erection.

**REMEMBER:** *Point with the parasympathetics, shoot with the sympathetics.*

### 52. Crohn Disease — Surgery Does Not Cure It

**PEARL:** **Symptomatic relapse after resection is NOT related to whether disease was present at the resection margins.** Surgery in Crohn disease is **palliative, never curative** — the risk of recurrence rises simply with **time since resection**.

**UNDERSTAND:** Crohn disease is a **panintestinal, transmural immunological disorder**, not a localised lesion. Because the predisposition affects the whole gut, **taking wider margins does not reduce recurrence** — which is why surgery uses **the most conservative resection possible** and why **stricturoplasty** is preferred over repeated resection: each resection risks **short bowel syndrome** without altering the disease.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Terminal ileum is the commonest site** (not proximal ileum); the disease shows **discontinuous "SKIP LESIONS"** (continuous involvement is ulcerative colitis).
- Features: **transmural inflammation, non-caseating granulomas, cobblestone mucosa, deep linear and aphthous ulcers, fat wrapping (creeping fat), fistulae, fissures, strictures and perianal disease**.
- Indications for surgery: **obstruction from stricture, fistula, abscess, perforation, massive haemorrhage, failure of medical therapy, growth retardation in children, and malignancy**.
- A **Crohn's mass/abscess requires DRAINAGE plus antibiotics (metronidazole and ciprofloxacin)** — antibiotics alone are insufficient.
- Postoperative recurrence prophylaxis: **smoking cessation (smoking worsens Crohn but protects against ulcerative colitis)**, azathioprine or anti-TNF therapy.
- Contrast **ulcerative colitis** — mucosal, continuous from the rectum, **crypt abscesses, pseudopolyps**, and **colectomy IS curative**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Three of the four options state the opposite of a true Crohn fact (proximal ileum, continuous lesions, antibiotics alone). The correct answer is the counter-intuitive one about margins.

**REMEMBER:** *The disease is in the whole gut, so wider margins buy nothing.*

### 53. Caecum in Large Bowel Obstruction

**PEARL:** In large bowel obstruction, the **caecum** — **not the splenic flexure** — is the segment most prone to ischaemia and perforation.

**UNDERSTAND:** **Laplace's law** — wall tension is proportional to radius, and the caecum has the largest diameter, so it takes the greatest wall stress and its blood supply fails first.

**MUST KNOW:** This applies with a **competent ileocaecal valve** (closed-loop obstruction) — the more dangerous situation. The **splenic flexure** is the classic **watershed** area for *ischaemic colitis*, which is a different question.

**REMEMBER:** *Biggest radius, highest tension — the caecum blows first.*

### 62. Hernias — Types and Eponyms

**PEARL:** ***RICHTER'S HERNIA — only PART of the bowel circumference is trapped, so it can STRANGULATE WITHOUT COMPLETE OBSTRUCTION (no absolute constipation).*** ***LITTRE'S HERNIA contains a MECKEL'S DIVERTICULUM; AMYAND'S HERNIA contains the APPENDIX; a SLIDING HERNIA has a retroperitoneal viscus (sigmoid colon, caecum or bladder) FORMING PART OF THE WALL OF THE SAC; a PANTALOON hernia has both direct and indirect sacs straddling the inferior epigastric vessels.*** ***"TAXIS" IS THE MANUAL REDUCTION OF AN INCARCERATED HERNIA.***

**UNDERSTAND:** Richter's hernia is dangerous precisely because it is ***quiet***: the lumen stays patent so the patient passes flatus and faeces and does not look obstructed, while the trapped knuckle of antimesenteric bowel wall becomes ischaemic and perforates. It is classic in ***FEMORAL hernias and at laparoscopic port sites*** — narrow-necked defects. The lesson generalises: ***a narrow neck strangulates early, and the absence of obstruction never excludes strangulation.***

**MUST KNOW:**

- ***FEMORAL HERNIA: appears BELOW AND LATERAL TO THE PUBIC TUBERCLE (an inguinal hernia is ABOVE AND MEDIAL), is commoner in WOMEN (especially thin elderly multiparous women), and STRANGULATES EARLY (~50%) because of its rigid narrow neck — so it is repaired promptly, even when asymptomatic.*** Approaches: ***LOCKWOOD (low), LOTHEISSEN (inguinal/mid), McEVEDY (high — preferred in the emergency because bowel can be inspected and resected).***
- **The ***FEMORAL RING boundaries: ANTERIOR/superior — inguinal ligament; POSTERIOR/inferior — pectineal (Cooper's) ligament and pecten pubis; MEDIAL — LACUNAR (Gimbernat's) ligament; LATERAL — the FEMORAL VEIN*** (not the conjoint tendon).
- **Contents of the inguinal canal: ***spermatic cord in men (VAS DEFERENS, TESTICULAR VESSELS, pampiniform plexus, genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve) or the ROUND LIGAMENT in women — NOT the broad ligament — plus the ILIOINGUINAL NERVE in both.***
- ***VENTRAL HERNIAS are anterior abdominal wall hernias — epigastric, umbilical/paraumbilical, incisional, parastomal, Spigelian and (by convention) lumbar. A FEMORAL HERNIA IS NOT VENTRAL***, since it passes beneath the inguinal ligament.
- ***MAYO'S "DOUBLE-BREASTING" REPAIR IS THE CLASSIC OPERATION FOR PARAUMBILICAL HERNIA*** (mesh repair is now usual for larger defects).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Strangulates without obstruction → **Richter's hernia**; bowel forming part of the sac wall → **sliding hernia**; reduction of an incarcerated hernia → **taxis**; true about femoral hernia → **appears below and lateral to the pubic tubercle**; NOT a ventral hernia → **femoral**; wrong femoral ring boundary → **lateral–conjoint tendon**; umbilical hernia after pregnancy → **Mayo's operation**.

**REMEMBER:** *A narrow neck kills quietly — Richter's strangulates while the bowels still open.*

### 63. Inguinal Hernia Repair

**PEARL:** ***"HERNIORRHAPHY" MEANS A TISSUE (suture) REPAIR; LICHTENSTEIN'S TENSION-FREE MESH REPAIR IS A HERNIOPLASTY.*** ***HERNIOTOMY (excision of the sac alone) SUFFICES IN CHILDREN AND FOR CONGENITAL HYDROCELE***; a patient with one inguinal hernia has roughly a ***50% lifetime risk of a contralateral hernia***, and the diagnosis is ***primarily CLINICAL.*** ***THE DESARDA REPAIR uses an UNDETACHED STRIP OF EXTERNAL OBLIQUE APONEUROSIS sutured to the conjoint tendon and inguinal ligament to reinforce the posterior wall.***

**UNDERSTAND:** Modern repair is built on one principle — ***TENSION IS THE ENEMY OF HEALING.*** Older pure-tissue repairs (Bassini, Shouldice, McVay) pull dissimilar structures together under tension, which hurts and recurs; ***mesh spreads the load and lowers recurrence from ~10% to 1–2%***, which is why Lichtenstein and laparoscopic mesh repairs became standard. The Desarda repair is the interesting compromise: ***a tissue repair that is nonetheless tension-free***, using a living aponeurotic strip, and is favoured where mesh is unaffordable or contamination precludes it.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Laparoscopic anatomy: ***the "TRIANGLE OF DOOM" (between the vas deferens medially and gonadal vessels laterally) contains the EXTERNAL ILIAC VESSELS; the "TRIANGLE OF PAIN" (lateral to the gonadal vessels, below the iliopubic tract) contains the LATERAL FEMORAL CUTANEOUS NERVE, the FEMORAL BRANCH OF THE GENITOFEMORAL NERVE and the FEMORAL NERVE — the GENITAL branch of the genitofemoral nerve lies in the triangle of DOOM, not of pain.*** ***NEVER PLACE TACKS OR STAPLES BELOW THE ILIOPUBIC TRACT LATERALLY*** — the classic cause of postoperative lateral thigh pain from ***LATERAL FEMORAL CUTANEOUS NERVE injury.***
- ***LAPAROSCOPIC (TEP/TAPP) REPAIR IS PARTICULARLY GOOD FOR BILATERAL AND RECURRENT (previously open) HERNIAS, AND CAN ALSO TREAT FEMORAL, SLIDING AND OBTURATOR HERNIAS*** — but a "hernia of a hydrocele" is not a hernia at all and is not addressed this way.
- **Complications of repair: ***CHRONIC GROIN PAIN (inguinodynia — the commonest significant problem), recurrence, seroma/haematoma, ischaemic orchitis and testicular atrophy, and mesh infection.***
- ***AN OBSTRUCTED OR STRANGULATED HERNIA NEEDS EMERGENCY SURGERY — TAXIS SHOULD NOT BE FORCED*** (risking "reduction en masse", which returns strangulated bowel to the abdomen).
- **Watchful waiting is acceptable for a ***minimally symptomatic direct inguinal hernia in an elderly man***, but never for a femoral hernia.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Wrong statement → **that Lichtenstein's mesh repair is a herniorrhaphy**; strip of external oblique aponeurosis repair → **Desarda**; NOT in the triangle of pain → **genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve**; tacker below the iliopubic tract → **lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh**; not addressed laparoscopically → **hernia of a hydrocele**.

**REMEMBER:** *Mesh removes tension; keeping tacks above the iliopubic tract removes the pain.*

### 64. Chronic Pain After Inguinal Hernia Repair

**PEARL:** The **commonest complication of inguinal hernia repair is CHRONIC GROIN PAIN (inguinodynia)** — affecting **10–15%** of patients — far more common than infection, haematoma, seroma or recurrence.

**UNDERSTAND:** Modern **tension-free mesh repair** solved the mechanical problem (recurrence fell below 2%) but created a new one: the mesh incites a **fibrotic reaction around the three nerves crossing the field — ilioinguinal, iliohypogastric and the genital branch of the genitofemoral** — and sutures or tacks can entrap them directly. So the complication profile shifted from **failure of repair to nerve-related morbidity**, which is why "chronic pain" is now the correct answer to a question that used to be "recurrence".

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Definition: pain persisting >3 months** after surgery. Types: **neuropathic** (nerve injury/entrapment — burning, shooting, dermatomal, allodynia) and **nociceptive/somatic** (mesh fibrosis, periostitis from a suture in the pubic tubercle).
- **Triangle of pain** (lateral to the cord, below the iliopubic tract) — contains the **lateral femoral cutaneous, femoral branch of genitofemoral and femoral nerves**; **never tack here** in laparoscopic repair. **Triangle of doom** (between the vas medially and gonadal vessels laterally) — contains the **external iliac vessels**.
- Management ladder: reassurance and time, NSAIDs and **neuropathic agents (gabapentin, pregabalin, amitriptyline)**, **nerve blocks** (diagnostic and therapeutic), physiotherapy, and finally **triple neurectomy ± mesh removal**.
- Prevention: **identify and preserve (or deliberately excise) the nerves**, avoid tacks in the triangle of pain, use lightweight large-pore mesh, and prefer **laparoscopic (TEP/TAPP) repair** for bilateral or recurrent hernias — it has less chronic pain and faster recovery.
- **Lichtenstein** open mesh repair remains the standard open operation; other complications are **seroma/haematoma, ischaemic orchitis and testicular atrophy (damage to the pampiniform plexus), urinary retention, recurrence (<2%)**.
- **Watchful waiting** is acceptable for a minimally symptomatic inguinal hernia in a man, but **femoral hernias should always be repaired** (high strangulation risk).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Most common complication" — the intuitive answers (infection, recurrence) are wrong; the mesh era answer is **chronic pain**.

**REMEMBER:** *Mesh fixed the recurrence and left the nerves complaining.*

### 65. Strangulated Femoral Hernia

**PEARL:** A **tense, tender, irreducible swelling BELOW and LATERAL to the pubic tubercle** in an **elderly woman** with bowel obstruction is a **STRANGULATED FEMORAL HERNIA**. An **inguinal** hernia lies **ABOVE and MEDIAL** to the pubic tubercle.

**UNDERSTAND:** The femoral canal is a rigid tunnel bounded by the **inguinal ligament in front, the pectineal (Cooper's) ligament behind, the sharp unyielding LACUNAR (Gimbernat's) ligament medially and the femoral vein laterally**. Because three of those four walls are **bone or unyielding ligament**, any bowel that enters is squeezed at a narrow neck and **strangulates early** — the hernia most likely of all to obstruct and become ischaemic. This is why femoral hernias are always repaired, and repaired promptly.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Femoral hernias are commoner in WOMEN (wider pelvis) and in the elderly and multiparous — but an inguinal hernia is still the commonest hernia in women overall.**
- **Up to 40% present as an emergency with strangulation** — **every unexplained small bowel obstruction demands examination of both groins**, including in the obese, where a small femoral hernia is easily missed (**Richter hernia** — only part of the bowel circumference is trapped, so the patient strangulates **without** obstruction).
- Repair approaches: **LOW (Lockwood) — below the inguinal ligament, best for elective repair; HIGH (McEvedy) — best in emergencies because it allows bowel inspection and resection; INGUINAL (Lotheissen)**. Laparoscopic **TEP/TAPP** is also used.
- **If the neck must be divided to reduce a strangulated femoral hernia, cut the LACUNAR LIGAMENT medially — beware an ABNORMAL OBTURATOR ARTERY ("artery of death") crossing it.**
- **Signs of strangulation: severe pain, tenderness, irreducibility, absent cough impulse, overlying erythema, and systemic features (tachycardia, fever, leucocytosis, raised lactate).** Obstruction can occur without strangulation, and vice versa.
- Femoral canal contents normally: **fat and the lymph node of Cloquet**; the canal exists to allow the femoral vein to expand.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The whole question is the **relationship to the pubic tubercle**. **Below and lateral = femoral; above and medial = inguinal.**

**REMEMBER:** *Below and lateral, rigid neck, strangles early — operate now.*

### 66. Named Hernia Repairs

**PEARL:**

- **Bassini** — sutures the **conjoint tendon to the inguinal ligament**; no double-breasting.
- **Shouldice** — **double-breasting of the transversalis fascia** creating a **4-layered** posterior wall (the best pure tissue repair).
- **Lichtenstein** — **tension-free synthetic mesh** over the defect; the current gold standard.
- **Desarda** — an **undetached strip of external oblique aponeurosis** reinforces the posterior wall; **no mesh**.
- **McVay (Cooper's ligament)** — conjoint tendon to **Cooper's ligament**; repairs **both inguinal and femoral** hernias.
- **Mayo** — **"double-breasting" overlap of fascia** for a small **umbilical/paraumbilical** hernia.
- **McEvedy** — **high (preperitoneal) approach for femoral hernia** through a transverse incision over the lower rectus; allows bowel resection if strangulated.

**MUST KNOW:** **Shouldice and Mayo both use "double-breasting"** — Shouldice for the inguinal canal, Mayo for the umbilicus (the classic confusion). **McVay is the tissue repair of choice for femoral hernia**; **McEvedy is the approach of choice when strangulation is suspected**.

**REMEMBER:** *Shouldice = 4 layers of transversalis; Mayo = umbilical overlap; McVay = Cooper's; McEvedy = high femoral.*

### 68. Richter's Hernia & Strangulation Physiology

**PEARL:** In strangulation, **venous outflow obstruction precedes arterial inflow obstruction**. **Richter's hernia can strangulate without complete bowel obstruction** (only part of the circumference is trapped), and **closed-loop obstruction strangulates rapidly**.

**UNDERSTAND:** Thin-walled veins collapse under a pressure that arteries resist → blood keeps arriving but cannot leave → congestion, oedema, rising pressure, and finally arterial cut-off and infarction.

**MUST KNOW:** So a patient can have **strangulation without vomiting or distension** — pain and tenderness out of proportion is the clue. Femoral hernias have the **highest strangulation rate**; Richter's is classically femoral or at a port site.

**REMEMBER:** *Veins go first; Richter's kills bowel without obstructing it.*

### 69. Gallstones and Their Complications

**PEARL:** ***ASYMPTOMATIC GALLSTONES ARE NOT ROUTINELY OPERATED ON — BUT PROPHYLACTIC CHOLECYSTECTOMY IS INDICATED FOR STONES >3 cm, gallbladder POLYPS >1 cm, a PORCELAIN (calcified) gallbladder, CHRONIC HAEMOLYTIC DISEASE (sickle cell, spherocytosis), an anomalous pancreaticobiliary junction, transplant and bariatric patients, and in populations with a high incidence of gallbladder cancer (northern India, Chile, Native Americans).*** ***PIGMENT STONES PREDOMINATE IN ASIA (cholesterol stones in the West).*** ***MIRIZZI SYNDROME = a stone impacted in the cystic duct/Hartmann's pouch compressing the common hepatic duct and causing jaundice.***

**UNDERSTAND:** The size threshold exists because ***a large stone chronically irritates the mucosa and is strongly associated with gallbladder CARCINOMA*** — a cancer that is usually incurable when it presents. The same logic runs through the whole list: ***every indication is a marker of either cancer risk (large stone, polyp, porcelain gallbladder, anomalous junction) or of predictably severe complications (haemolysis, immunosuppression).*** Where neither applies, the risk of surgery exceeds the risk of the stones.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Complications of gallstones: ***biliary colic, acute and chronic cholecystitis, MUCOCELE (obstructed cystic duct with sterile mucus) and empyema, CHOLEDOCHOLITHIASIS with obstructive jaundice and CHOLANGITIS (CHARCOT'S TRIAD; REYNOLDS' PENTAD adds shock and confusion), GALLSTONE PANCREATITIS, gallstone ileus, Mirizzi syndrome and carcinoma.***
- ***SUSPECTED COMMON BILE DUCT STONES (raised bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase with a dilated duct) ARE INVESTIGATED WITH MRCP (or endoscopic ultrasound) FIRST; ERCP IS RESERVED FOR THERAPY*** — proceeding straight to ERCP exposes the patient to pancreatitis for a purely diagnostic purpose.
- ***ACUTE CHOLANGITIS NEEDS RESUSCITATION, ANTIBIOTICS AND URGENT BILIARY DRAINAGE (ERCP)*** — it is the commonest cause of a PYOGENIC LIVER ABSCESS (contrast the amoebic abscess: a young man, right lobe, "ANCHOVY SAUCE" pus, positive serology, treated with metronidazole).
- **After CBD exploration a ***T-TUBE drains the duct (one limb in the CBD — it is NOT a transhepatic drain); a T-TUBE CHOLANGIOGRAM is done once the patient is well and the bile clear, and RESIDUAL STONES CAN BE EXTRACTED THROUGH THE MATURE TRACT (BURHENNE'S TECHNIQUE) AFTER 4–6 WEEKS.***
- ***IN GALLSTONE PANCREATITIS, CHOLECYSTECTOMY SHOULD BE PERFORMED DURING THE SAME ADMISSION, AS SOON AS THE PATIENT IS FIT*** — delay carries a high risk of recurrent pancreatitis.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Asymptomatic 4 cm gallstone → **laparoscopic cholecystectomy**; incorrect about gallstones → **that cholesterol stones are commonest in Asia**; gallstones with raised bilirubin and ALP → **MRCP**; incorrect about the T-tube → **that it is used for transhepatic drainage**; commonest cause of pyogenic liver abscess → **ascending cholangitis**; timing of cholecystectomy in biliary pancreatitis → **before discharge**.

**REMEMBER:** *Operate on the asymptomatic gallbladder only when the stone, the wall or the patient carries a cancer risk.*

### 70. Biliary Malignancy and Choledochal Cysts

**PEARL:** ***RISK FACTORS FOR GALLBLADDER CANCER: gallstones (especially >3 cm), POLYPS >1 cm, PORCELAIN gallbladder, chronic typhoid carriage, CHOLEDOCHAL CYSTS and an anomalous pancreaticobiliary junction — ADENOMYOMATOSIS IS NOT A RECOGNISED RISK FACTOR.*** ***INCIDENTAL T1a GALLBLADDER CANCER (confined to the LAMINA PROPRIA) AFTER SIMPLE CHOLECYSTECTOMY WITH CLEAR MARGINS NEEDS NO FURTHER TREATMENT; T1b AND ABOVE REQUIRE EXTENDED (RADICAL) CHOLECYSTECTOMY.*** ***A TODANI TYPE III CYST (CHOLEDOCHOCELE) IS TREATED BY ENDOSCOPIC SPHINCTEROTOMY***, unlike types I and IV, which need excision with hepaticojejunostomy. ***BISMUTH–CORLETTE TYPE III HILAR CHOLANGIOCARCINOMA involves the confluence with UNILATERAL extension to second-order radicles.***

**UNDERSTAND:** The T1a/T1b distinction turns on the ***gallbladder's unique anatomy — it has NO SUBMUCOSA and only a thin muscular layer, and its bed is in direct contact with the liver.*** Once the tumour reaches the MUSCLE layer (T1b), lymphatic and direct hepatic spread become likely, so ***re-resection of liver segments IVb/V with portal lymphadenectomy is required.*** Confined to lamina propria, it cannot yet have spread — hence cholecystectomy alone is curative.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Gallbladder cancer is ***notably common in NORTHERN INDIA***, presents late with jaundice and weight loss, and has a poor prognosis; most are found incidentally on histology after cholecystectomy — ***HENCE EVERY GALLBLADDER SPECIMEN MUST BE SENT FOR HISTOPATHOLOGY.***
- **Bismuth–Corlette classification of hilar (Klatskin) tumours: ***I — below the confluence; II — reaching the confluence; IIIa — confluence plus RIGHT hepatic duct; IIIb — plus LEFT hepatic duct; IV — bilateral second-order radicles or multifocal (generally unresectable).*** Resection usually requires ***hepatectomy with caudate lobe resection and biliary reconstruction.***
- **Choledochal cyst management by Todani type: ***I and IV — complete excision with ROUX-EN-Y HEPATICOJEJUNOSTOMY (never simple drainage, because of the cancer risk); II — simple excision; III — endoscopic sphincterotomy; V (CAROLI) — segmental resection or transplantation.***
- ***CHOLANGIOCARCINOMA PRESENTS WITH PAINLESS OBSTRUCTIVE JAUNDICE; CA 19-9 SUPPORTS THE DIAGNOSIS; and BILIARY DRAINAGE BEFORE RESECTION IS SELECTIVE, since it introduces infection.***
- **Adenomyomatosis is a benign hyperplastic change (Rokitansky–Aschoff sinuses, "comet-tail" artefact) and needs treatment only if symptomatic.**

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Incorrect gallbladder cancer risk factor → **adenomyomatosis**; management of T1a gallbladder cancer → **no further treatment**; Todani type III → **endoscopic sphincterotomy**; confluence with unilateral second-order extension → **Bismuth–Corlette III**.

**REMEMBER:** *No submucosa in the gallbladder — so once the tumour reaches muscle, it needs the liver bed out too.*

### 71. Liver Tumours and Portal Hypertension

**PEARL:** ***A CIRRHOTIC LIVER WITH A RAPIDLY ARTERIALLY ENHANCING MASS AND AN AFP OF 490 ng/mL IS HEPATOCELLULAR CARCINOMA.*** ***STAGING SYSTEMS FOR LIVER CANCER INCLUDE BARCELONA CLINIC (BCLC), OKUDA AND CLIP — ASTLER–COLLER IS A COLORECTAL SYSTEM.*** ***KASABACH–MERRITT SYNDROME = THROMBOCYTOPENIA + CONSUMPTIVE COAGULOPATHY + HAEMOLYSIS within a large vascular tumour — NOT hepatic adenoma.*** ***TIPSS IS CONTRAINDICATED (or unhelpful) IN PORTAL VEIN THROMBOSIS***, but is used for refractory ascites, refractory variceal bleeding and as a bridge to transplantation.

**UNDERSTAND:** TIPSS works by creating a channel ***between a hepatic vein and an intrahepatic PORTAL vein branch*** — so it presupposes a patent portal vein to decompress. If the portal vein itself is thrombosed, there is nothing to shunt from, and the physiology is pre-hepatic rather than sinusoidal. Its major complication follows equally directly: ***diverting portal blood past the hepatocytes causes or worsens HEPATIC ENCEPHALOPATHY*** in up to a third of patients.

**MUST KNOW:**

- ***THE BCLC SYSTEM IS PREFERRED BECAUSE IT LINKS STAGE TO TREATMENT: very early/early — RESECTION, ABLATION or TRANSPLANTATION (MILAN CRITERIA: one lesion ≤5 cm or up to three ≤3 cm with no vascular invasion or extrahepatic spread); intermediate — TACE; advanced — systemic therapy (sorafenib/lenvatinib, atezolizumab–bevacizumab); terminal — best supportive care.***
- ***SURVEILLANCE OF CIRRHOSIS WITH 6-MONTHLY ULTRASOUND (± AFP) IS WHAT MAKES CURATIVE TREATMENT POSSIBLE***; AFP alone is neither sensitive nor specific.
- **Liver resection depends on ***FUTURE LIVER REMNANT AND FUNCTION (Child–Pugh, portal pressure, ICG clearance); PORTAL VEIN EMBOLISATION can hypertrophy the remnant before major hepatectomy.***
- **Kasabach–Merritt is classically associated with ***KAPOSIFORM HAEMANGIOENDOTHELIOMA/tufted angioma rather than ordinary infantile haemangioma***, and is treated with steroids, vincristine, sirolimus or embolisation.
- **Other portal hypertension interventions: ***endoscopic banding, beta-blockers, splenectomy/devascularisation, and shunt surgery in selected cases; hepatic ENCEPHALOPATHY and stent stenosis are the main TIPSS complications.***

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Cirrhosis with an enhancing mass and raised AFP → **hepatoma**; NOT a liver cancer staging system → **Astler–Coller**; NOT part of Kasabach–Merritt → **hepatic adenoma**; NOT an indication for TIPSS → **portal vein thrombosis**.

**REMEMBER:** *TIPSS needs a patent portal vein to decompress — and buys ascites control at the price of encephalopathy.*

### 72. Acute Pancreatitis

**PEARL:** ***GALLSTONES ARE THE COMMONEST CAUSE OF ACUTE PANCREATITIS WORLDWIDE*** (alcohol second). ***METABOLIC CAUSES ARE HYPERCALCAEMIA, HYPERPARATHYROIDISM AND HYPERTRIGLYCERIDAEMIA — HYPERKALAEMIA IS NOT A CAUSE.*** ***SIRS CRITERIA (two or more): TEMPERATURE >38 or <36 °C, HEART RATE >90, RESPIRATORY RATE >20 or PaCO2 <32 mmHg, and WHITE CELL COUNT >12,000 or <4,000 — HYPOTENSION IS NOT A SIRS CRITERION*** (it defines shock). ***SURGERY/INTERVENTION IS INDICATED FOR INFECTED NECROSIS — STERILE NECROSIS SHOULD NOT BE DRAINED OR DISTURBED.***

**UNDERSTAND:** The rule about sterile necrosis is one of the most important in modern surgery: ***intervening on sterile necrosis converts a survivable inflammatory illness into an infected one and increases mortality.*** Hence the modern approach — ***"DELAY, DRAIN, DEBRIDE": wait at least 4 weeks for the collection to wall off, then use a STEP-UP approach (percutaneous or endoscopic transgastric drainage first, and minimally invasive necrosectomy only if that fails)*** rather than early open necrosectomy.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Diagnosis needs ***2 of 3: characteristic PAIN (epigastric, radiating to the back, relieved by sitting forward), AMYLASE/LIPASE >3× normal (LIPASE is more specific and stays raised longer), and characteristic IMAGING.*** ***THE ENZYME LEVEL DOES NOT CORRELATE WITH SEVERITY.***
- **Severity scoring: ***RANSON, APACHE II, GLASGOW, BISAP and the modified CT SEVERITY INDEX; the REVISED ATLANTA classification defines MILD (no organ failure or complications), MODERATELY SEVERE (transient organ failure <48 h or local complications) and SEVERE (PERSISTENT ORGAN FAILURE >48 h).*** ***PERSISTENT ORGAN FAILURE — not the enzyme level, and not the CT appearance — DRIVES MORTALITY.***
- **Early management: ***aggressive but goal-directed FLUID RESUSCITATION (Ringer's lactate), analgesia, EARLY ENTERAL FEEDING (nasogastric or oral as tolerated — NOT prolonged fasting or routine TPN), NO PROPHYLACTIC ANTIBIOTICS, and URGENT ERCP ONLY IF THERE IS COEXISTING CHOLANGITIS OR PERSISTENT BILIARY OBSTRUCTION.***
- **Vascular complications: ***PSEUDOANEURYSM most often of the SPLENIC ARTERY (treated by ANGIOEMBOLISATION), and SPLENIC VEIN THROMBOSIS causing LEFT-SIDED (sinistral) PORTAL HYPERTENSION with ISOLATED GASTRIC VARICES — cured by splenectomy.***
- **Other local complications: ***acute peripancreatic fluid collection, PSEUDOCYST (>4 weeks, drained only if symptomatic), acute necrotic collection and WALLED-OFF NECROSIS.***

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Commonest cause of acute pancreatitis → **gallstones**; NOT a metabolic cause → **hyperkalaemia**; NOT a SIRS criterion → **BP <90/60**; indication for intervention → **infected necrotic collection**; commonest pseudoaneurysm → **splenic artery**.

**REMEMBER:** *Leave sterile necrosis alone; delay, then step up only for infection.*

### 73. Chronic Pancreatitis, Annular Pancreas and Pancreatic Cancer

**PEARL:** ***A DILATED PANCREATIC DUCT (>7 mm) WITH INTRADUCTAL STONES AND INTRACTABLE PAIN IS TREATED BY A LATERAL PANCREATICOJEJUNOSTOMY — PUESTOW'S (Partington–Rochelle) PROCEDURE.*** ***AN INFLAMMATORY MASS IN THE HEAD IS TREATED BY BEGER'S (duodenum-preserving head resection) OR FREY'S PROCEDURE; a small-duct disease confined to the tail by DISTAL PANCREATECTOMY.*** ***ANNULAR PANCREAS CAUSING NEONATAL DUODENAL OBSTRUCTION IS BYPASSED BY DUODENODUODENOSTOMY — the annulus is NEVER divided.*** ***PEUTZ–JEGHERS SYNDROME (STK11) CARRIES THE HIGHEST RELATIVE RISK OF PANCREATIC ADENOCARCINOMA of the familial syndromes.***

**UNDERSTAND:** Chronic pancreatitis surgery is chosen by ***"BIG DUCT versus SMALL DUCT" disease.*** A dilated, obstructed duct implies raised intraductal pressure as the pain mechanism, so ***DRAINAGE (Puestow) relieves it without sacrificing pancreatic tissue*** — preserving endocrine and exocrine function. Small-duct disease has no duct to drain, so ***RESECTION (or denervation) is required.*** Similarly, the annular pancreas is never divided because ***the ring contains pancreatic ducts — dividing it causes a pancreatic fistula*** — so the obstruction is bypassed instead.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Chronic pancreatitis: ***alcohol is the commonest cause in adults; TROPICAL (idiopathic juvenile) pancreatitis is important in India, with large intraductal calculi, diabetes and a risk of pancreatic cancer.*** Presents with pain, steatorrhoea and diabetes; imaging shows ***calcification and a "CHAIN OF LAKES" duct.*** Treat with ***abstinence, analgesia, PANCREATIC ENZYME REPLACEMENT and fat-soluble vitamins, then endoscopic or surgical drainage.***
- **Pancreatic adenocarcinoma: ***painless obstructive jaundice with a palpable gallbladder (Courvoisier), weight loss and new-onset diabetes; CA 19-9; a PANCREATIC-PROTOCOL CT determines resectability by ARTERIAL AND VENOUS INVOLVEMENT; WHIPPLE'S PANCREATODUODENECTOMY for resectable head tumours, with adjuvant chemotherapy.*** ***ONLY 15–20% ARE RESECTABLE AT DIAGNOSIS.***
- **Hereditary risks: ***PEUTZ–JEGHERS (STK11), hereditary pancreatitis (PRSS1), BRCA2, CDKN2A/FAMMM, Lynch syndrome and cystic fibrosis.***
- ***DUODENAL ATRESIA, ANNULAR PANCREAS AND DOWN SYNDROME TRAVEL TOGETHER*** — the "double bubble" baby needs an echocardiogram and a karyotype as well as an operation.
- **Palliation of unresectable pancreatic cancer: ***biliary and duodenal stenting, coeliac plexus block for pain, pancreatic enzymes and nutritional support.***

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Dilated duct with stones in chronic pancreatitis → **Puestow's procedure**; annular pancreas in a Down syndrome neonate → **duodenoduodenostomy**; highest risk of pancreatic adenocarcinoma → **STK11**.

**REMEMBER:** *Big duct, drain it; small duct, resect it; annular pancreas, bypass it.*

### 74. Bile Leak After Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy

**PEARL:** A collection in the gallbladder fossa with fever on **day 2–3 after lap chole** is a **bile leak** — the first step is **ultrasound-guided percutaneous drainage**, not ERCP, not re-exploration.

**UNDERSTAND:** Management of any post-operative bile collection follows the universal surgical sequence **drain the collection first, define the anatomy second, repair last**. An undrained bile collection is an infected space causing sepsis; controlling the sepsis is what buys time. Only once drained do you ask *where* the leak is (MRCP/ERCP) and whether it needs stenting (cystic duct stump, duct of Luschka) or reconstruction (CBD transection).

**MUST KNOW:**

- Commonest source of a post-cholecystectomy leak: **cystic duct stump** (clip slippage), then **subvesical duct of Luschka**.
- **ERCP + stent** works for a low-pressure leak from the stump/Luschka because it abolishes the sphincter pressure gradient — but only *after* drainage.
- **Hepaticojejunostomy** (Roux-en-Y) is for a **major duct transection (Strasberg E / Bismuth)** and is deliberately **delayed** until sepsis is controlled and the patient stabilised — early repair in an inflamed field strictures.
- **MRCP** is non-invasive road-mapping; it never treats, so it can't be the *next step* in a septic patient.
- The **critical view of safety** (2 structures entering the gallbladder, cleared hepatocystic triangle) is the standard preventive manoeuvre; **Calot's triangle** = cystic duct, common hepatic duct, inferior liver surface.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Stem gives fever + RUQ pain + fluid in GB fossa a few days after lap chole. The distractors are all real treatments in the *wrong order*. Rule: **drain → define → repair.**

**REMEMBER:** *Sepsis first, anatomy second, surgery last.*

### 75. Pancreatic Cystic Neoplasms — Cyst Fluid Analysis

**PEARL:** **Mucin positive + high CEA + HIGH AMYLASE = IPMN.** Amylase is high only in IPMN because it **communicates with the pancreatic duct**.

**UNDERSTAND:** Two markers answer two different questions. **CEA/mucin** answer *is it mucinous, i.e. premalignant?* **Amylase** answers *does it connect to the ductal system?* Serous cystadenoma is neither mucinous nor connected (low, low); mucinous cystic neoplasm is mucinous but has no ductal connection (high CEA, low amylase); IPMN is both (high CEA, high amylase). A **pseudocyst** is not neoplastic — very high amylase but **low CEA**.

**MUST KNOW:**

| | Mucin | CEA | Amylase | Cytology |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Serous cystadenoma | – | Low | Low | Glycogen-rich, **PAS +**, central scar/microcystic honeycomb |
| Mucinous cystic neoplasm | + | **High** | Low | Columnar mucinous, **ovarian-type stroma**, women, body/tail |
| IPMN | + | **High** | **High** | Tall columnar mucinous, **duct communication**, head, "fish-mouth" ampulla |
| Pseudocyst | – | **Low** | **Very high** | Inflammatory debris, no epithelium |

- **CEA >192 ng/mL** is the standard cut-off for a mucinous lesion.
- **MCN and main-duct IPMN are premalignant → resect.** Serous cystadenoma is benign → observe unless symptomatic.
- **Worrisome/high-risk features** for IPMN: main duct >10 mm, enhancing mural nodule, obstructive jaundice, size >3 cm.
- **EUS-FNA** is the way the fluid is obtained.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** A three-marker stem where the examiner expects you to pivot on **amylase** — the only marker separating IPMN from MCN. If they say "ovarian-type stroma" or "middle-aged woman, tail", it's MCN.

**REMEMBER:** *Amylase means the cyst talks to the duct — that's IPMN.*

### 76. Classifications of Bile Duct Injury

**PEARL:** The **Hannover** classification is the one that includes **concomitant vascular injury** as well as the anatomical location of the bile duct injury.

**UNDERSTAND:** Each classification was built to answer a different question, and the exam tests which question. Strasberg and Bismuth describe *where* the duct is damaged; McMahon describes *how big* the hole is; Hannover was created because outcome after repair depends heavily on whether the **right hepatic artery** was also injured — an ischaemic duct strictures no matter how technically good the anastomosis. So Hannover is the only one that codes vascular involvement.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Hannover A–E:** A = peripheral leak (cystic stump, duct of Luschka); B = stricture without leak; C = lateral CHD/CBD injury; D = complete transection of the CHD; E = late main-duct stricture without leak. **Vascular injury is coded within types C and D.**
- **Strasberg A–E** — the most widely used, purely anatomical: A = cystic duct/Luschka leak, B = occluded aberrant right duct, C = transected aberrant right duct, D = lateral CBD injury, **E1–E5 = Bismuth types 1–5** (major circumferential injury).
- **Bismuth–Corlette** — originally for stricture level, now best known for **hilar cholangiocarcinoma (Klatskin) tumour** extension.
- **McMahon** — by **width** of the injury (minor <25% of duct circumference vs major).
- Injury with an associated **right hepatic artery injury** carries the worst prognosis → higher stricture and re-operation rate; repair is **Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy**, delayed, by a specialist HPB unit.
- Prevention: achieve the **critical view of safety**; convert if the anatomy is unclear.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Straight matching question — *"Which classification of bile duct injury includes vascular injury?"* The instinctive answer is Strasberg (the most famous one), which is exactly the trap. **Vascular → Hannover.**

### 77. Risk Factors for Gallbladder Carcinoma

**PEARL:** Risk factors include **gallstones >3 cm (the most important), porcelain gallbladder, ADENOMATOUS polyps >10 mm, anomalous pancreatobiliary junction, choledochal cyst, primary sclerosing cholangitis, chronic Salmonella typhi carriage and obesity**. **A SINGLE polyp smaller than 1 cm is NOT a risk factor.**

**UNDERSTAND:** Every risk factor works through **prolonged mucosal irritation and chronic inflammation → dysplasia → carcinoma**. A big stone abrades the mucosa for decades; a porcelain gallbladder is the end-stage of that chronic inflammation; an anomalous pancreatobiliary junction allows **pancreatic enzymes to reflux into the biliary tree**, producing continuous chemical injury. A small solitary polyp causes no irritation and is usually a **cholesterol polyp — not even a true neoplasm**, which is why it carries no risk.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Polyp management: <5 mm — no follow-up needed; 6–9 mm — ultrasound surveillance; ≥10 mm, rapid growth, sessile, solitary, age >50, or coexisting PSC/gallstones — CHOLECYSTECTOMY.**
- **Porcelain gallbladder** (calcified wall): the risk is now recognised as lower than the classic 25% figure, but **selective/incomplete mucosal calcification still warrants cholecystectomy**.
- Presentation is **late and non-specific** (RUQ pain, weight loss, anorexia); **jaundice is a late and ominous sign**. Most are found **incidentally at cholecystectomy for stones** — the only ones with a good prognosis. **CA 19-9** is used for monitoring.
- **Adenocarcinoma** in >90%; spreads early to **segments IVb and V of the liver** and to the **cystic and pericholedochal nodes**.
- **Treatment: T1a (lamina propria only) → simple cholecystectomy is adequate. T1b and beyond → EXTENDED (radical) cholecystectomy — resection of liver segments IVb/V plus portal lymphadenectomy**, with excision of the port sites if found after laparoscopic surgery.
- **India — especially the Gangetic belt — has among the highest incidence rates in the world**; female preponderance; **avoid percutaneous biopsy of a resectable mass** (risk of tract seeding).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Which is NOT a risk factor?" — the answer is the **small solitary polyp**. The 3 cm stone and 1 cm polyp thresholds are the two numbers examiners use.

**REMEMBER:** *Three centimetres of stone and one centimetre of polyp — those are the lines.*

### 78. Imaging the Obstructed Biliary Tree

**PEARL:** In obstructive jaundice with a **distended gallbladder and dilated ducts on ultrasound**, the next test to **LOCALISE the cause is MRCP** — non-invasive, complete ductal mapping. **ERCP is reserved for THERAPY** (or tissue sampling), not for first-line diagnosis, and **ERCP is LEAST useful in acute pancreatitis WITHOUT choledocholithiasis**.

**UNDERSTAND:** The two tests answer different questions. **MRCP is purely diagnostic and carries no procedural risk**, so it should come first when you only need to know *where and why* the duct is blocked. **ERCP is invasive and carries a 3–5% risk of post-ERCP pancreatitis**, so its risk is only justified when an intervention is intended — stone extraction, stenting, sphincterotomy or brushings. Performing an ERCP in acute pancreatitis without stones therefore offers **all the hazard and none of the benefit** — it may even worsen the pancreatitis.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **"MRCP before ERCP"** is the general rule; go **straight to ERCP** when there is **cholangitis, a known stone with obstruction, or a planned stent**.
- **ERCP indications: obstructive jaundice needing drainage, choledocholithiasis, cholangitis, bile leak, periampullary/pancreatic head tumours (stenting and brushings), recurrent pancreatitis (to diagnose PANCREAS DIVISUM), sphincter of Oddi manometry, and pancreatic duct strictures.**
- **COURVOISIER's LAW: in a jaundiced patient, a PALPABLE, NON-TENDER, DISTENDED GALLBLADDER is unlikely to be due to stones** — because chronic stone disease produces a fibrotic, contracted gallbladder. It therefore suggests a **malignant distal obstruction (pancreatic head or periampullary carcinoma)**. Exceptions: double impaction, a stone in the cystic duct with obstruction below, and Mirizzi syndrome.
- **Endoscopic ultrasound** is the most sensitive test for **small distal CBD stones and small pancreatic/ampullary tumours**, and permits FNA. **PTC** is used when ERCP fails or the obstruction is **hilar/proximal**.
- **Post-cholecystectomy fluid collection with fever and leucocytosis → percutaneous ULTRASOUND-GUIDED PIGTAIL DRAINAGE first** (sepsis control), then define the anatomy (MRCP/ERCP), then plan repair — the **SNAP** sequence.
- **Cholangiocarcinoma risk factors: PRIMARY SCLEROSING CHOLANGITIS (the strongest), CHOLEDOCHAL CYSTS and CAROLI DISEASE, liver flukes (*Opisthorchis viverrini*, *Clonorchis sinensis*), hepatolithiasis, thorotrast, and chronic typhoid carriage. HEPATITIS B and C are risk factors for HEPATOCELLULAR carcinoma, NOT for cholangiocarcinoma** (though a weak association with intrahepatic CCA is now recognised — the exam treats HBV as the odd one out).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Two recurring stems — **"most useful to LOCALISE" → MRCP**, and **"ERCP least useful" → acute pancreatitis without stones**.

**REMEMBER:** *Map it with MRCP; only reach for ERCP when you intend to do something.*

### 79. Hydatid Cyst: WHO-IWGE Staging Drives Management

**PEARL:** **CE1 (simple unilocular, "double-line" sign) and CE3a → ALBENDAZOLE ± PAIR; CE2 (multiseptated "rosette/honeycomb") and CE3b (daughter cysts in a solid matrix) → SURGERY (pericystectomy); CE4 (heterogeneous "ball of wool") and CE5 (thick CALCIFIED wall) are INACTIVE → OBSERVE, no intervention.**

**UNDERSTAND:** The classification is an **activity scale, not merely a description**. A **calcified (CE5) cyst is dead** — the parasite has been walled off and destroyed by the host, so there is nothing to kill and nothing to spill; intervening only risks anaphylaxis, bleeding and secondary infection. **Active cysts (CE1, CE2) contain viable protoscolices**, so they must be sterilised or removed. Reading the CT is therefore reading the **biological activity of the parasite**.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **PAIR (Puncture, Aspiration, Injection of a scolicidal, Re-aspiration) is done UNDER ALBENDAZOLE COVER (started days before and continued for weeks after)** to prevent secondary echinococcosis if spillage occurs. **Contraindicated in CE2/CE3b (the septa prevent complete drainage), inaccessible cysts, and cysts communicating with the biliary tree** (a scolicidal agent would cause sclerosing cholangitis).
- **Scolicidal agents: hypertonic (20%) saline, 0.5% silver nitrate, cetrimide. FORMALIN is NO LONGER used** (sclerosing cholangitis). Pack the field with scolicidal-soaked swabs before opening any cyst.
- **Surgical options: total or partial PERICYSTECTOMY, hepatic segmentectomy, or "unroofing" with omentoplasty.** The greatest intraoperative risk is **spillage causing ANAPHYLAXIS and disseminated secondary cysts** — have adrenaline and steroids ready.
- **Complications: rupture into the biliary tree (jaundice, cholangitis), into the peritoneum (anaphylaxis), or into the pleura/bronchus; secondary bacterial infection.**
- **Serology (ELISA/IHA) supports the diagnosis; eosinophilia is present in only ~25%.** **Casoni's test is obsolete.**
- ***E. granulosus* — dog definitive host, sheep intermediate, human accidental dead-end; liver (70%) > lung.** ***E. multilocularis* — fox, alveolar echinococcosis, behaves like a malignancy, treated with radical resection and lifelong albendazole.**

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The CT appearance gives the CE stage, and the stage gives the management. **Calcified = CE5 = observe.**

**REMEMBER:** *Calcified means dead — leave it alone; active and unilocular means PAIR; honeycomb means surgery.*

### 80. Bile Duct Stones — Naming by Timing

**PEARL:** Stones in the CBD **within 2 years of cholecystectomy are RETAINED**; **after 2 years they are RECURRENT, and are almost always PRIMARY (brown pigment) stones** formed de novo in the duct.

**UNDERSTAND:** The two-year cut-off is a **surrogate for origin**. A cholesterol stone that escaped the gallbladder before its removal is unlikely to stay silent beyond two years. A stone appearing later must have **formed in the duct itself**, which requires **bile stasis and bacterial deconjugation of bilirubin** — hence its brown pigment composition, quite different from the cholesterol stones of the gallbladder.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Primary (brown pigment) stones**: soft, earthy, form in the duct, associated with **stasis, strictures, and biliary infection (E. coli β-glucuronidase deconjugates bilirubin)**; common in Asia with **Clonorchis/Ascaris** infestation.
- **Secondary stones**: **cholesterol stones from the gallbladder** that migrated down.
- **Choosing the investigation**: **ultrasound first**; **a CBD > 8 mm with abnormal liver enzymes suggests choledocholithiasis**. When suspicion is intermediate or biochemistry is equivocal, use **MRCP (or endoscopic ultrasound) — non-invasive with high sensitivity**. **ERCP is reserved for therapy**, because of pancreatitis, bleeding and perforation risks.
- **Charcot's triad (pain, fever, jaundice) = cholangitis; Reynolds' pentad adds hypotension and confusion** → urgent biliary decompression.
- **Choledochal cyst**: from an **anomalous pancreaticobiliary junction**, classified by **TODANI**; presents with the triad of **pain, jaundice and a mass**. **Complete EXCISION with Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy is required — never simple drainage** — because of the risk of **cholangiocarcinoma**. **Type III (choledochocele) is the exception**, treated by **endoscopic sphincterotomy**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The interval since cholecystectomy is the entire question. The MRCP question tests when imaging is diagnostic versus therapeutic.

**REMEMBER:** *Within two years it was left behind; after two years it was made there.*

### 81. Medical Dissolution of Gallstones

**PEARL:** Ursodeoxycholic acid works only for **RADIOLUCENT CHOLESTEROL stones < 10 mm in a functioning gallbladder with a patent cystic duct**, in patients with **uncomplicated biliary pain**. **Radio-opaque stones will not dissolve.**

**UNDERSTAND:** UDCA works by **reducing biliary cholesterol saturation**, allowing cholesterol to leach out of the stone surface. It can therefore only act on stones that are **predominantly cholesterol** — and **radio-opacity indicates calcium content**, which UDCA cannot touch. Size matters because dissolution proceeds from the **surface**, so large stones would take years.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Dose **10–15 mg/kg/day**, continued for **6–24 months**; **recurrence is ~50% at 5 years** after stopping, which is the main reason it is rarely used.
- **Pigment stones do not respond at all.**
- Long-term UDCA is indicated after cholecystectomy for **recurrent choledocholithiasis**.
- UDCA also has established roles in **primary biliary cholangitis** and in **intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy**.
- Only about **10–15% of gallstones are radio-opaque** — hence "gallstones are usually radiolucent, renal stones usually radio-opaque".
- **Paracolic lymph nodes lie along the MARGINAL ARTERY (of Drummond)** — the anastomotic vessel running along the mesenteric border of the colon; trace it to find them. Colonic nodal groups are **epicolic (on the bowel wall) → paracolic (along the marginal artery) → intermediate (along the named colic arteries) → principal/preaortic (at the origin of the SMA/IMA)**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Considered in all EXCEPT" → **radio-opaque stones**. The other three are the selection criteria.

**REMEMBER:** *Only radiolucent cholesterol dissolves.*

### 82. Balloon Tamponade for Variceal Bleeding

**PEARL:** **Sengstaken–Blakemore tube — 3 lumens (gastric aspiration, gastric balloon, oesophageal balloon).** **Minnesota tube — 4 lumens, adding an OESOPHAGEAL aspiration port.**

**UNDERSTAND:** The extra port on the Minnesota tube exists to solve a specific hazard: with the oesophageal balloon inflated, **saliva and blood pool above it** and are **aspirated into the lungs**. Being able to suck out that column above the balloon is the whole advantage — a design change driven by the commonest complication of the older tube.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Technique: **inflate the GASTRIC balloon first and apply traction at the gastro-oesophageal junction**; inflate the oesophageal balloon **only if bleeding continues**, and deflate it periodically to avoid **pressure necrosis**. Use for a maximum of **24 hours** as a bridge to definitive therapy.
- Complications: **aspiration pneumonia, oesophageal rupture, mucosal ulceration and necrosis, airway obstruction if the balloon migrates** — always **intubate first**.
- Definitive management of variceal bleeding: **resuscitation → terlipressin/octreotide + prophylactic antibiotics → URGENT ENDOSCOPY with band ligation (preferred) or sclerotherapy → balloon tamponade or an oesophageal stent if uncontrolled → TIPS as salvage**.
- **Band ligation has fewer complications than sclerotherapy** and is first line for oesophageal varices; **gastric varices** are treated with **cyanoacrylate glue injection**.
- Prophylaxis: **non-selective beta-blockers (propranolol, carvedilol)** and/or **band ligation programmes**.
- **Antibiotic prophylaxis (ceftriaxone) reduces mortality** in variceal bleeding with cirrhosis — often forgotten.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Count the ports on the image: **three = Sengstaken–Blakemore, four = Minnesota**.

**REMEMBER:** *The fourth lumen sucks out what would otherwise be aspirated.*

### 83. Child–Turcotte–Pugh Score

**PEARL:** The Child–Turcotte–Pugh score uses **bilirubin, albumin, prothrombin time/INR, ascites and encephalopathy**. **CREATININE is NOT included** — that belongs to the **MELD** score.

**UNDERSTAND:** CTP was designed to measure **hepatic synthetic and functional reserve**, so every variable reflects a **liver function**: bilirubin (excretion), albumin and INR (synthesis), ascites and encephalopathy (clinical decompensation). **MELD was designed later to predict short-term mortality for transplant allocation**, and deliberately added **creatinine** because renal failure is the dominant prognostic factor in advanced liver disease.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Scoring: each variable **1–3 points**. **Class A = 5–6 (well compensated, ~10% surgical mortality); Class B = 7–9 (~30%); Class C = 10–15 (decompensated, ~75–80% mortality)** for intra-abdominal surgery.
- **Mnemonic — "Pour Another Beer At Eleven": Prothrombin time, Albumin, Bilirubin, Ascites, Encephalopathy.**
- Originally devised to predict outcome after **portocaval shunt surgery**; now used for **any intra-abdominal operation in a cirrhotic**, and to guide hepatocellular carcinoma treatment.
- **MELD = bilirubin, creatinine and INR** (with sodium in MELD-Na); used for **transplant prioritisation**.
- **The most common mediastinal tumour overall is THYMOMA**, followed by germ cell tumours and lymphoma; **in CHILDREN the commonest is a NEUROGENIC tumour**. Compartments: **anterior — thymoma, teratoma/germ cell, lymphoma, thyroid ("4 Ts")**; **middle — cysts (pericardial commonest), lymph nodes, vascular masses**; **posterior — neurogenic tumours**.
- **Prostate cancer**: **active surveillance** for **low-risk disease (Gleason 6, PSA < 10, small-volume T1c)** — particularly in men over 70 — with DRE and PSA every 3–6 months and repeat biopsy every 1–3 years.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Which parameter will you NOT use" → **creatinine**. Learn CTP and MELD as a contrasting pair.

**REMEMBER:** *Child-Pugh measures the liver; MELD adds the kidney.*

### 87. IPMN

**PEARL:** **Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm** — cyst fluid shows **positive mucin staining, raised CEA, and raised amylase**.

**UNDERSTAND:** High **amylase** = the cyst **communicates with the pancreatic duct** (IPMN or pseudocyst); high **CEA + mucin** = a **mucinous** (premalignant) lesion.

### 89. Breast Cancer — Staging Traps and Prognosis Order

**PEARL:** **Contralateral (opposite-side) nodes = M1, not N3.** Skin changes (peau d'orange/erythema) over >1/3 of the breast = **T4d, inflammatory carcinoma**. **Ipsilateral supraclavicular node = N3c.**

**UNDERSTAND:** The nodal categories only ever describe **ipsilateral** drainage — axillary, infraclavicular, internal mammary, supraclavicular. Tumour that has reached the other side of the body has, by definition, travelled haematogenously or across watershed lymphatics, so the staging system calls it distant disease.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **T4a** chest wall, **T4b** skin ulceration/satellite nodules/oedema, **T4c** both, **T4d** inflammatory carcinoma.
- **N3a** infraclavicular; **N3b** axillary + internal mammary; **N3c** **ipsilateral supraclavicular**.
- **Inflammatory carcinoma** = dermal lymphatic invasion; it is a **clinical** diagnosis, **not** a histological subtype; treatment is **neoadjuvant chemotherapy first** — it is a contraindication to primary surgery and to breast conservation.
- **Prognosis, best → worst: Tubular → adenoid cystic → cribriform → papillary → medullary → invasive lobular → invasive ductal (NOS) → inflammatory (worst).**
- **Invasive lobular** carcinoma: **E-cadherin negative**, single-file cells, bilateral/multicentric, poorly seen on mammography.
- **Tamoxifen** — SERM: **antagonist in breast**, **agonist in uterus, bone, liver**; used in **ER+ premenopausal** women. Adverse effects: **endometrial carcinoma**, VTE, hot flushes, fatty liver, and (protectively) preserved bone density. Postmenopausal ER+ → **aromatase inhibitors** (arthralgia, osteoporosis).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The staging question hangs entirely on spotting **"contralateral" → M1**; candidates who read only the skin changes pick T4d N3c M0 and lose the mark.

**REMEMBER:** *Cross the midline and you've left the N's behind — that's M1.*

### 90. Phyllodes Tumour

**PEARL:** Arises from **hormonally responsive breast STROMA**; biopsy shows **increased stromal cellularity with a "leaf-like" (phyllodes) architecture**. Usually benign, in women **>40**. Treat by **wide excision with a ≥1 cm margin**; **NO axillary dissection** and **no role for FNAC**.

**UNDERSTAND:** It is a **fibroepithelial** tumour like a fibroadenoma, but the **stroma** is the neoplastic component and it can turn malignant. Because the malignant element is **sarcomatous (mesenchymal)**, it spreads by **blood, not lymphatics** — which is why the axilla is left alone.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Malignant phyllodes metastasises haematogenously to the LUNGS; lymph node metastasis is rare** — the standard exam trap.
- **FNAC cannot distinguish it from fibroadenoma** — you need a **core biopsy** (and even then the final grade comes from the excised specimen).
- **Recurrence is local and related to margin width** — inadequate margins are the main cause of recurrence; large or malignant lesions may need mastectomy.
- Clinically: **large, rapidly growing, bosselated, mobile mass; skin may ulcerate from PRESSURE NECROSIS, not invasion** — a key discriminator from carcinoma (which tethers and retracts).
- Also called **cystosarcoma phyllodes** or **serocystic disease of Brodie** — a misleading name, since most are benign.
- **A giant fibroadenoma is >5 cm** and is simply **enucleated** through a submammary incision; it occurs around puberty.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "False about this condition" → **"metastasises to axillary lymph nodes"**.

**REMEMBER:** *Leaf-like stroma, spreads by blood not lymph — wide excision, never the axilla.*

### 91. Breast Congenital Anomalies — The Terminology

**PEARL:** **Polythelia** = accessory **nipples** (commonest). **Polymastia** = accessory **breast tissue**. **Amastia** = congenital absence of **breast + nipple**. **Athelia** = absence of **nipple** only. **Amazia** = breast tissue absent but **nipple–areola present** — almost always **iatrogenic**. **Symmastia** = midline webbing between the breasts.

**UNDERSTAND:** The Greek roots do the work: **-thelia = nipple, -mastia/-mazia = breast gland; poly- = extra, a- = absent.** Once you separate "nipple" from "gland", every term reads itself.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Accessory breast tissue and nipples arise anywhere along the **embryonic MILK LINE**, from the axilla to the groin — the **axilla is the commonest site**, and axillary breast tissue can **enlarge and become tender in pregnancy and lactation**.
- **Amazia is the exam's favourite** because it is **acquired**: radiation to the chest wall in childhood, **excision of a "lump" that was actually the breast bud**, or **I&D of a suspected abscess** in a prepubertal girl. This is why **a prepubertal breast lump should never be biopsied — it is almost always the normal breast bud**.
- **Poland syndrome** = absence of the **sternocostal head of pectoralis major** ± breast/nipple hypoplasia, with ipsilateral **hand anomalies (syndactyly, brachydactyly)**.
- **Polythelia** has a weak reported association with **renal tract anomalies**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Pure terminology matching, or "all are true about amazia EXCEPT" → "amazia is absence of the nipple" (that is athelia).

**REMEMBER:** *Thelia = nipple, mastia = gland. Amazia keeps the nipple and is usually the surgeon's fault.*

### 92. Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy in Breast Cancer

**PEARL:** **Sentinel lymph node biopsy is performed in CLINICALLY NODE-NEGATIVE early breast cancer** — typically alongside **breast conservation therapy**. It is **not** done when the axilla is already clinically or cytologically positive; those patients go to **axillary dissection** (or neoadjuvant chemotherapy).

**UNDERSTAND:** The sentinel node is the **first node draining the tumour**, so if it is free of tumour the rest of the basin is almost certainly free too — the whole procedure is a **staging tool that spares the morbidity of a full dissection (lymphoedema, numbness, shoulder dysfunction)**. It is logically pointless when nodal disease is already proven: you cannot use a test to exclude what you have already demonstrated.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Technique: dual localisation — radioisotope (99mTc-sulphur colloid) plus blue dye (isosulfan/methylene blue)**, injected peritumorally or subareolarly; identification rate ~97%. Watch for **anaphylaxis and transient falsely low pulse-oximeter readings** with blue dye.
- **Contraindications:** clinically positive axilla (proven on **ultrasound-guided FNAC**), inflammatory breast cancer, previous axillary surgery.
- **If the sentinel node is positive:** **ACOSOG Z0011** showed that in patients with **T1–T2 tumours, breast conservation plus whole-breast radiotherapy, and 1–2 positive sentinel nodes**, completion axillary dissection can be **omitted** without a survival difference. More than 2 positive nodes, or mastectomy without radiotherapy → **axillary dissection**.
- **Axillary levels (relative to pectoralis minor):** I lateral, II behind, III medial. **Level I + II clearance is standard**; nerves at risk are the **long thoracic (winged scapula), thoracodorsal, and intercostobrachial (upper inner arm numbness)**.
- **Breast conservation therapy = wide local excision with clear margins + WHOLE-BREAST RADIOTHERAPY** — radiotherapy is mandatory, and BCT plus radiotherapy has **equivalent survival to mastectomy**. Contraindications: multicentric disease, diffuse malignant microcalcification, prior chest irradiation, persistent positive margins, **pregnancy (first and second trimester — radiotherapy cannot be given)**, and inflammatory carcinoma.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The image or stem shows blue dye/gamma probe → the question is **which patient**. Anchor on **node-negative**.

**REMEMBER:** *Sentinel node answers a question — don't ask it when you already know the answer.*

### 93. Molecular Subtypes of Breast Cancer

**PEARL:** **LUMINAL A = ER and/or PR positive, HER2 NEGATIVE, LOW Ki-67** — the commonest subtype and the one with the **best prognosis**, treated primarily with **endocrine therapy**. **Luminal B = ER+ with high Ki-67 and/or HER2+; HER2-enriched = ER−/PR−/HER2+; TRIPLE NEGATIVE (basal-like) = all three negative.**

**UNDERSTAND:** The classification is a **treatment map, not just a label**. Each subtype is defined by **which drug target the tumour expresses**: an oestrogen receptor makes it answerable to **tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor**; HER2 amplification makes it answerable to **trastuzumab**; and if it has neither, only **cytotoxic chemotherapy** remains — which is why triple-negative disease has the worst prognosis despite being highly chemosensitive. **Ki-67** simply measures how fast the tumour is dividing, separating luminal A from luminal B.

**MUST KNOW:**

| Subtype | ER/PR | HER2 | Ki-67 | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Luminal A** | **+** | **−** | **Low** | **Endocrine therapy**; best prognosis |
| **Luminal B** | + | ± | **High** | Endocrine + chemotherapy (± anti-HER2) |
| **HER2-enriched** | − | **+** | High | **Trastuzumab/pertuzumab + chemotherapy** |
| **Triple negative / basal-like** | − | − | High | **Chemotherapy** (± immunotherapy, PARP inhibitors if BRCA); worst prognosis |

- **BRCA1 → triple-negative/basal-like**; **BRCA2 → more often ER-positive (luminal)**.
- **Endocrine therapy: TAMOXIFEN in premenopausal women** (SERM — antagonist in breast, **agonist in endometrium → endometrial carcinoma risk**, plus VTE and hot flushes); **AROMATASE INHIBITORS (anastrozole, letrozole) in POSTmenopausal women** (arthralgia, osteoporosis) — they are useless premenopausally because ovarian oestrogen synthesis is not aromatase-limited in the same way.
- **Trastuzumab causes REVERSIBLE cardiotoxicity — monitor LVEF**; do not combine with an anthracycline concurrently.
- **Oncotype DX / MammaPrint** gene assays guide whether ER-positive, node-negative patients need chemotherapy.
- Clinical signs in the stem — **hard fixed lump, nipple retraction, skin discolouration/peau d'orange, palpable nodes** — indicate locally advanced disease (**tethering to Cooper's ligaments and dermal lymphatic invasion**).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Definition recall. **Luminal A = hormone positive, HER2 negative, low proliferation.** The trap option adds "high proliferation" (that is luminal B).

**REMEMBER:** *Luminal A has a receptor to block and is in no hurry — the friendliest breast cancer.*

### 94. DCIS After Breast Conservation

**PEARL:** After **breast-conserving surgery for DCIS with clear margins**, the standard next step is **ADJUVANT RADIOTHERAPY to the breast**. **Chemotherapy has NO role in DCIS** — it is an **in-situ** disease with no capacity to metastasise.

**UNDERSTAND:** DCIS is malignant epithelium **still confined by the basement membrane**, so it cannot reach lymphatics or blood vessels — hence **no nodal or distant spread, and no indication for systemic cytotoxic therapy**. The only real risk is **local recurrence (about half of which returns as INVASIVE cancer)** from residual disease in the same breast. Radiotherapy addresses precisely that risk, roughly halving it, which is why it is the answer whenever the breast has been conserved.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Whole-breast radiotherapy after BCS reduces local recurrence by ~50%** but does not change overall survival. It may be omitted in carefully selected **low-risk** DCIS (small, low-grade, wide margins, older patient).
- **Adequate margin for DCIS is 2 mm** (versus "no ink on tumour" for invasive cancer). A 6 mm clearance is comfortably adequate.
- **ENDOCRINE therapy (tamoxifen or an aromatase inhibitor) for 5 years** is offered for **ER-positive DCIS** to reduce ipsilateral recurrence and contralateral new primaries.
- **Sentinel node biopsy is NOT routine in DCIS** — but it is done when a **MASTECTOMY** is performed (because the option is lost afterwards) or when the lesion is large/high-grade with a high chance of occult invasion.
- **DCIS is usually detected as MICROCALCIFICATIONS on screening mammography** (branching, pleomorphic, linear); **COMEDO type with central necrosis and high nuclear grade** carries the highest recurrence risk. **Van Nuys Prognostic Index** (size, margin, grade, age) guides management.
- **PAGET disease of the nipple** = DCIS extending into the nipple epidermis (eczematous, unilateral, does NOT respond to steroids — biopsy any persistent nipple eczema).
- **LCIS is different** — a **risk marker**, not a precursor: it is not excised for margins but managed by **surveillance ± risk-reducing endocrine therapy**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The trap is offering chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy. **In situ = local treatment only.**

**REMEMBER:** *It cannot spread, so treat only the breast — surgery plus radiotherapy.*

### 95. Radical Mastectomy — What Is Removed

**PEARL:** **Halsted radical mastectomy removes the entire breast with skin and nipple-areola complex, BOTH pectoralis major and minor, and axillary nodes at levels I, II and III.** **Supraclavicular nodes are NOT removed.**

**UNDERSTAND:** Halsted's operation was designed on the theory that breast cancer spreads by **contiguous local extension**, so everything anatomically continuous with the tumour was excised. Supraclavicular nodes lie **beyond the axillary field and their involvement signifies distant (M1 in older staging, N3 now) disease** — removing them would not achieve local control, and their involvement changes the treatment from surgical to systemic.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Modern operations: **breast conservation (wide local excision) + radiotherapy = equivalent survival to mastectomy**; **modified radical mastectomy (Patey — removes pectoralis MINOR only; Auchincloss/Madden — preserves BOTH pectorals)**; **simple/total mastectomy**; **skin-sparing and nipple-sparing mastectomy** for reconstruction.
- **Axillary levels are defined by pectoralis minor: level I lateral, level II behind, level III medial.**
- **Sentinel lymph node biopsy** has replaced routine axillary dissection in the **clinically node-negative** axilla.
- **Structures at risk in axillary dissection: long thoracic nerve (winged scapula), thoracodorsal nerve (latissimus dorsi), intercostobrachial nerve (numb inner arm), axillary vein**.
- **Lauren classification of gastric cancer: INTESTINAL (type I)** — environmental, gastric atrophy and intestinal metaplasia, **men > women, older, gland-forming, well differentiated, HAEMATOGENOUS spread**, APC mutations and microsatellite instability; **DIFFUSE (type II)** — familial, **blood group A, women > men, younger, signet ring cells, poorly differentiated, TRANSMURAL and LYMPHATIC spread**, loss of **E-cadherin**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Not removed in radical mastectomy" → **supraclavicular nodes**. Both pectoral muscles ARE removed in the classical Halsted operation.

**REMEMBER:** *Halsted took everything in the axilla but nothing above the clavicle.*

### 96. Breast Self-Examination and BI-RADS

**PEARL:** **Breast self-examination can be performed STANDING OR LYING DOWN**, once a month, ideally on **days 3–6 of the cycle** (any fixed day if postmenopausal), using the **pads of the middle three fingers**.

**UNDERSTAND:** Days 3–6 are chosen because the breast is at its **least oestrogen-stimulated and therefore least nodular and tender** — cyclical engorgement in the luteal phase creates lumpiness that masks and mimics pathology. Examining at the same point each cycle also makes **month-to-month comparison meaningful**, which is the entire value of self-examination.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Both **visual inspection (contour, dimpling, puckering, nipple retraction or discharge, skin changes)** and **palpation** are required, in **standing and supine** positions and with **arms raised**.
- Palpation patterns: **vertical strips, concentric circles, or radial spokes** — cover the axillary tail and press the nipple gently.
- **BI-RADS: 0** incomplete, needs more imaging; **1** negative and **2** benign → **routine annual screening**; **3** probably benign (**≤ 2%** malignancy) → **6-monthly follow-up for 2 years**; **4** suspicious (4A > 2–10%, 4B > 10–50%, 4C > 50–95%) → **BIOPSY**; **5** highly suggestive (**≥ 95%**) → biopsy and treat; **6** biopsy-proven malignancy.
- **Triple assessment** — clinical examination, imaging (mammography and/or ultrasound) and pathology (core biopsy) — for any palpable lump.
- **Mammographic screening** is generally recommended from age **40–50 years**, every 1–2 years; **ultrasound is preferred in women under 35** (dense breasts) and **MRI for high-risk/BRCA carriers**.
- **The commonest complication of mastectomy is SEROMA** — prevented by **closed suction drains**; others are wound infection, flap necrosis, lymphoedema and **intercostobrachial nerve injury (numb inner upper arm)**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Incorrect regarding self-examination" → the claim that it must be done **only while standing**. The BI-RADS 3 answer is **6-monthly follow-up**, not biopsy.

**REMEMBER:** *Examine when the breast is quietest — just after the period.*

**PEARL:** Fibroadenoma is an **aberration of normal lobular development (ANDI)** — not a true neoplasm.

**MUST KNOW:** In the **ANDI** classification it is a disorder of the **early reproductive period (15–25 years)**; it is hormone-responsive (grows in pregnancy, regresses after menopause). ### 98. Breast Conservation Surgery — Contraindications

**PEARL:** BCS is best avoided with: **multicentric tumour, diffuse microcalcifications on mammography, a large tumour-to-breast ratio, persistently positive margins after re-excision, previous breast/chest wall radiation, SLE or other collagen vascular disease (severe radiation reaction), ankylosing spondylitis, and severe orthopnoea** (cannot lie flat for radiotherapy).

**UNDERSTAND:** Almost every contraindication is really a contraindication to the **radiotherapy** that must follow BCS, or to achieving clear margins.

**MUST KNOW:** **Pregnancy in the first/second trimester** is a contraindication (radiotherapy must be deferred); **multifocal** disease (same quadrant) is acceptable, **multicentric** (different quadrants) is not. BCS + radiotherapy gives **survival equal to mastectomy**.

**REMEMBER:** *If she can't have the radiotherapy or clear margins, she can't have BCS.*

### 99. Post-Thyroidectomy Hypocalcaemia

**PEARL:** **Carpopedal spasm 2–3 days after total thyroidectomy = hypocalcaemia from parathyroid ischaemia.** Thyroid surgery is the **commonest cause of hypoparathyroidism**.

**UNDERSTAND:** The parathyroids share their blood supply (inferior thyroid artery) with the thyroid, so total thyroidectomy — especially with **central compartment neck dissection** — devascularises them. The lag of 24–72 hours reflects the half-life of circulating PTH and the time for calcium to fall; that delay is what the stem is testing.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Earliest symptom: **circumoral and fingertip paraesthesia**, then carpopedal spasm, then tetany/laryngospasm/seizures.
- **Chvostek sign** — tap over the facial nerve anterior to the ear → facial twitch. **Trousseau sign** — BP cuff inflated 3 min → carpopedal spasm (**more specific**).
- ECG: **prolonged QT**.
- **Transient** hypoparathyroidism is common; **permanent is rare** (<2% in expert hands). Treat with oral calcium + **calcitriol** (active vitamin D, bypasses the PTH-dependent 1α-hydroxylation step).
- Distinguish from the other complications and their timing: **recurrent laryngeal nerve injury** → hoarseness **immediately**; **bilateral RLN palsy** → stridor, emergency; **external branch of superior laryngeal nerve** (cricothyroid) → loss of high pitch, weak voice; **haematoma** → hours, airway compromise, open at the bedside; **thyroid storm** → within hours.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Timing is the discriminator — "2–3 days later" is calcium; "immediately on extubation" is nerve; "within 6 hours with neck swelling" is haematoma.

**REMEMBER:** *Day 0 hoarse = nerve. Day 2 twitchy = calcium.*

### 100. Preoperative Preparation in Graves Disease

**PEARL:** **POTASSIUM IODIDE (Lugol's iodine) is given for 10–14 days before thyroidectomy specifically to REDUCE THE VASCULARITY of the gland and hence intraoperative bleeding.** Antithyroid drugs and beta-blockers render the patient euthyroid, but they do not shrink the blood supply.

**UNDERSTAND:** Iodine has two distinct effects. Acutely it inhibits hormone release and organification (the **WOLFF–CHAIKOFF effect**), which is why it is used in thyroid storm. Separately — and this is the surgical point — it causes **involution of the hyperplastic gland: the follicles shrink, colloid re-accumulates and the abnormal vascularity regresses**, producing a firmer, less bloody gland. The effect is **transient ("escape" occurs after about 10–14 days)**, which is exactly why it is started shortly before, and never long before, surgery.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Preoperative preparation sequence: antithyroid drug (carbimazole/PTU) until EUTHYROID → BETA-BLOCKER (propranolol) for symptom control and to blunt the adrenergic surge → LUGOL'S IODINE for the last 10–14 days.** Operating on a thyrotoxic patient risks **THYROID STORM**.
- **Thyroid storm treatment ("the 5 Bs"): Beta-blocker (propranolol), Block synthesis (PROPYLTHIOURACIL — preferred here, as it also blocks peripheral T4→T3 conversion), Block release (iodine, given at least ONE HOUR AFTER the antithyroid drug, or it will fuel new hormone synthesis), Block conversion (steroids), and supportive care (cooling, fluids); treat the precipitant.**
- **Antithyroid drugs: CARBIMAZOLE/methimazole is first-line (better compliance, less hepatotoxicity) — but PROPYLTHIOURACIL is preferred in the FIRST TRIMESTER of pregnancy (carbimazole causes aplasia cutis and choanal atresia) and in thyroid storm.** Both can cause **AGRANULOCYTOSIS — warn every patient to report a sore throat and stop the drug immediately.**
- **Definitive treatment options in Graves: antithyroid drugs, RADIOIODINE (avoid in pregnancy and active eye disease, or cover with steroids), or SURGERY (large goitre, compressive symptoms, suspicion of malignancy, severe eye disease, pregnancy intolerant of drugs).**
- **Postoperative complications by timing:** immediate hoarseness (recurrent laryngeal nerve), hours (haematoma → **open at the bedside**), 24–48 hours (thyroid storm), **2–3 days (hypocalcaemia)**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Note the exact wording — "to minimise **intraoperative bleeding**" selects **iodine**, not the drug that makes the patient euthyroid.

**REMEMBER:** *Iodine shrinks the gland and dries the field — the last two weeks before the knife.*

### 101. MEN 2A and the RET Proto-Oncogene

**PEARL:** **Medullary thyroid carcinoma (amyloid-rich stroma, raised calcitonin) + phaeochromocytoma (raised metanephrines) + parathyroid hyperplasia (raised calcium and PTH) with a family history = MEN 2A (Sipple syndrome)**, caused by a **GAIN-of-function mutation in the RET PROTO-ONCOGENE (10q11.2)**.

**UNDERSTAND:** **RET is a receptor tyrosine kinase; the MEN 2 mutations make it constitutively active**, so it behaves as a classic **oncogene — a single activating (gain- of-function) allele is enough, giving autosomal dominant inheritance with high penetrance.** Contrast **MEN 1 (menin) and other tumour suppressors, which require LOSS of function of both alleles**. Paradoxically, **LOSS-of-function RET mutations cause HIRSCHSPRUNG DISEASE** — the same gene, opposite direction, entirely different disease.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **MEN 2A: medullary thyroid carcinoma (~100%), phaeochromocytoma (50%), parathyroid hyperplasia (20–30%)** — may also feature cutaneous lichen amyloidosis and Hirschsprung disease.
- **MEN 2B (MEN 3): MTC + phaeochromocytoma + MUCOSAL NEUROMAS and intestinal ganglioneuromatosis + MARFANOID habitus — and NO parathyroid disease.** It is the most aggressive; MTC develops in infancy.
- **MEN 1 (menin, 11q13, tumour SUPPRESSOR — "3 Ps"): Parathyroid hyperplasia (commonest and earliest), Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours (GASTRINOMA commonest functioning, usually DUODENAL and multiple; also insulinoma), and Pituitary adenoma (prolactinoma).**
- **RET carriers require PROPHYLACTIC TOTAL THYROIDECTOMY** — timing by mutation risk (in infancy for MEN 2B, by age 5 for high-risk MEN 2A). **ALWAYS EXCLUDE AND TREAT THE PHAEOCHROMOCYTOMA FIRST** (alpha then beta blockade) — operating on an unrecognised phaeochromocytoma can be fatal.
- **Medullary thyroid carcinoma arises from PARAFOLLICULAR C CELLS (neural crest): secretes CALCITONIN (and CEA), stroma stains with CONGO RED for AMYLOID, spreads early to nodes, and is NOT iodine-avid — so radioiodine and TSH suppression do not work.** Treat with **total thyroidectomy + central compartment node dissection**; **vandetanib/cabozantinib and selective RET inhibitors** for advanced disease.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The question tests **oncogene vs tumour suppressor**. MEN 2 = **RET, gain of function**; MEN 1 = menin, loss of function.

**REMEMBER:** *RET switched ON gives MEN 2; RET switched OFF gives Hirschsprung.*

### 102. Retrosternal Goitre

**PEARL:** **Over 95% of retrosternal goitres can be removed through a CERVICAL (neck) incision** — sternotomy is rarely needed. Surgery is indicated for **symptoms or suspicion of malignancy**, not for all patients.

**UNDERSTAND:** Almost all retrosternal goitres are **SECONDARY** — a downward extension of a cervical nodular goitre that has been dragged into the mediastinum by swallowing and negative intrathoracic pressure. Because they retain their **blood supply from the superior and inferior thyroid arteries in the neck**, they can be delivered upward once those vessels are controlled cervically. Only the rare **PRIMARY** (ectopic mediastinal) goitre, supplied by **intrathoracic vessels**, requires sternotomy.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Definition: **> 50% of the gland lies below the suprasternal notch** (definitions vary).
- Presentation: often **asymptomatic**, or with **dysphagia, tracheal compression, stridor, hoarseness and SVC obstruction**. **Pemberton's sign** — facial plethora and neck vein distension on raising the arms above the head.
- **CECT neck and thorax is the investigation of choice** (though iodinated contrast may delay radioiodine treatment).
- Indications for sternotomy: **primary/ectopic goitre, invasive malignancy, a very large or recurrent goitre, or extension below the aortic arch**.
- **Preoperative preparation in thyrotoxicosis** requires **antithyroid drugs to euthyroidism, beta-blockade and Lugol's iodine for 10 days** to reduce gland vascularity.
- **Thyroglossal cyst** — a **midline** neck swelling that moves **BOTH on swallowing AND on tongue protrusion** (tethered to the hyoid and foramen caecum); commonest site **infrahyoid**; treated by **Sistrunk's operation (excision of the cyst with the central portion of the hyoid body and the tract to the foramen caecum)** — the only way to prevent recurrence.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "True regarding retrosternal goitre" → **most are removable through the neck**. Blood supply is from the **thyroid**, not thoracodorsal, arteries.

**REMEMBER:** *It came down from the neck, so it can go back up through the neck.*

### 103. Intraoperative PTH — The Miami Criterion

**PEARL:** **Miami criterion: an adequate parathyroidectomy is confirmed when intraoperative PTH falls to MORE THAN 50% BELOW the highest pre-excision value, measured 10 MINUTES after removal.**

**UNDERSTAND:** PTH has a **half-life of only 3–5 minutes**, so removing the sole hyperfunctioning gland should produce a **steep, measurable fall within two half-lives**. Failure of the level to halve means **another hyperfunctioning gland is still secreting** — so the operation must convert from a focused approach to **bilateral neck exploration** there and then, rather than discovering persistent disease weeks later.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Most **primary hyperparathyroidism (~85%) is a single adenoma**, which is why focused/minimally invasive parathyroidectomy is feasible — after localisation with **sestamibi scan and ultrasound** (± 4D-CT).
- Failure of the PTH to drop suggests **multiglandular disease or hyperplasia** — as in **MEN 1 and MEN 2A**, where **subtotal (3½ gland) parathyroidectomy or total parathyroidectomy with autotransplantation** is required.
- Indications for surgery in asymptomatic primary hyperparathyroidism: **age < 50, serum calcium > 1 mg/dL above normal, creatinine clearance < 60, T-score ≤ −2.5 or fragility fracture, nephrolithiasis or nephrocalcinosis, 24-h urine calcium > 400 mg**.
- Postoperative risks: **hypocalcaemia (including HUNGRY BONE syndrome), recurrent laryngeal nerve injury**.
- **The commonest site of a gastrinoma in MEN 1 is the DUODENUM** (not the pancreas) — usually small, multiple, and within **Passaro's gastrinoma triangle** (cystic duct, junction of the second and third parts of the duodenum, and the neck/body of the pancreas). Diagnose **Zollinger–Ellison syndrome** with **gastrin > 1000 pg/mL with gastric pH < 2**, or a **secretin stimulation test showing a rise of > 200 pg/mL**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Both numbers matter — **50%** and **10 minutes**. The options permute one-third/half and 10/30 minutes.

**REMEMBER:** *Two half-lives, so it should halve in ten minutes.*

### 104. Urethral Injury and the Cardinal Rule of Blood at the Meatus

**PEARL:** **Blood at the external urethral meatus = do NOT pass a urethral catheter.** Perineal bruise + blood at meatus after a straddle injury = **bulbar (anterior) urethral injury**; drain by **suprapubic catheter** once the bladder fills.

**UNDERSTAND:** Blind catheterisation converts a partial urethral tear into a complete transection and introduces infection into the periurethral haematoma. Waiting for the bladder to fill is not passivity — a palpable, full bladder is what makes suprapubic puncture safe and confirms the kidneys are producing urine.

**MUST KNOW:**

| | Posterior (proximal) | Anterior (distal) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Site | Membranous > prostatic | **Bulbar** > penile |
| Mechanism | **Pelvic fracture** | **Straddle** injury, fall astride, instrumentation |
| Signs | Retention, **high-riding prostate (Vermooten sign)**, deep pelvic haematoma | Perineal **butterfly haematoma**, blood at meatus |
| Urine extravasation | Above urogenital diaphragm, into pelvis | Superficial perineal pouch (Colles' fascia) — scrotum, penis, anterior abdominal wall |

- **Investigation of choice: retrograde urethrography (RGU)** before any instrumentation.
- **RGU** = contrast instilled at the meatus → shows the **anterior** urethra (penile, bulbar). **MCU/VCU** = contrast into bladder, film during voiding → shows the **posterior** urethra and bladder; it is the **investigation of choice for vesicoureteric reflux and posterior urethral valves**.
- Anterior injuries → suprapubic diversion, delayed urethroplasty. Posterior → suprapubic catheter + delayed repair (or primary realignment).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The trap option is always **"immediate Foley catheterisation"**. Also asked as an image-based question: identify RGU vs MCU by *which end the contrast enters and which segment is filled*.

**REMEMBER:** *Blood at the meatus — hands off the urethra.*

### 105. Struvite Stones and Acetohydroxamic Acid

**PEARL:** **Acetohydroxamic acid is a urease inhibitor used for struvite (triple-phosphate) stones.** Struvite crystals are **coffin-lid** shaped and the stone is classically **staghorn**.

**UNDERSTAND:** Struvite stones form only in **alkaline, infected urine**. Urease-producing organisms split urea into ammonia, driving urine pH above 7.2, which precipitates magnesium ammonium phosphate. Treating the stone therefore means attacking the enzyme — AHA non-competitively inhibits urease, halting the pH rise and stone growth. It cannot dissolve existing stone; complete surgical clearance of every fragment is essential or the infection (and the stone) recurs.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Urease producers: Proteus (classic), Klebsiella, Pseudomonas, Staphylococcus saprophyticus, Ureaplasma. E. coli does NOT produce urease.**
- **PCNL is the treatment of choice for staghorn and for any stone >2 cm**; ESWL is for stones <2 cm in favourable positions and is a poor choice for staghorn (steinstrasse, incomplete clearance).
- Crystal shapes: **coffin-lid = struvite**; **envelope/octahedral = calcium oxalate**; **hexagonal = cystine**; **rhomboid or rosette = uric acid**.
- Radiology: **cystine is faintly radio-opaque ("ground glass"), uric acid is radiolucent** (needs CT/IVU), calcium and struvite are radio-opaque on plain KUB.
- **Cystine** stones → alkalinise urine + **penicillamine/tiopronin**; **uric acid** → alkalinise + allopurinol (may dissolve completely).
- AHA side effects (haemolytic anaemia, DVT, tremor) restrict its use to patients unfit for or refusing surgery.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Two-part stem — coffin-lid crystals + a plain **X-ray KUB** showing a large stone → answer **X-ray KUB, PCNL**. Or the drug-matching question: AHA → struvite.

**REMEMBER:** *Urease splits urea, alkali makes struvite, AHA blocks urease.*

### 106. Testicular Tumours: Orchidectomy Then Chemotherapy

**PEARL:** A solid testicular mass with **retroperitoneal nodal disease** is treated by **radical INGUINAL orchidectomy followed by CHEMOTHERAPY (BEP)** — never by a scrotal approach, and (in the modern era) rarely by radiotherapy.

**UNDERSTAND:** Two anatomical facts dictate everything. The testis drains **lymphatically to the para-aortic nodes at the level of L2** — following its embryological origin — **not** to the inguinal nodes; that is why retroperitoneal nodes are the first station and why the scrotal skin must not be violated (a **scrotal incision seeds the inguinal nodes and changes the drainage basin**, upstaging the disease). And germ cell tumours are **exquisitely chemosensitive**, so systemic disease is curable — testicular cancer is the model of a metastatic solid tumour cured by chemotherapy.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **High inguinal orchidectomy with early cord clamping is both diagnostic and therapeutic. Trans-scrotal biopsy is contraindicated.**
- **Markers before and after surgery: AFP (never raised in pure seminoma), β-hCG (may be mildly raised in seminoma from syncytiotrophoblasts), LDH (tumour bulk).** **A raised AFP means the tumour is treated as NON-seminomatous, whatever the histology says.**
- **Seminoma** — radiosensitive and chemosensitive, spreads in an orderly lymphatic fashion, excellent prognosis. **Non-seminomatous (embryonal, yolk sac, choriocarcinoma, teratoma)** — earlier haematogenous spread; **choriocarcinoma is the most aggressive** with lung metastases and very high hCG.
- Regimen: **BEP — bleomycin, etoposide, cisplatin**, 3–4 cycles. **Residual retroperitoneal mass after chemotherapy in NSGCT → RPLND** (it may contain mature teratoma, which is chemoresistant and must be excised — "growing teratoma syndrome").
- **Offer sperm banking before treatment.** Risk factors: **cryptorchidism** (orchidopexy reduces but does not abolish the risk and makes surveillance possible), previous contralateral tumour, infertility, Klinefelter (mediastinal germ cell tumours).
- **Ultrasound is the first-line imaging** for any testicular lump; a solid intratesticular mass is malignant until proven otherwise.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The stem gives the stage through the nodes. **Node-positive → systemic disease → chemotherapy after orchidectomy**, not RPLND or radiotherapy first.

**REMEMBER:** *Through the groin, never the scrotum — then chemotherapy for the para-aortic nodes.*

### 107. Fournier Gangrene

**PEARL:** **Fournier gangrene** is a **necrotising fasciitis of the perineum, genitalia and perianal region** caused by a **MIXED AEROBIC AND ANAEROBIC (polymicrobial, type I) infection** — typically in a **diabetic, alcoholic or immunosuppressed** man. Treatment is **immediate, radical surgical DEBRIDEMENT plus broad-spectrum antibiotics**.

**UNDERSTAND:** The synergy between organisms is what makes it so fast. **Aerobes consume oxygen and produce an anaerobic microenvironment; anaerobes then produce heparinase and collagenase, thrombosing the subcutaneous vessels.** That thrombosis is why the infection spreads along **fascial planes far beyond the visible skin change**, why antibiotics alone fail (the drug cannot reach dead, thrombosed tissue), and why debridement must go back to **bleeding, healthy tissue** and be repeated daily.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Pain out of proportion to the physical signs, crepitus, dishwater discharge, skin anaesthesia and rapidly advancing erythema are the early clues; frank necrosis is late.** **Gas on X-ray/CT** supports it but its absence excludes nothing. **LRINEC score** aids diagnosis but should never delay surgery.
- **Source is usually anorectal, urogenital or cutaneous.** **The TESTES ARE CHARACTERISTICALLY SPARED** — they have a separate blood supply from the abdomen (testicular arteries), so **orchidectomy is NOT routinely performed**. Likewise **urinary diversion is not compulsory** (a urethral catheter usually suffices), and **anti-gas-gangrene serum has no role** (this is not clostridial myonecrosis).
- **Antibiotics: broad-spectrum triple cover — a carbapenem or piperacillin-tazobactam + clindamycin (antitoxin effect) + vancomycin/linezolid for MRSA.**
- **Adjuncts: aggressive resuscitation, glycaemic control, colostomy for faecal diversion if the anus is involved, hyperbaric oxygen (adjunctive, controversial), and eventual reconstruction/skin grafting.** Mortality remains **20–40%**.
- **Type I necrotising fasciitis = polymicrobial (as here); Type II = monomicrobial GROUP A STREPTOCOCCUS** (young, healthy patients, may follow trivial trauma or varicella, associated with **streptococcal toxic shock**).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Which statement is accurate?" — the true one is the **mixed aerobic and anaerobic microbiology**. Orchidectomy and compulsory urinary diversion are the standard false options.

**REMEMBER:** *Mixed flora thrombose the fascia — cut it all out, and leave the testes alone.*

### 108. Varicocele and Bipolar TURP

**PEARL:** **Percutaneous EMBOLISATION of the gonadal (testicular) vein is the first-line intervention for a symptomatic varicocele**, with surgical ligation reserved for failure or recurrence (~20%). **BIPOLAR TURP uses NORMAL SALINE irrigation and therefore abolishes the risk of TUR SYNDROME (dilutional hyponatraemia/water intoxication).**

**UNDERSTAND:** Both facts turn on a single physical principle. In **monopolar** TURP the current must travel through the patient to a distant pad, so the irrigant **must be non-conductive and electrolyte-free (glycine, sorbitol)** — and when that hypo-osmolar fluid is absorbed through open prostatic venous sinuses, it causes **dilutional hyponatraemia, cerebral oedema and heart failure**. In **bipolar** TURP the current passes only between two electrodes at the resectoscope tip, so **isotonic saline can be used** — absorption then causes no osmotic disturbance. That is why bipolar resection is chosen for the **elderly cardiac patient**, in whom fluid overload would be catastrophic.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **TUR SYNDROME: confusion, restlessness, nausea, bradycardia, hypertension then hypotension, visual disturbance and seizures, from absorption of >2 L of irrigant; sodium falls below 125 mmol/L.** Risk rises with **resection time >60 minutes, large glands and a high irrigation bag height**. Treat with **fluid restriction, furosemide and cautious hypertonic saline**; glycine metabolism to ammonia can also cause **transient blindness and encephalopathy**.
- **Other TURP complications: RETROGRADE EJACULATION (the commonest, up to 65–75% — warn every patient), bleeding, urethral stricture, incontinence (rare) and erectile dysfunction.**
- **Varicocele: "BAG OF WORMS", dragging discomfort worse on standing and relieved on lying down; LEFT-sided in ~90%** (the left testicular vein drains at a right angle into the left renal vein, has a longer course and often incompetent valves). **A NEW, right-sided, or non-emptying varicocele in an older man demands imaging for a RENAL CELL CARCINOMA obstructing the renal vein or IVC.**
- **Indications to treat a varicocele: pain, testicular atrophy or growth arrest in an adolescent, and INFERTILITY with abnormal semen parameters** (repair can improve sperm count and motility). Grading: **I palpable only on Valsalva, II palpable at rest, III visible through the scrotal skin**.
- **Other scrotal swellings: HYDROCELE — fluctuant, TRANSILLUMINANT, you cannot get above it, treated by Jaboulay eversion or LORD's PLICATION; epididymal cyst/spermatocele — transilluminant, above and behind the testis, excised; testicular tumour — solid, does not transilluminate → urgent ultrasound and INGUINAL orchidectomy.**

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Both stems name the co-morbidity or complication to be avoided (**cardiac failure → bipolar/saline**; symptomatic varicocele → the least invasive effective option, **embolisation**).

**REMEMBER:** *Bipolar lets you irrigate with saline, so there is nothing to dilute.*

### 109. Urethral Injury — Anterior vs Posterior

**PEARL:** **Straddle/perineal blow → BULBAR (anterior) urethral injury** with a **butterfly perineal haematoma**. **Pelvic fracture → MEMBRANOUS (posterior) urethral injury** with a **high-riding prostate**. In either case, **NEVER pass a urethral catheter**.

**UNDERSTAND:** The **bulbar urethra is crushed against the inferior pubic ramus** by a direct perineal blow, and blood tracks within **Colles' fascia** — which is attached to the perineal body, fascia lata and Scarpa's fascia, confining the haematoma into the classic **butterfly** shape over perineum, scrotum and penis. The **membranous urethra is fixed by the urogenital diaphragm**, so it shears when the pelvic ring is disrupted and the prostate rides up.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Blood at the meatus is the cardinal sign** — it mandates **retrograde urethrography before any catheterisation**. Passing a Foley can **convert a partial tear into a complete transection**.
- If the bladder is palpable/distended → **SUPRAPUBIC catheter**. If **not yet distended**, the correct next step is to **wait for the bladder to fill and the urge to void**, then place a suprapubic catheter.
- **Vermooten sign** = high-riding, boggy prostate on rectal examination in posterior urethral injury.
- Posterior injuries are associated with **pelvic fracture and EXTRAPERITONEAL bladder rupture** (managed by catheter drainage); **INTRAPERITONEAL bladder rupture** follows a blow to a full bladder and needs **surgical repair**.
- Late complications: **stricture, incontinence and impotence** (worse with posterior injuries).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The **mechanism** in the stem decides the site: manhole/bicycle/straddle → bulbar; road traffic accident with pelvic fracture → membranous.

**REMEMBER:** *Perineal blow hits the bulb; pelvic fracture shears the membrane.*

### 110. Horseshoe Kidney

**PEARL:** The isthmus of a horseshoe kidney is **arrested by the INFERIOR MESENTERIC ARTERY at L3–L4**. **Division of the isthmus does NOT improve function** and risks urinary fistula.

**UNDERSTAND:** The two lower poles fuse in the pelvis at 4–6 weeks, and the fused kidney then **ascends until the isthmus catches on the inferior mesenteric artery** — which is why it always lies **just below the IMA origin** and lower than a normal kidney. The obstruction that causes symptoms is not the isthmus itself but the **high ureteric insertion and anterior ureteric course over it**, so dividing the isthmus achieves nothing.

**MUST KNOW:**

- The commonest renal fusion anomaly; **male predominance ~2:1**; the **adrenals remain in their normal position**.
- IVU shows the **"flower vase" appearance** — medially deviated lower poles with ureters draped over the isthmus.
- Complications: **hydronephrosis and PUJ obstruction, recurrent infection, stones**, and increased risk of **renal cell carcinoma, WILMS tumour and transitional cell carcinoma**.
- Associations: **Turner syndrome, trisomy 18, VACTERL**.
- It is **vulnerable to blunt trauma** because it lies across the vertebral column.
- **Renal cell carcinoma**: **partial nephrectomy (nephron-sparing surgery) is the treatment of choice for tumours < 4 cm** (also for bilateral tumours, a solitary kidney, or pre-existing renal impairment); **radical nephrectomy for larger/locally advanced disease**. RCC is **radio- and chemo-RESISTANT**; advanced disease is treated with **tyrosine kinase inhibitors (sunitinib, sorafenib), mTOR inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors**.
- **Putty kidney** — homogeneous "ground-glass" calcification of a destroyed kidney — is a sign of **genitourinary tuberculosis**, which classically presents with **STERILE PYURIA**; diagnose with **three early-morning urine samples for AFB and culture**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "False regarding horseshoe kidney" → **division of the isthmus improves function**. The IMA relation is asked separately.

**REMEMBER:** *It stopped rising because the inferior mesenteric artery was in the way.*

### 111. Renal Stone Risk Factors

**PEARL:** **Hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria and HYPERURICOSURIA promote calcium stones.** **Citrate and magnesium INHIBIT stone formation** — so hypocitraturia and hypomagnesuria (not *hyper*) are the risk factors.

**UNDERSTAND:** Stone formation occurs when the **concentration product exceeds the formation product** — that is, when solute concentration outruns the **inhibitory capacity of the urine**. Citrate works by **chelating calcium**, keeping it in solution; magnesium binds oxalate. Uric acid promotes calcium oxalate stones by **heterogeneous nucleation** — urate crystals act as a template on which calcium oxalate grows.

**MUST KNOW:**

- Stone types: **calcium oxalate (commonest, ~75%)**; **struvite/triple phosphate (infection stones — urease-producing Proteus, Klebsiella; form STAGHORN calculi in ALKALINE urine)**; **uric acid (radiolucent, ACID urine, in gout, myeloproliferative disease and chronic diarrhoea)**; **cystine (in cystinuria, hexagonal crystals, faintly radio-opaque)**.
- Prevention: **high fluid intake (> 2.5 L/day), normal dietary calcium (restriction increases oxalate absorption), reduced sodium and animal protein, POTASSIUM CITRATE, and thiazides for hypercalciuria**.
- **Allopurinol** for hyperuricosuric calcium stones and uric acid stones; **urinary alkalinisation dissolves uric acid stones**.
- **Acetohydroxamic acid** is a urease inhibitor for struvite stones.
- **Primary hyperoxaluria, enteric hyperoxaluria (after small bowel resection or in Crohn disease), and vitamin C excess** raise oxalate.
- **Hinchey classification of acute diverticulitis: I pericolic/mesenteric abscess; II pelvic abscess; III PURULENT peritonitis; IV FAECAL peritonitis.** Stages III and IV need surgery (**Hartmann's procedure or resection with primary anastomosis**).

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The trick is that **hypercitraturia and hypermagnesuria are PROTECTIVE** — the question tests whether you read the prefix.

**REMEMBER:** *Citrate and magnesium hold calcium in solution.*

### 113. Venous Ulcer

**PEARL:** Venous ulcer: **gaiter area of the lower leg (medial malleolus most often)**, caused by **ambulatory venous hypertension**, with **sloping/shelving edges** and a granulating base — **punched-out edges belong to the ISCHAEMIC (arterial) or neuropathic ulcer**, not the venous one.

**UNDERSTAND:** Edge morphology is a read-out of how the tissue is dying. A venous ulcer heals and breaks down repeatedly at its margin, so **epithelium creeps inward, producing a sloping edge**. An arterial ulcer dies where perfusion stops, with viable tissue right up to the boundary — hence a **sharp, punched-out** edge. Undermined edges mean the pathology spreads beneath the skin (**tuberculosis**); everted/rolled edges mean the tissue is proliferating (**squamous cell carcinoma**); a **rolled, pearly** edge means BCC.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Ambulatory venous hypertension** from valvular incompetence or deep venous obstruction → capillary fibrin cuffs and leukocyte trapping → lipodermatosclerosis (**"inverted champagne bottle" leg**), haemosiderin pigmentation, venous eczema, atrophie blanche, and finally ulceration.
- **CEAP: C4** pigmentation/eczema/lipodermatosclerosis, **C5 HEALED ulcer, C6 ACTIVE ulcer**.
- **Treatment: multilayer graduated COMPRESSION bandaging** is the mainstay — but **measure the ABPI first**: compression is dangerous if **ABPI <0.8**. Add elevation, exercise, wound care, pentoxifylline; treat superficial reflux (**EVLA/RFA/foam**) to reduce recurrence.
- **Marjolin ulcer** — squamous cell carcinoma arising in a chronic venous ulcer or scar; slow-growing, painless, **everted edges** → biopsy any non-healing ulcer.
- Ulcer edges to memorise: **sloping = venous/healing**; **punched-out = arterial, neuropathic (also syphilitic gumma)**; **undermined = tuberculous**; **everted = SCC**; **rolled/beaded = BCC (rodent ulcer)**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "All are true EXCEPT" with the edge as the false option. Also the ABPI trap — never bandage without it.

**REMEMBER:** *Venous ulcers slope; arterial ulcers are punched out.*

### 114. CEAP Classification of Varicose Veins

**PEARL:** **C2 = varicose veins**; the suffix **A = asymptomatic, S = symptomatic** — so uncomplicated varicose veins in an asymptomatic patient are **C2A**.

**UNDERSTAND:** CEAP separates **what you see (Clinical)** from **why it happened (Etiology), where (Anatomy) and how (Pathophysiology)**. The clinical class is a strict **ladder of increasing severity from telangiectasia to active ulcer**, so classification is a matter of identifying the **most advanced** finding present, then adding the symptom suffix.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **C0** no visible disease; **C1** telangiectasia/reticular veins; **C2** varicose veins; **C3** oedema; **C4a** pigmentation or eczema; **C4b** lipodermatosclerosis or **atrophie blanche**; **C4c** corona phlebectatica (fan-shaped telangiectasia at the ankle); **C5** HEALED ulcer; **C6** ACTIVE ulcer.
- **E**: primary, secondary (post-thrombotic), congenital. **A**: superficial, deep, perforator. **P**: reflux, obstruction, or both.
- **Duplex ultrasound is the investigation of choice** (reflux > 0.5 s in superficial veins).
- Treatment ladder: **compression stockings and leg elevation → endovenous thermal ablation (laser or radiofrequency — now first line) → ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy or cyanoacrylate glue → open surgery (high ligation and stripping, phlebectomy)**.
- **Venous ulcers** sit in the **gaiter area above the medial malleolus**, are shallow with sloping edges and granulation, and are managed with **four-layer compression bandaging** after excluding arterial disease (**ABPI > 0.8**).
- Ulcer discrimination: **arterial — punched-out, painful, on toes/pressure points, absent pulses**; **venous — gaiter area, sloping edge, pigmentation**; **NEUROPATHIC/TROPHIC — PAINLESS, punched-out with regular margins, over PRESSURE POINTS (plantar surface), surrounded by callus**; **tuberculous — undermined edge**; **malignant (Marjolin) — everted edge**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Identify the highest clinical class in the image, then add A or S from the history.

**REMEMBER:** *Class by the worst sign, suffix by the symptoms.*

### 115. Milroy Disease and Primary Lymphoedema

**PEARL:** **Congenital lymphoedema presents BEFORE the age of 2 years**; when familial it is **MILROY disease** — inherited as an **AUTOSOMAL DOMINANT** trait. **Lymphoedema PRAECOX (2–35 years) is the commonest type and is called MEIGE disease.**

**UNDERSTAND:** Primary lymphoedema is classified by **age of onset** because that reflects the **severity of the underlying lymphatic hypoplasia**. Complete aplasia manifests at birth; hypoplasia that is marginally adequate decompensates only in adolescence (praecox, often at puberty in girls); minimal hypoplasia may not fail until adulthood (tarda).

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Congenital (< 2 years) — Milroy if familial; often bilateral, may involve multiple limbs, genitalia and face; more common in MALES**; caused by **VEGFR-3 (FLT4)** mutations.
- **Praecox (2–35 years) — Meige disease; commonest overall; usually unilateral and limited to below the knee; more common in FEMALES**.
- **Tarda (> 35 years) — rare; always exclude an underlying malignancy** causing obstruction.
- **Secondary lymphoedema** is far more common worldwide: **filariasis (Wuchereria bancrofti — the commonest cause globally)**, and in developed countries **axillary/inguinal node dissection, radiotherapy and malignancy**.
- Clinical: **non-pitting oedema in established disease** (pitting only early), **positive Stemmer sign** (cannot pinch the skin at the base of the second toe), **peau d'orange and hyperkeratosis**.
- Management: **complex decongestive therapy — skin care, manual lymphatic drainage, compression bandaging and exercise**; surgery (**vascularised lymph node transfer, lymphaticovenous anastomosis, Charles/Homans debulking**) for selected cases. **Diuretics are ineffective.**
- **Complication to know: lymphangiosarcoma (Stewart–Treves syndrome)** in long-standing lymphoedema.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The age of onset defines the class; the eponym then follows. Note the inheritance is **dominant, not recessive**.

**REMEMBER:** *Before two is congenital and familial Milroy; after two is praecox and Meige.*

### 116. Tests for Varicose Veins

**PEARL:** **Brodie–Trendelenburg test and Morrissey's cough impulse test assess SAPHENOFEMORAL incompetence.** **Fegan's test locates PERFORATOR incompetence**; **modified Perthes' test assesses DEEP venous patency**.

**UNDERSTAND:** Each test isolates one component of the venous system by **controlling where blood can enter or leave**. Occluding the saphenofemoral junction with a tourniquet and watching what fills tells you whether reflux is coming **from above (SFJ) or from below (perforators)**. Perthes' test works the opposite way — by **occluding the superficial system and asking the patient to exercise**, it forces flow into the deep system and reveals whether it is patent.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Brodie–Trendelenburg part I** — release the tourniquet after standing; **rapid filling from above = saphenofemoral incompetence**. **Part II** — keep the tourniquet on; **gradual filling from below upward = perforator incompetence**.
- **Morrissey's cough impulse** — an expansile impulse at the saphenous opening on coughing = SFJ incompetence.
- **Fegan's test** — palpate for fascial defects at the sites of perforators with the patient standing, mark them, then confirm with the leg elevated.
- **Modified Perthes' test** — tourniquet below the SFJ, ask the patient to walk; **increasing pain and distension indicate deep venous obstruction** (a contraindication to superficial vein stripping).
- **Schwartz test** — tapping a distal varix transmits a thrill to the SFJ.
- **Duplex ultrasound has replaced all of these clinically**, but the eponyms remain examinable.
- **Mondor's disease** — thrombophlebitis of superficial breast/chest wall veins, presenting as a **tender subcutaneous cord with a groove on stretching the skin**; risk factors include **smoking, breast surgery or biopsy, trauma, and underlying breast cancer**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The question groups all four eponyms — sort them by **which part of the venous system each interrogates**.

**REMEMBER:** *Tourniquet above tests the junction; tourniquet plus walking tests the deep veins.*

### 117. Neonatal Surgical Obstruction

**PEARL:** ***SUCTION RECTAL BIOPSY IS THE DIAGNOSTIC TEST OF CHOICE FOR HIRSCHSPRUNG DISEASE***, demonstrating ***ABSENT GANGLION CELLS with hypertrophied nerve trunks and increased acetylcholinesterase*** (contrast enema shows the transition zone and delayed evacuation but is not diagnostic). ***MECONIUM ILEUS OBSTRUCTS AT THE TERMINAL ILEUM — NOT AT THE DUODENOJEJUNAL FLEXURE — is almost always associated with CYSTIC FIBROSIS, shows a "SOAP-BUBBLE" pattern with an UNUSED MICROCOLON on contrast enema, and may be relieved by a GASTROGRAFFIN ENEMA*** (the historic BISHOP–KOOP operation is now rarely used).

**UNDERSTAND:** The pathology explains the level: ***in cystic fibrosis, PANCREATIC EXOCRINE INSUFFICIENCY leaves meconium abnormally thick and sticky, and it impacts where the lumen is narrowest and the contents most desiccated — the TERMINAL ILEUM.*** The colon distal to the block has never been used, hence the ***MICROCOLON.*** And a hyperosmolar GASTROGRAFFIN enema works therapeutically by ***drawing fluid into the lumen to soften the impacted meconium*** — a rare example of a contrast study being the treatment.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Hirschsprung disease: ***failure of craniocaudal neural crest migration leaving a DISTAL aganglionic segment (rectosigmoid in 75%); presents with FAILURE TO PASS MECONIUM IN 48 HOURS, distension and bilious vomiting, with an EXPLOSIVE GUSH on rectal examination; associated with DOWN SYNDROME; treated by PULL-THROUGH surgery.*** ***HIRSCHSPRUNG-ASSOCIATED ENTEROCOLITIS IS THE LIFE-THREATENING COMPLICATION.***
- **Complicated meconium ileus (volvulus, atresia, perforation with meconium peritonitis and intra-abdominal calcification) requires ***surgery — resection with primary anastomosis or a temporary stoma.***
- **Neonatal obstruction by level: ***DUODENAL ATRESIA — "double bubble", Down syndrome; JEJUNOILEAL ATRESIA — "triple bubble"/multiple levels, from an intrauterine vascular accident; MALROTATION WITH VOLVULUS — bilious vomiting demanding an URGENT upper GI contrast study; ANORECTAL MALFORMATION — always examine the perineum.***
- ***BILIOUS VOMITING IN ANY NEONATE IS A SURGICAL EMERGENCY UNTIL MALROTATION IS EXCLUDED.***
- **Cystic fibrosis should be confirmed by ***sweat chloride and CFTR genotyping*** in every case of meconium ileus.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Investigation of choice for Hirschsprung disease → **suction rectal biopsy**; incorrect about meconium ileus → **that the occlusion is at the DJ flexure**; the Bishop–Koop procedure → **meconium ileus**.

**REMEMBER:** *No ganglion cells on suction biopsy; thick meconium stuck at the terminal ileum with an unused colon beyond.*

### 118. Anorectal Malformation and VACTERL

**PEARL:** ***AN ANORECTAL MALFORMATION IN A NEWBORN SHOULD PROMPT A SEARCH FOR THE VACTERL ASSOCIATION: Vertebral defects (hemivertebrae), Anorectal malformation, Cardiac defects, TRACHEO-OESOPHAGEAL FISTULA with oesophageal atresia, Renal anomalies and LIMB defects (radial ray/missing forearm bones).*** ***WILMS TUMOUR IS NOT PART OF VACTERL.***

**UNDERSTAND:** VACTERL is a ***non-random association of defects arising during BLASTOGENESIS (the first 4 weeks)***, when the caudal mesoderm, hindgut, foregut septation, cardiac looping, renal induction and limb buds are all forming together. It is therefore ***a checklist, not a syndrome***: finding one component obliges you to look for the others, because a missed cardiac or renal anomaly can be more dangerous than the malformation that brought the baby to attention.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Work-up on finding an anorectal malformation: ***ECHOCARDIOGRAM, RENAL AND SPINAL ULTRASOUND, vertebral X-rays, a nasogastric tube to exclude oesophageal atresia, and limb examination.***
- **Anorectal malformations are classified by whether the rectum ends ***ABOVE or BELOW the levator ani*** and by the fistula (perineal, rectourethral, rectovesical, rectovaginal). ***LOW lesions have a good prognosis with a primary perineal operation; HIGH lesions need a DIVERTING COLOSTOMY followed by POSTERIOR SAGITTAL ANORECTOPLASTY (PSARP) and later closure.***
- ***CONTINENCE OUTCOME DEPENDS ON THE LEVEL OF THE LESION AND ON SACRAL DEVELOPMENT (the sacral ratio)*** — high lesions and sacral dysgenesis predict poor continence, which must be discussed with parents.
- **Oesophageal atresia with tracheo-oesophageal fistula: ***suspect with polyhydramnios, drooling, choking on the first feed and a coiled nasogastric tube on X-ray; the commonest type is proximal atresia with a DISTAL fistula (type C).***
- **Other associations to distinguish: ***CHARGE syndrome, OEIS complex, and the caudal regression syndrome of a diabetic mother.***

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Least likely association with anorectal malformation → **Wilms tumour**.

**REMEMBER:** *Find one VACTERL defect and you are obliged to hunt for five more.*

### 119. The Vanishing Testis

**PEARL:** At diagnostic laparoscopy for an impalpable undescended testis, **BLIND-ENDING testicular vessels and vas above the internal ring prove a "VANISHING TESTIS" (testicular regression) — NOTHING FURTHER NEEDS TO BE DONE.** Further exploration is unnecessary because no testicular tissue exists.

**UNDERSTAND:** Blind-ending **VESSELS** are the key finding, not blind-ending vas. The gonad develops **before** and independently of the vas, and the vessels follow the testis wherever it goes — so vessels that simply stop mean the testis **descended and then infarcted in utero (usually from antenatal torsion)**. A blind-ending **vas alone** does not exclude a testis, since the vas and gonad have separate origins. So the surgical decision rests entirely on tracing the **vessels**.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **If the vessels ENTER the internal ring, the testis is inguinal or in the scrotal remnant → INGUINAL EXPLORATION.** If a **viable intra-abdominal testis** is found → **ORCHIDOPEXY (single-stage, or a staged FOWLER–STEPHENS procedure dividing the testicular vessels when the vas' collateral supply must be relied on).**
- **Timing: ORCHIDOPEXY between 6 and 18 months of age** (ideally by 12 months). Spontaneous descent essentially ceases after **6 months**; delay costs germ cells.
- **Undescended testis risks: INFERTILITY (bilateral much worse), MALIGNANCY (3–8× — SEMINOMA; the risk is reduced but NOT abolished by orchidopexy, which mainly makes the testis examinable), TORSION, TRAUMA and an associated INDIRECT INGUINAL HERNIA (in >90%).**
- **The CONTRALATERAL descended testis also carries a slightly increased cancer risk** — teach self-examination.
- **Retractile testis** (a brisk cremasteric reflex, the testis can be brought to the scrotum and stays) needs only observation; a **true undescended testis cannot be manipulated into the scrotum**.
- **Laparoscopy is the gold standard for the IMPALPABLE testis** — ultrasound is unreliable and does not change management.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The finding is **blind-ending vessels**. The trap is offering further exploration in each anatomical compartment.

**REMEMBER:** *Follow the vessels — if they end blindly, so does the search.*

### 120. Biliary Atresia and the Kasai Procedure

**PEARL:** The **Kasai PORTOENTEROSTOMY** is performed for **extrahepatic biliary atresia**. Associated anomalies (in ~20%, the "biliary atresia splenic malformation" syndrome) include **POLYSPLENIA, situs inversus, malrotation, a preduodenal portal vein, absent inferior vena cava, and cardiac defects — but NOT renal agenesis.**

**UNDERSTAND:** The association is with a **LATERALITY (situs) defect**, not a renal one. The embryological insult occurs at the stage when **left–right asymmetry is being established**, so the anomalies cluster around organs whose position and number depend on laterality — spleen (poly- or asplenia), gut rotation, the great veins and the heart. The kidneys are established independently, which is why absent kidney is the outlier.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Biliary atresia presents with PERSISTENT (>2 weeks) CONJUGATED hyperbilirubinaemia, PALE/acholic stools and dark urine in an otherwise thriving newborn.** **Any jaundice persisting beyond 14 days must have a SPLIT (fractionated) bilirubin measured** — conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia is never physiological.
- **Investigations: ultrasound (absent or small gallbladder, the "TRIANGULAR CORD SIGN"), HIDA scan with phenobarbitone priming (uptake but NO excretion into the gut), and LIVER BIOPSY (bile ductular proliferation, bile plugs, portal fibrosis); intraoperative cholangiogram is definitive.**
- **TIMING IS EVERYTHING: the Kasai must be performed BEFORE 60 DAYS OF AGE** — beyond that, biliary cirrhosis is established and success rates fall steeply.
- **Even after a successful Kasai, most children eventually need LIVER TRANSPLANTATION** — biliary atresia is the **commonest indication for paediatric liver transplantation**.
- Differential for neonatal conjugated jaundice: **choledochal cyst, neonatal hepatitis, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, ALAGILLE syndrome (paucity of bile ducts, butterfly vertebrae, peripheral pulmonary stenosis, posterior embryotoxon), galactosaemia, TPN-associated cholestasis, and CF.**
- **Liver transplantation for SECONDARY (metastatic) tumours is exceptional** and is considered essentially only for **neuroendocrine metastases** and, in some centres, **selected metastatic germ cell (testicular) tumours** with a controlled primary and liver-only disease. **Melanoma, breast and active renal cell carcinoma are contraindications.**

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Not usually associated" → the answer is the anomaly outside the laterality spectrum (**absent kidney**).

**REMEMBER:** *A laterality defect — spleens, situs, rotation and veins go wrong together; and operate before 60 days.*

### 121. Pulmonary Sequestration

**PEARL:** **Pulmonary sequestration receives its arterial supply from a SYSTEMIC artery — usually the DESCENDING THORACIC AORTA** — not from the pulmonary artery.

**UNDERSTAND:** The sequestered lung bud arises as an **accessory outgrowth of the foregut caudal to the normal lung**, so it recruits its supply from the **primitive splanchnic (systemic aortic) plexus** and never connects to the pulmonary artery or the tracheobronchial tree. That aberrant systemic vessel is the diagnostic feature — and the surgical hazard, since it retracts into the abdomen if divided carelessly.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Intralobar (~75%)**: **within the normal lung, shares its pleura, usually the LEFT LOWER LOBE (posterobasal)**; **systemic arterial supply from the descending thoracic aorta with PULMONARY VENOUS drainage**; presents in older children/adults with **recurrent infection**.
- **Extralobar (~25%)**: **outside the lung with its OWN pleura**, usually **left-sided and infradiaphragmatic or basal**; **systemic arterial supply and SYSTEMIC venous drainage (azygos/hemiazygos)**; presents in neonates and is associated with **congenital diaphragmatic hernia and other anomalies**.
- Diagnosis: **CT angiography** to demonstrate the feeding vessel; treatment is **surgical resection** (or embolisation of the feeding artery).
- Differential of a congenital lung lesion: **congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM), congenital lobar emphysema, bronchogenic cyst**.
- **Bochdalek hernia**: **respiratory distress within 6 hours of birth is a POOR prognostic sign** (it indicates severe pulmonary hypoplasia); contents typically **retroperitoneal fat, left kidney, spleen, or bowel**; diagnosed **prenatally at 16–24 weeks**; a differential of a cardiophrenic mass is a **pericardial cyst**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The blood supply is the answer, and the intralobar/extralobar distinction turns on **pleural covering and venous drainage**.

**REMEMBER:** *Lung tissue that never joined the airway keeps its embryonic aortic supply.*

### 122. Kasai Procedure and Biliary Atresia

**PEARL:** The **Kasai hepatoportoenterostomy** — a Roux-en-Y jejunal loop anastomosed to the **transected porta hepatis** — is the operation for **extrahepatic biliary atresia**. Associated anomalies include **polysplenia, situs inversus, absent inferior vena cava, preduodenal portal vein and cardiac defects** — but **NOT renal agenesis**.

**UNDERSTAND:** In type III atresia there is **no patent duct to anastomose to**, so a conventional biliary-enteric bypass is impossible. The Kasai works because **microscopic biliary ductules remain open at the transected porta hepatis**; laying bowel directly against that raw surface allows bile to drain from these ductules. Success therefore depends entirely on **operating before those ductules obliterate** — hence the age limit.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Perform the Kasai before 60 days of age** — success falls sharply thereafter; ultimately **many still require liver transplantation**.
- Types: **I — atresia of the common bile duct (a Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy suffices); II — common hepatic duct; III — right and left hepatic ducts (~90%, requires the Kasai)**.
- Presentation: **persistent CONJUGATED jaundice beyond 14 days of life, pale/acholic stools and dark urine, hepatomegaly**. Any infant with conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia needs urgent evaluation.
- Investigations: **ultrasound (absent or contracted gallbladder, "triangular cord" sign), HIDA scan with phenobarbitone priming (no excretion into the gut), and liver biopsy (bile ductular proliferation, portal fibrosis, bile plugs)**.
- The **biliary atresia splenic malformation (BASM) syndrome** is the association with polysplenia, situs inversus, preduodenal portal vein and interrupted IVC.
- Postoperative complication: **ascending cholangitis**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The image of a Roux loop to the liver hilum identifies the operation; the question then asks which association is false — **absent kidney**.

**REMEMBER:** *Anastomose the bowel to the raw porta while the tiny ductules are still open.*

### 123. Ladd's Procedure

**PEARL:** For **malrotation with midgut volvulus**: **detorsion (anticlockwise) + division of Ladd's bands + broadening of the mesentery + incidental appendicectomy + placement of small bowel on the RIGHT and colon on the LEFT**.

**UNDERSTAND:** The bowel is not returned to normal anatomy — it is deliberately left in a **wide-based non-rotated position**, because a broad mesentery cannot twist again.

**MUST KNOW:** Presentation: **bilious vomiting in a neonate = malrotation with volvulus until proven otherwise** — a surgical emergency. Upper GI contrast study is the gold standard (**abnormal DJ flexure position**); ultrasound shows the **whirlpool sign** and SMA/SMV inversion. **Appendicectomy is done because the appendix ends up in the left side of the abdomen**, which would confuse any future diagnosis.

**REMEMBER:** *Bilious vomiting in a newborn → Ladd's: untwist, divide bands, widen mesentery, take the appendix.*

## Neurosurgery and Thoracic Surgery

### 124. Diffuse Axonal Injury

**PEARL:** Unconscious from the moment of impact, **normal CT**, and MRI showing **microhaemorrhages in the corpus callosum and dorsolateral midbrain with no mass effect** = **diffuse axonal injury**.

**UNDERSTAND:** DAI is a **shearing** injury, not a mass lesion. Rotational acceleration makes brain regions of differing density slide against one another, tearing axons and their microvasculature at grey–white junctions and along midline structures that are tethered (corpus callosum, dorsolateral brainstem). Because there is no space-occupying clot, CT is normal and there is **no midline shift** — the injury is microscopic and diffuse, which is exactly why the patient is deeply unconscious out of all proportion to the scan.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Classic triad for the stem:** high-velocity RTA + coma from impact + normal CT.
- **MRI is the investigation of choice; SWI (susceptibility-weighted imaging)/GRE is the most sensitive sequence** — it blooms on haemosiderin and detects far more lesions than CT or standard T2.
- **Adams grading:** Grade 1 grey–white junction only → Grade 2 adds **corpus callosum** → Grade 3 adds **dorsolateral brainstem** (worst prognosis).
- Other sites: fornix, internal/external capsule, cerebellar peduncles.
- Contrast with **extradural** (lucid interval, biconvex, middle meningeal artery), **subdural** (crescentic, bridging veins, elderly/alcoholic), **SAH** (thunderclap, blood in cisterns) — all visible on CT.
- Treatment is supportive; prognosis is the worst of the traumatic intracranial injuries.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** Any stem that pointedly says **"CT was normal"** after a severe head injury is signalling DAI. A second version asks the **best imaging modality** — answer **MRI, SWI sequence**.

**REMEMBER:** *Deep coma + clean CT = shearing, not bleeding.*

### 125. Intracranial Aneurysm: Coiling vs Clipping

**PEARL:** **ENDOVASCULAR COILING is the interventional radiologist's procedure** for a cerebral aneurysm and, since the **ISAT trial**, is the **preferred treatment for most ruptured aneurysms** when the anatomy is suitable. **CLIPPING is the neurosurgical alternative**; **NIMODIPINE is not a treatment for the aneurysm at all — it prevents VASOSPASM**.

**UNDERSTAND:** Coiling packs the aneurysm sac from **inside the lumen** with platinum coils that induce thrombosis, so the parent vessel is never exposed and the brain is never retracted — which is why it produces **lower morbidity and better one-year outcomes** than craniotomy in ISAT. The trade-off is **incomplete occlusion and recanalisation**, so coiled aneurysms need **surveillance angiography**. Clipping obliterates the neck **from outside** and is more durable, so it is preferred for **wide-necked, middle cerebral artery, or distal aneurysms and when a large haematoma needs evacuating anyway**.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Subarachnoid haemorrhage: "the worst headache of my life", thunderclap onset, neck stiffness, photophobia, and sometimes a sentinel bleed. NCCT within 6 hours is >95% sensitive; if negative and suspicion persists, do a LUMBAR PUNCTURE at 12 hours looking for XANTHOCHROMIA** (which distinguishes true SAH from a traumatic tap). Then **CT angiography** to define the aneurysm.
- **Commonest sites: anterior communicating artery, posterior communicating artery (→ a PAINFUL PUPIL-INVOLVING THIRD NERVE PALSY), and the middle cerebral artery bifurcation.** Associations: **polycystic kidney disease, Ehlers–Danlos type IV, coarctation, fibromuscular dysplasia, smoking and hypertension**.
- **NIMODIPINE 60 mg 4-hourly for 21 days** reduces delayed cerebral ischaemia from **vasospasm** (which peaks at **days 4–14**) — it improves outcome without necessarily reversing angiographic spasm. Maintain **euvolaemia**; "triple-H therapy" is now largely replaced by induced hypertension alone.
- **Other complications: rebleeding (the greatest early risk — hence securing the aneurysm within 24–72 hours), HYDROCEPHALUS (may need external ventricular drainage), seizures, and HYPONATRAEMIA from cerebral salt wasting or SIADH** — treat salt wasting with **salt and fluid, not restriction**.
- **Grading: Hunt and Hess or WFNS clinically; FISHER grade on CT predicts vasospasm.**

**EXAM CONNECTION:** The question names the **operator** ("interventional radiologist") — that alone selects **coiling**. Nimodipine is the recurring distractor.

**REMEMBER:** *Coils fill the sac from inside; clips close the neck from outside; nimodipine only protects the brain afterwards.*

### 126. Miscellaneous High-Yield Surgical Facts

**PEARL:** A cluster of one-line facts that appear as straight recall questions.

- **Paget–Schroetter syndrome** = **effort thrombosis of the SUBCLAVIAN VEIN**, compressed between the anterior scalene, clavicle and first rib. Seen in throwers, swimmers, weightlifters; presents with a swollen, painful, cyanosed arm worsened by exercise. Treat with catheter-directed thrombolysis + first rib resection. Lined by **squamous epithelium**, contains turbid fluid full of **cholesterol crystals**. A **congenital branchial fistula** opens at the junction of the **middle and lower third**. Beware: a cystic neck mass in a patient **>40 years** is a **metastatic cystic node from an HPV-related tonsillar/base-of-tongue SCC** until proven otherwise.
- **Surgical site infection rates** — Clean 1–2% (prophylaxis makes no difference); **clean-contaminated 6–9% → 3% with prophylaxis**; contaminated 13–20% → 6%; dirty 40% → 7%.
- **Nerve grafts** — used for peripheral nerves including brachial plexus, and for facial palsy via a **cross-facial nerve graft**. Donors: **sural nerve** (commonest), medial antebrachial cutaneous, sensory branch of posterior interosseous. Repair uses **tension-free epineurial sutures** ± fibrin glue; **glue alone is inadequate**.
- **Suture techniques:** interrupted, **continuous**, mattress (vertical/horizontal), subcuticular (best cosmesis).
- **Non-heart-beating (Maastricht) donors — Category III = awaiting death in hospital, treatment being withdrawn, transplant team present ("controlled").** I = dead on arrival, II = failed resuscitation, IV = cardiac arrest in a brain-dead donor. **I and II are uncontrolled; III and IV are controlled.**
- **Blumberg sign** = rebound tenderness — peritoneal irritation. **Rovsing** = LIF pressure causes RIF pain. **Murphy** = arrest of inspiration on RUQ palpation (cholecystitis). **Psoas/obturator** = retrocaecal/pelvic appendix.
- **Varicose veins, CEAP C2 asymptomatic → compression stockings.** Intervention (RFA, EVLA, foam sclerotherapy, ligation) is for symptoms or complications (**C4 pigmentation/eczema, C5 healed ulcer, C6 active ulcer**).
- **Suspected perforated peptic ulcer** — first investigation is the **erect X-ray**; CECT only if the X-ray is negative and doubt remains.
- **Hepatoma:** cirrhotic background + **AFP >400 ng/mL** + arterial hyperenhancement with venous **washout** on CECT is diagnostic — **biopsy is not required**. Haemangioma shows **peripheral nodular enhancement with centripetal fill-in**; hepatic adenoma is linked to **OCPs/anabolic steroids**.

### 127. Inguinal Node Enlargement — Find the Primary

**PEARL:** The anal canal **BELOW the dentate line** drains to the **superficial inguinal nodes** — so **anal carcinoma** presents with inguinal lymphadenopathy. Above the dentate line, drainage is to the **internal iliac and inferior mesenteric** nodes.

**UNDERSTAND:** The dentate line is an **embryological watershed** — endoderm (hindgut) above, ectoderm (proctodeum) below — and lymphatic drainage follows embryology, not geography. Everything below drains like skin; everything above drains like gut.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Testicular tumours drain to PARA-AORTIC nodes, NOT inguinal** — because the testis descends from the posterior abdominal wall and takes its lymphatics with it. **Inguinal nodes are involved only if the SCROTAL SKIN is breached** (e.g. by previous surgery through the scrotum) — which is why orchidectomy for tumour is done by the **inguinal, not scrotal, approach**.
- **Everything else below the dentate line/skin of the perineum drains inguinally:** anal margin, vulva, penis and scrotal skin, lower vagina, lower third of the anal canal, and the skin of the buttock, lower abdominal wall and lower limb.
- **The whole dentate line rule:** *above* — columnar epithelium, **visceral (painless)** sensation, superior rectal vein (portal), internal iliac nodes, **adenocarcinoma**. *Below* — squamous, **somatic (exquisitely painful)**, inferior rectal vein (systemic), **inguinal nodes**, **squamous cell carcinoma**.
- Practical corollary: **internal haemorrhoids are painless; an anal fissure or thrombosed external pile is agonising.**
- **Anal SCC** is strongly **HPV-related** and treated primarily with **chemoradiation (Nigro regimen)**, not surgery.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Enlarged inguinal node — most likely primary?" → **anal cancer**; the intended trap is **testicular cancer**.

**REMEMBER:** *Below the dentate line drains like skin (inguinal); the testis drains where it came from (para-aortic).*

### 128. Bladder-Drained Pancreas Transplant

**PEARL:** Draining the exocrine pancreatic secretions into the **bladder** loses **bicarbonate** in the urine → **metabolic ACIDOSIS** (plus **chemical cystitis, reflux pancreatitis, urethritis and prostatic abscess**, and dehydration from the obligate fluid loss).

**UNDERSTAND:** The pancreas secretes **1–2 L/day of alkaline, enzyme-rich fluid**. Divert that into the bladder and you are effectively running a continuous bicarbonate drain, while exposing urothelium to activated proteolytic enzymes it was never designed to meet.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **The exam trap is "metabolic alkalosis" — it is ACIDOSIS.** Losing bicarbonate from below (bladder, or diarrhoea/fistula) causes a **normal anion gap (hyperchloraemic) acidosis**; losing acid from above (vomiting) causes alkalosis.
- **Enteric drainage is now standard** — it is more physiological and avoids all these urological complications. Bladder drainage survives mainly because **urinary amylase can be measured as an early marker of REJECTION** — its historical advantage.
- **Simultaneous pancreas–kidney (SPK) transplant** is the operation of choice in **type 1 diabetes with ESRD**; it gives insulin independence and stabilises or improves secondary complications (neuropathy, retinopathy).
- Bicarbonate losses may need **oral sodium bicarbonate supplementation** in bladder-drained recipients; up to 25% eventually require **enteric conversion**.
- The same "bicarbonate lost from below" logic explains the acidosis of **high-output ileostomy, pancreatic fistula, ureterosigmoidostomy and proximal RTA**.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Not a complication of bladder drainage of a transplanted pancreas" → **metabolic alkalosis**.

**REMEMBER:** *Alkaline juice down the drain = acid left behind.*

### 129. FDG-PET and Neuroendocrine Tumours

**PEARL:** **TYPICAL (well-differentiated, low-grade) CARCINOID is NOT FDG-avid** — it is imaged with **somatostatin receptor scintigraphy (⁶⁸Ga-DOTATATE PET or octreotide scan)**. **Atypical carcinoid, large cell neuroendocrine carcinoma and small cell carcinoma ARE FDG-avid.**

**UNDERSTAND:** FDG-PET measures **glucose consumption**, which tracks **proliferation and dedifferentiation**. A well-differentiated tumour with a **low Ki-67 divides slowly and consumes little glucose**, so it is invisible on FDG-PET — but it retains its differentiated feature, **abundant somatostatin receptors**, which is what DOTATATE binds. As grade rises, the tumour **loses somatostatin receptors and gains glycolytic activity** — so the two scans are **inversely informative**, and a tumour that is DOTATATE-negative but FDG-positive is high-grade with a poor prognosis ("**flip-flop**" phenomenon).

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Other FDG-PET false negatives: well-differentiated hepatocellular carcinoma, renal cell carcinoma (and the urinary tract generally, because FDG is excreted in urine), prostate cancer (use PSMA-PET), mucinous adenocarcinoma, bronchioloalveolar carcinoma/lepidic adenocarcinoma, and lesions <8–10 mm.** **False POSITIVES: infection, granulomatous disease (TUBERCULOSIS, SARCOIDOSIS), inflammation, post-surgical change, brown fat and physiological brain/myocardial/bowel uptake.**
- **Patient preparation: fast 4–6 hours, control blood glucose (hyperglycaemia competes with FDG and reduces tumour uptake), rest and keep warm to minimise muscle and brown-fat uptake.**
- **Carcinoid tumours: commonest sites are the small bowel (ileum) and appendix; they secrete SEROTONIN, and CARCINOID SYNDROME (flushing, diarrhoea, wheeze, right-sided valvular fibrosis — TRICUSPID REGURGITATION and PULMONARY STENOSIS) requires either LIVER METASTASES or an extraportal primary (bronchial, ovarian), because the liver otherwise inactivates serotonin.** Diagnose with **urinary 5-HIAA and chromogranin A**; treat with **OCTREOTIDE** (and give octreotide before surgery to prevent CARCINOID CRISIS).
- **Theranostics: ⁶⁸Ga-DOTATATE for imaging, ¹⁷⁷Lu-DOTATATE (PRRT) for treatment** — the same receptor used to find and to treat.

**EXAM CONNECTION:** "Which will NOT be FDG-avid?" — the answer is the **best-differentiated, slowest-growing** tumour.

**REMEMBER:** *Slow, well-differentiated tumours ignore FDG — find them with somatostatin receptors instead.*

### 130. Surgical Numbers Worth Memorising

**PEARL:** **JENKINS' RULE — suture length should be FOUR TIMES the wound length** for closing a midline laparotomy (with 1 cm bites, 1 cm apart, mass closure, continuous, slowly absorbable monofilament). **FRENCH (Fr) gauge = OUTER circumference in mm ≈ 3 × outer diameter — so Fr measures the OUTER DIAMETER (1 Fr = 0.33 mm).**

**UNDERSTAND:** Jenkins' 4:1 ratio exists because **abdominal wound dehiscence is a problem of suture cutting through tissue, not of suture breakage**. Too little suture length means bites that are too far apart or tied too tightly, so the tissue strangulates and the suture cheese-wires through; the 4:1 ratio guarantees enough length for closely spaced, appropriately loose bites that distribute tension. Sutures should be **approximated, not strangulated**.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Burst abdomen (dehiscence) typically occurs around DAY 5–8, heralded by a serosanguinous ("SALMON-PINK") discharge**; risk factors — poor technique, raised intra-abdominal pressure (cough, ileus), malnutrition, sepsis, steroids, diabetes, obesity, jaundice, malignancy. **Management: cover with saline-soaked packs, resuscitate, and resuture in theatre.**
- **A larger Fr number means a WIDER catheter.** Typical adult urinary catheter **14–16 Fr**; a **three-way irrigating catheter (20–22 Fr)** for haematuria and clot retention.
- **NG (Ryle's) tube: insert with the CONSCIOUS patient SITTING and the NECK FLEXED** (flexion closes the airway and opens the oesophagus; extension does the opposite and risks tracheal intubation). Confirm position by **aspirating gastric contents with pH testing and, definitively, by X-RAY** — auscultation of an air bolus is unreliable. Approximate marks: **40 cm cardia, 50 cm body, 60 cm pylorus, 70 cm duodenum.**
- **Incisions: KOCHER's — right subcostal (open cholecystectomy); GIBSON — oblique along the inguinal ligament (lower ureter, pelvic kidney, transplant); PFANNENSTIEL — suprapubic transverse; McBURNEY/GRID-IRON — appendicectomy; ROOFTOP/CHEVRON — liver and pancreas; BATTLE — obsolete pararectal.**
- **ATLS sequence in trauma: CERVICAL SPINE STABILISATION and airway FIRST, then breathing, then circulation (IV access, fluids/blood), then disability and exposure — CT only after the primary survey and only in a stabilised patient.**
- **SIRS criteria (2 or more): temperature >38 or <36 °C; heart rate >90/min; RESPIRATORY RATE >20/min or PaCO₂ <32 mmHg; WBC >12,000 or <4000/mm³ or >10% band forms.** (The "RR >22" figure belongs to **qSOFA**, not SIRS — a favourite trap.)
- **Frozen section is used intraoperatively for MARGIN assessment, SENTINEL NODE status and confirming the presence of metastasis — it is NOT a substitute for definitive paraffin-section diagnosis in all cases** (fat, calcified and small lesions are unreliable, and a definitive diagnosis of some cancers should never rest on it).
- **CYSTINE stones are the most ESWL-RESISTANT** (hard, homogeneous); **calcium oxalate MONOhydrate and brushite are also hard**; **uric acid and struvite fragment easily**. Fragments collecting in the ureter after ESWL = **STEINSTRASSE**.
- **INSULINOMA is the commonest functioning pancreatic neuroendocrine tumour** (Whipple's triad; 90% benign, solitary and intrapancreatic); **gastrinoma is the commonest functioning PNET IN MEN-1, and in MEN-1 gastrinomas are most often DUODENAL and multiple.**

**EXAM CONNECTION:** These are pure recall one-liners: **4:1 suture rule, Fr = outer diameter, sitting with neck flexed, cystine resists ESWL, insulinoma commonest PNET.**

**REMEMBER:** *Four times the wound, outer diameter in French, chin down for the Ryle's tube.*

### 131. Neck Dissection Incisions and Duke Criteria

**PEARL:** The **MacFEE incision** — **two PARALLEL TRANSVERSE cervical incisions along skin creases with an intervening skin bridge** — gives the best cosmesis and the safest flap vascularity for **modified radical neck dissection**. Under the **modified DUKE criteria**, **TWO SEPARATE blood cultures growing a TYPICAL organism (viridans streptococci, S. aureus, S. gallolyticus, HACEK, or community-acquired ENTEROCOCCUS with no primary focus) is a MAJOR criterion.**

**UNDERSTAND:** Both facts turn on **avoiding a false conclusion from a single observation**. Neck incisions that create **trifurcations over the carotid** risk **flap-tip necrosis and carotid blowout** — especially in irradiated tissue — so MacFee's parallel design keeps the bridge broadly perfused. Similarly, a **single** positive culture with a **skin commensal (coagulase-negative staphylococci)** almost always means contamination, whereas **persistent bacteraemia with an organism that has a genuine affinity for the endocardium** is meaningful — which is why the criteria specify **both the organism and the number of cultures**.

**MUST KNOW:**

- **Neck dissection incisions: MacFEE (two transverse), SCHOBINGER (a trifurcate flap), HAYES MARTIN (double-Y), APRON, and HOCKEY-STICK.** MacFee gives less exposure and is unsuitable for bulky fixed nodes.
- **Radical neck dissection removes levels I–V plus the STERNOCLEIDOMASTOID, INTERNAL JUGULAR VEIN and SPINAL ACCESSORY NERVE. The MODIFIED radical dissection preserves one or more of these three (type I preserves the accessory nerve) — preserving the accessory nerve prevents SHOULDER SYNDROME.**
- **Duke MAJOR criteria: (1) typical organism in two separate cultures, or persistently positive cultures, or a single positive culture for COXIELLA BURNETII/phase I IgG >1:800; (2) evidence of endocardial involvement — VEGETATION, abscess, new valve dehiscence, or new valvular REGURGITATION.** **MINOR: predisposition, fever >38 °C, vascular phenomena (Janeway lesions, emboli, mycotic aneurysm), immunological phenomena (Osler nodes, Roth spots, glomerulonephritis, RF), and microbiological evidence not meeting a major criterion.** **Definite = 2 major, or 1 major + 3 minor, or 5 minor.**
- **Viridans streptococci (oral flora) are the classic cause of SUBACUTE endocarditis on an abnormal valve (mitral valve prolapse, rheumatic disease); S. AUREUS causes ACUTE endocarditis on normal valves and in IV drug users (tricuspid); coagulase-negative staphylococci → PROSTHETIC valves; S. gallolyticus (bovis) → look for COLONIC CARCINOMA; HACEK → culture-negative endocarditis.**
- **Take three sets of blood cultures from separate sites before antibiotics; TOE is more sensitive than TTE.**

**EXAM CONNECTION:** For Duke, check **both the organism and the number of cultures**. A single culture only counts for **Coxiella** (or for enterococcus with no source, in some readings).

**REMEMBER:** *Two cultures, typical organism — that's a major criterion; two transverse creases — that's MacFee.*
